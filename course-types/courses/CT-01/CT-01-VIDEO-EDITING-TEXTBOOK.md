# Part I — Foundations of Video Editing

## Chapter 1 — The Language and Purpose of Video Editing

### 1.1 What video editing means

Video editing is the process of selecting, arranging, shortening, combining and shaping recorded or generated visual and audio material so that an audience can understand a message or experience a story. The editor does not merely join files. The editor controls what the audience sees, hears, knows and feels, and in what order.

A recording session may produce hours of material. The final programme may last only one minute. Editing turns that raw material into a deliberate sequence. The result can be a film scene, documentary, product advertisement, interview, music video, tutorial, social-media clip, training film or event recap.

**Core definition:** An edit is an intentional arrangement of picture and sound over time, designed for a defined audience and purpose.

### 1.2 The four dimensions of an edit

Every edit has four connected dimensions:

1. **Story and information.** What is being communicated? What does the viewer need to know first, next and last?
2. **Time and rhythm.** How long does each image or sound remain? Where should the audience pause, anticipate or move quickly?
3. **Space and continuity.** Where are people and objects? Does the sequence make the action understandable?
4. **Technical delivery.** Can the intended audience play the file with correct image, sound, captions and format?

A technically perfect file can still be a poor edit if its message is unclear. A powerful story can still fail if the audio is missing or the delivered file is corrupt. Professional work must satisfy all four dimensions.

### 1.3 Editing, post-production and production

These terms are related but not identical.

- **Production** is the recording or creation of source material: camera footage, sound, graphics, animation or screen recordings.
- **Editing** is the selection and arrangement of that material.
- **Post-production** is the broader finishing process. It can include editing, sound design, color correction, titles, visual effects, captions, mastering, quality control and delivery.
- **Finishing** is the final technical and creative preparation of an approved edit for delivery.

The boundaries vary between studios. A small creator may perform every task. A large production may assign editing, sound, color, VFX and mastering to different specialists.

### 1.4 Non-linear editing

A non-linear editor (NLE) stores instructions about how source media should be arranged. In a typical workflow, the original video and audio files remain unchanged. The project records references to the media, edit points, effects, track layout and other settings.

This is different from physically cutting a film strip. An editor can usually move backward, revise earlier decisions, duplicate sequences and create alternate versions without destroying the source recording.

**Important distinction:**
- Source media = the original or approved media file.
- Project = the editable instructions and references.
- Timeline/sequence = the ordered edit inside the project.
- Export/render = a new playable file created from the timeline.

Do not assume that saving the project also creates a playable video. Do not assume that exporting a video preserves the editable project.

### 1.5 The editor's responsibilities

A professional editor must:
- understand the brief and intended audience;
- verify media rights and permissions;
- preserve source assets and maintain organized project files;
- make editorial choices that serve the message;
- keep dialogue understandable and sound controlled;
- maintain visual continuity or break it intentionally;
- meet the delivery specification;
- check the actual exported file;
- document versions, approvals and limitations;
- hand off the project in a form another authorized person can understand.

### 1.6 A basic editorial model

A useful story model is:

**Setup → Development → Payoff**

- **Setup:** establishes the subject, place, question or situation.
- **Development:** provides action, evidence, explanation or complication.
- **Payoff:** answers the question, reveals the result or gives the audience a satisfying conclusion.

This is a planning tool, not a mandatory formula. A documentary may remain open-ended. An advertisement may begin with the result and then explain how it was achieved. The editor chooses the structure that best serves the brief.

### 1.7 Worked example: a handmade product story

Brief: create a 45-second video showing how a craft maker produces a handmade object.

Raw material:
- a wide shot of the workspace;
- a medium shot of the maker preparing materials;
- close-ups of hands working;
- a recording of a tool being used;
- a final reveal of the object;
- a short statement from the maker.

Possible edit:
1. Open with a close-up of the finished object to create curiosity.
2. Show the workspace to establish location.
3. Show preparation and the main process.
4. Use natural sound from the tool as an audio bridge.
5. Include the maker's statement to add human context.
6. End with the finished object and a clear final frame.

The editor has changed chronology, but must not misrepresent the process. If the finished object was not created during the recorded session, the sequence should not falsely imply that it was.

### 1.8 Chapter notes — memorize and understand

- Editing is decision-making over time, not just joining clips.
- Story clarity is more important than decorative effects.
- A project file, source media and exported video are different things.
- Non-linear editing allows revision, but does not remove the need for backups.
- Every shot and sound should have a purpose.
- Technical delivery and creative quality must both be checked.

### 1.9 Practice work

**Exercise B — Build a story:** Record five short shots of a simple process. Arrange them into setup, development and payoff. Create a second version that opens with the result. Compare which version communicates more clearly.

**Exercise C — Explain the workflow:** In your own words, explain the difference between source media, project, timeline and export.

### 1.10 Review questions

1. Define video editing.
2. Explain the difference between production and post-production.
3. What does a non-linear editor store?
4. Why can a technically correct video still be a weak edit?
5. What are setup, development and payoff?
6. Why must an editor preserve source media?
7. What is the difference between a project file and an export?

**Answer guidance**

2. Production creates/records source material; post-production shapes and finishes it.

## Chapter 2 — Media Fundamentals, Files and Technical Properties

### 2.1 Digital video as a sequence of images

Digital video is commonly represented as a sequence of still images called **frames**, displayed in rapid succession. Motion appears because the images change over time. Sound is stored as digital audio samples.

A video file may contain one or more streams:
- video stream(s);
- audio stream(s);
- subtitles or caption streams;
- metadata and timing information.

The file's container packages these streams. The container does not by itself tell you every detail about how the video or audio was encoded.

### 2.2 Frame rate

**Frame rate** is the number of frames displayed or recorded per second. It is expressed as frames per second (fps) or sometimes as a fractional rate.

Common rates include 23.976, 24, 25, 29.97, 30, 50, 59.94 and 60 fps. These values are not interchangeable in every workflow.

A higher frame rate can capture or display motion with more temporal samples. It does not automatically make a video more cinematic or more professional. The correct rate depends on capture, delivery, motion requirements and the production standard.

**Example:** A clip recorded at 50 fps may be conformed to a 25 fps timeline for half-speed playback if the intent and project settings support it. If it is simply placed in a 25 fps timeline at normal duration, the editor may discard or blend frames depending on application settings.

**Study note:** Never change frame rate casually after detailed editing has begun. Inspect source properties and the delivery brief first.

### 2.3 Resolution and aspect ratio

**Resolution** describes the number of pixels in the image, commonly written width × height.

Examples:
- 1920 × 1080: Full HD landscape.
- 3840 × 2160: UHD landscape.
- 1080 × 1920: Full HD vertical.

**Aspect ratio** describes the proportional relationship between width and height.

- 16:9: common landscape format.
- 9:16: common vertical/mobile format.
- 1:1: square.
- 4:3: traditional television and some archival formats.

Resolution and aspect ratio are related but not the same. A 1080 × 1920 image is vertical 9:16; 1920 × 1080 is landscape 16:9.

A high pixel count cannot restore missed focus, motion blur, poor exposure or severe compression. Evaluate the source image itself.

### 2.4 Containers and codecs

A **container** is the file format that packages media streams and metadata. Examples include MP4, MOV, MXF and MKV.

A **codec** is a method used to encode and decode audio or video. Examples include H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC, ProRes, DNxHR and AV1 for video, and AAC or PCM for audio.

Therefore, “MP4” is not a complete technical description. Two MP4 files can have different codecs, bitrates, frame rates, color properties and audio streams.

**Practical rule:** When a file does not import or play correctly, inspect its streams and codec rather than relying only on the filename extension.

### 2.5 Bitrate and compression

**Bitrate** is the amount of encoded data used per unit of time, often expressed in bits per second (bit/s), kilobits per second (kbit/s) or megabits per second (Mbit/s).

Compression reduces data size. Lossy compression discards information to reduce file size. Repeated lossy encoding can create visible or audible degradation.

A larger bitrate can preserve more detail for a given codec and content, but bitrate alone does not guarantee quality. Quality also depends on:
- codec efficiency;
- image complexity and motion;
- source quality;
- encoder settings;
- resolution and frame rate;
- generation loss from previous exports.

Do not select export settings by guessing a universal bitrate. Follow the delivery specification or a documented platform requirement.

### 2.6 Audio fundamentals

Digital audio represents sound as samples measured at a sample rate. Common production rates include 44.1 kHz and 48 kHz. Video post-production commonly uses 48 kHz, but the delivery specification is authoritative.

**Bit depth** describes the number of bits used to represent each audio sample in linear PCM. Greater bit depth provides more available quantization levels and can increase recording/editing headroom. It does not fix clipping that already occurred at the microphone or preamp.

**Channels** describe separate audio paths:
- mono: one channel;
- stereo: two channels, commonly left and right;
- multichannel: more than two channels, such as surround formats.

Check channel mapping. A file can contain audio but still play incorrectly if the channels are mapped or routed improperly.

### 2.7 Timecode and duration

**Timecode** is a way to label positions in video using hours, minutes, seconds and frames, such as 01:02:15:12. The exact interpretation depends on frame rate and timecode convention.

Duration can be expressed in seconds or timecode. A clip's displayed duration may differ slightly from a simple frame count divided by nominal fps when fractional frame rates or drop-frame timecode are involved.

When giving edit notes, identify the source or sequence and use the correct time reference. A note that says “fix at 00:30” is ambiguous if it is unclear whether the time refers to the source clip or the edited sequence.

### 2.8 Metadata and media inspection

Metadata may include:
- filename and file size;
- creation date and camera information;
- dimensions and frame rate;
- codec and bitrate;
- duration and timecode;
- audio sample rate and channel count;
- color primaries, transfer characteristics and matrix coefficients.

Do not treat metadata as infallible. Some files contain incomplete or incorrect tags. Verify playback and inspect visually/audibly.

### 2.9 Practical inspection procedure

1. Copy the source to the project storage and preserve the original.
2. Open the file in a trusted media player.
3. Inspect video dimensions, frame rate, codec and duration.
4. Inspect audio presence, sample rate and channel count.
5. Play the start, middle and end of long recordings.
6. Check image orientation, focus, exposure and compression.
7. Listen for clipping, hum, wind, missing channels and sync problems.
8. Record the properties and any uncertainty in the media inventory.

### 2.10 Chapter notes

- A frame is one still image in a video sequence.
- Frame rate concerns temporal sampling; resolution concerns pixel dimensions.
- Aspect ratio concerns image shape.
- A container packages streams; a codec encodes/decodes them.
- Bitrate is only one influence on compression quality.
- Audio sample rate and bit depth describe different properties.
- Metadata should be checked against actual playback.

### 2.11 Practice and assessment

**Exercise A:** Inspect five video files and record filename, duration, dimensions, frame rate, codec and audio channels.

**Exercise B:** Compare a landscape and vertical crop of the same shot. Note which important objects are lost or moved.

**Exercise C:** Write a delivery specification for a 30-second mobile video. Include dimensions, frame rate, container, codec, audio and caption requirements. Mark every requirement that needs confirmation.

**Review questions**
1. What is a frame rate?
2. How is resolution different from aspect ratio?
3. Why is MP4 not a codec?
4. What does bitrate measure?
5. What is the difference between sample rate and bit depth?
6. Why inspect the beginning, middle and end of a long clip?
7. Why should export settings come from a written specification?

**Answer guidance:** Frame rate = frames per second; resolution = pixel dimensions; aspect ratio = width-to-height proportion; MP4 = container; bitrate = encoded data per second; sample rate = audio samples per second; bit depth = bits per sample; spot-checking reveals corruption, recording gaps and sync problems; a written specification prevents incompatible or incomplete delivery.

# Part II — Building and Refining an Edit

## Chapter 3 — Project Organization, Ingest and Preparation

### 3.1 Why organization is part of editing

A project is not professional merely because the timeline looks good. If media is missing, folders are confusing or the project cannot be reopened, the work is fragile.

Good organization reduces search time, prevents accidental overwriting, supports collaboration and makes future revisions possible.

### 3.2 The project folder

A simple project can use this structure:

```text
ProjectName_YYYY-MM-DD/
├── 01_Project/
├── 02_Source_Video/
├── 03_Source_Audio/
├── 04_Stills_Graphics/
├── 05_Music_SFX/
├── 06_Proxies/
├── 07_Exports/
├── 08_Review/
├── 09_Documents/
└── 10_Archive/
```

The exact folder names may change to match studio conventions. The important principles are:
- separate originals from generated files;
- keep project files in a known location;
- distinguish proxies from camera originals;
- keep exports away from source folders;
- document external media locations;
- avoid renaming or moving files after import unless you are prepared to relink them.

### 3.3 Ingest: controlled transfer

**Ingest** is the controlled process of copying, verifying, inspecting and organizing source media before editing.

A safe ingest procedure:
1. Confirm the project owner has authorized use of the material.
2. Create the project folders.
3. Copy media from cards or devices to project storage.
4. Preserve the original source until the copy is verified.
5. Compare file counts and file sizes.
6. Use checksums when the workflow requires stronger integrity verification.
7. Open and play representative files.
8. Record media properties and any issues.
9. Create a second backup according to project risk and storage policy.
10. Only then proceed to editing.

A checksum is a calculated value used to detect whether file contents changed during transfer. Matching checksums provide strong evidence that two files are identical, assuming the same reliable algorithm and correct process were used.

### 3.4 Naming conventions

A useful filename should be descriptive, consistent and sortable.

Example:
`CT01_CraftStory_CamA_Take03_2026-11-16.mov`

Avoid names such as `final-final-new2.mp4`. Use a version and status system:
- `V001_ROUGH`
- `V002_CLIENT_REVIEW`
- `V003_PICTURE_LOCK`
- `MASTER_APPROVED`

Never label a draft as approved. Do not overwrite the previous approved version.

### 3.5 Bins and source organization

A bin is an organizational container inside an NLE. It may represent media type, scene, camera, interview subject or story function.

Possible bin structure:
- A_CAM
- B_CAM
- INTERVIEWS
- B_ROLL
- MUSIC
- SOUND_EFFECTS
- GRAPHICS
- SELECTS
- SEQUENCES

Choose the structure that helps the project. A 30-second creator edit may need only a few bins. A feature documentary may need a detailed logging and archival system.

### 3.6 Proxies and optimized media

A **proxy** is a lower-resolution or easier-to-decode copy used to improve editing performance. The original remains the high-quality source for finishing/export, unless the workflow explicitly specifies otherwise.

A proxy workflow must maintain a reliable link between proxy and original. Clearly label generated files and test that the editor can switch between them.

Do not assume that proxy media is always used for export. Verify the NLE's proxy/optimized-media settings and render behavior.

### 3.7 Media inventory

Maintain a source inventory:

| Asset ID | Filename | Type | Duration | Technical properties | Rights/source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| V001 | [filename] | Video | [time] | [resolution/fps/codec] | [owner/license] | [issues] |
| A001 | [filename] | Audio | [time] | [sample rate/channels] | [owner/license] | [issues] |
| G001 | [filename] | Graphic | — | [dimensions/format] | [owner/license] | [issues] |

This inventory helps locate files and identify missing or unauthorized assets.

### 3.8 Relinking and missing media

An NLE project may refer to media using file paths. If a file is moved or renamed, the project may show it as offline.

Safe recovery:
1. Stop editing and save a separate project copy.
2. Identify the missing file and its original path.
3. Locate the verified source copy.
4. Use the NLE's relink/locate function.
5. Relink a single known file first if the application supports it.
6. Confirm duration, frame rate and content match.
7. Check other clips and nested sequences.
8. Save and close the project.
9. Reopen it to confirm the media remains online.

Never relink to a similarly named but unverified file.

### 3.9 Worked practical case

You receive eight video clips, one voice-over and one music track. The camera card must be returned.

Correct process:
- copy all files to project storage;
- compare file count and sizes;
- play all clips;
- record properties and rights;
- create a second backup;
- import into the NLE;
- create bins;
- save the project;
- close and reopen it;
- confirm media online.

The card should not be erased until the project owner or responsible operator confirms that the copy and backup meet the production's policy.

### 3.10 Chapter notes

- Organize before detailed editing.
- Preserve originals and verify copies.
- Keep source media, proxies, project files and exports distinct.
- Use consistent names and version states.
- A media inventory is a professional record.
- Test relinking and reopening; do not assume a project is portable.

### 3.11 Assignment

Prepare a complete project folder for a 60-second story. Submit:
1. folder tree;
2. source inventory;
3. backup verification record;
4. rights/source notes;
5. project file;
6. screenshot of media online;
7. one paragraph explaining how you would recover missing media.

**Assessment:** organization 20%; ingest verification 20%; accurate media properties 20%; rights and backup documentation 20%; successful reopen test 20%.

## Chapter 4 — Timeline, Tracks and the First Assembly

### 4.1 The timeline

The timeline is the workspace where clips are arranged in time. It normally has a horizontal time axis and separate tracks for picture and sound.

The **playhead** indicates the current timeline position. A **clip** is a segment of media placed into the sequence. An **edit point** is the boundary between adjacent clips or segments.

A sequence may contain:
- video tracks;
- audio tracks;
- titles and graphics;
- effects;
- transitions;
- markers and annotations.

The exact interface differs across NLEs, but the concepts are transferable.

### 4.2 Source viewer and program viewer

The source viewer previews an original media clip before or while selecting a range. The program viewer previews the current timeline output.

Confusing the two can cause errors. Before an edit, confirm which viewer has focus and where the playhead is located.

### 4.3 Tracks and compositing order

Video tracks are layered. In many NLEs, a clip on a higher visible track covers a clip below it where both occupy the same time. Transparency, blending modes and compositing can change this behavior.

Audio tracks may play together. Dialogue, music, ambience and effects should be organized so that they can be controlled independently.

A simple layout:
- V2: titles and graphics;
- V1: main picture;
- A1: dialogue;
- A2: natural sound/ambience;
- A3: music;
- A4: sound effects.

This is only an example. Use the track layout appropriate to the project.

### 4.4 Insert and overwrite

An **insert edit** places material at a point and shifts later timeline content to make room. An **overwrite edit** replaces material in the target range without necessarily moving later clips.

These operations are powerful and can damage timing if used without checking the target track and playhead position.

Before using either operation:
1. identify the target sequence;
2. check the playhead position;
3. check track targeting/patching;
4. confirm whether existing material should move or be replaced;
5. perform the edit;
6. play across the edit point;
7. undo immediately if the wrong region changed.

### 4.5 Building a rough assembly

A rough assembly is the first structured version of the story. It should prioritize meaning and order over effects.

Recommended method:
1. Write a one-sentence story statement.
2. Identify the opening, development and ending.
3. Review source clips and mark useful ranges.
4. Place the strongest material in a logical order.
5. Add essential dialogue or natural sound.
6. Add temporary music only if it helps evaluate rhythm.
7. Watch the full assembly without stopping.
8. Note confusing or repetitive moments.
9. Save a checkpoint.
10. Revise the structure before detailed finishing.

### 4.6 Shot function

A shot can serve one or more purposes:
- **Establishing:** shows location or context.
- **Action:** shows something happening.
- **Reaction:** shows a person's response.
- **Detail:** emphasizes an important object or action.
- **Evidence:** supports a factual claim.
- **Transition:** moves between locations, times or ideas.
- **Emotional emphasis:** allows the audience to feel or understand a moment.

A shot that does not contribute information, emotion, geography, rhythm or transition may not be needed.

### 4.7 Continuity in the first assembly

Continuity is the audience's ability to understand how shots relate in time and space. Check:
- screen direction;
- subject position;
- eyelines;
- action progression;
- object placement;
- changes in lighting or wardrobe;
- audio sync and ambience.

Continuity errors can be intentional, but the viewer should understand the creative reason. Unintentional errors distract from the story.

### 4.8 Worked example: a 30-second product demonstration

Story statement: “This tool makes the finishing step of the process faster and more consistent.”

Possible structure:
- 0–4 s: show the product and the material.
- 4–10 s: show the problem or unfinished surface.
- 10–22 s: show the tool in use with a clear close-up.
- 22–27 s: show the result.
- 27–30 s: product name or final message.

The timings are planning estimates. They must change if the action needs more time or the audience needs more explanation.

### 4.9 Timeline review checklist

- Does the opening establish a reason to watch?
- Is the sequence understandable without the editor explaining it?
- Are there unintended gaps or black frames?
- Are clips on the intended tracks?
- Is dialogue synchronized?
- Does music mask important words?
- Does the ending feel complete?
- Is a checkpoint saved?

### 4.10 Chapter notes

- Timeline = ordered edit over time.
- Playhead = current timeline position.
- Source viewer = original media preview.
- Program viewer = edited sequence preview.
- Insert shifts later content; overwrite replaces a target range.
- Tracks separate and layer picture and sound.
- Build story structure before decorative effects.

### 4.11 Practice and review

**Guided exercise:** Create a 20–30 second assembly from at least three shots. Put dialogue or natural sound on a separate audio track and music on another track.

**Independent assignment:** Create a 30–45 second rough cut with a clear beginning, development and ending. Submit the project, an edit note and a screenshot of the timeline.

**Review questions**
1. What does the playhead show?
2. What is the difference between source and program viewers?
3. How does insert differ from overwrite?
4. Why should dialogue and music use separate tracks?
5. Name five possible functions of a shot.
6. Why should the rough assembly be reviewed from beginning to end?
7. What should be done before a major revision?

**Answer guidance:** Current timeline position; source media versus sequence output; insert shifts later material while overwrite replaces; independent control; establishing/action/reaction/detail/evidence/transition/emphasis; to judge story flow and pacing; save a checkpoint.

## Chapter 5 — Trimming, Edit Points and Continuity

### 5.1 Why trimming matters

Trimming determines exactly when a shot begins and ends. A few frames can change the meaning of an action, the rhythm of a scene or the clarity of a cut.

The purpose is not to make every shot as short as possible. The purpose is to preserve the information and feeling that the shot needs to communicate.

### 5.2 Source range and timeline range

A source clip has its own duration. When a portion is used in a sequence, the selected range has an in-point and out-point.

- **In-point:** where the selected portion begins.
- **Out-point:** where the selected portion ends.
- **Head:** beginning portion of a clip.
- **Tail:** ending portion of a clip.
- **Handle:** unused media before or after the selected range, when available.

Handles are important for transitions and later adjustments. If a clip uses every available frame, there may be no extra media to extend a dissolve or trim.

### 5.3 Common trim operations

- **Ripple trim:** changes a clip boundary and closes or opens the resulting gap by moving downstream material.
- **Roll edit:** moves an edit point between two adjacent clips while keeping the overall sequence duration unchanged.
- **Slip edit:** changes the source frames used inside a clip while keeping its timeline position and duration unchanged.
- **Slide edit:** moves a clip along the timeline while adjusting its neighboring edit points to preserve the surrounding sequence duration.
- **Split/cut:** divides a clip into separate timeline segments.

Names and shortcuts differ between applications. Learn the operation's effect, not only its keyboard shortcut.

### 5.4 Ripple versus non-ripple behavior

A non-ripple trim may leave a gap or overlap depending on the application and edit mode. A ripple trim closes or opens the space by shifting later material.

Before a ripple operation, inspect all tracks. A picture clip may move while dialogue or music stays in place, causing sync problems. Use sync locks or linked selections only when you understand their effect.

### 5.5 Continuity and motivated cut points

A cut is often strongest when it occurs:
- after an action is completed;
- during a movement that continues into the next shot;
- when the viewer's attention shifts;
- when a speaker changes thought;
- at a natural pause;
- when a new shot provides useful information.

Avoid cutting away before the viewer understands an important action. Also avoid holding on a shot after it has stopped contributing, unless the pause itself creates meaning.

### 5.6 Match on action

A **match on action** joins two views of the same movement so that the action appears continuous.

Example:
1. Shot A shows a person beginning to open a door.
2. Cut while the hand is moving.
3. Shot B shows the door continuing to open from another angle.

For a convincing result, the direction, speed and timing of the movement should align. If the hand position jumps or the door changes state, the audience may notice a continuity error.

### 5.7 The 180-degree guideline and screen direction

The 180-degree guideline helps maintain consistent screen direction in a scene. Imagine an axis through two subjects or along the main action. Cameras placed on one side of the axis generally preserve the subjects' left/right relationship.

Crossing the axis can reverse screen direction. It is not always wrong, but the edit should reorient the viewer through a neutral shot, visible camera movement, a new establishing view or a deliberate change in perspective.

### 5.8 Trim procedure

1. Play the full scene and identify the purpose of the shot.
2. Find the moment where the useful action or information begins.
3. Set the in-point with enough context for the audience.
4. Find the moment where the shot has completed its purpose.
5. Set the out-point while preserving necessary reaction or sound.
6. Check the adjacent edit point.
7. Play several seconds before and after the cut.
8. Check linked audio and all related tracks.
9. Review the scene at normal speed.
10. Save a new version if the trim changes story structure significantly.

### 5.9 Worked example

A person picks up a cup, pours water and drinks. The first shot shows the hand reaching for the cup; the second shows the pour; the third shows the person drinking.

If the first shot ends before the hand touches the cup, the action may feel incomplete. If it continues long after the cup is lifted, the edit may feel slow. Find the natural action boundary and use the next shot to continue the movement.

### 5.10 Common trimming errors

- **Jump in object position:** align action and object state across shots.
- **Action begins too late:** include the preparation that makes the action understandable.
- **Action ends too early:** preserve the completion or consequence.
- **Unexpected gap:** inspect ripple mode and track targeting.
- **Audio drifts after ripple edit:** inspect sync locks, linked clips and track selection.
- **No handles for transition:** shorten the selected range or use another transition strategy.
- **Over-trimming reactions:** restore enough time for the audience to register emotion.

### 5.11 Chapter notes

- In/out points define the selected source range.
- Ripple edits move downstream material; roll edits adjust a shared boundary.
- Slip changes source content within a fixed timeline position; slide moves a clip and adjusts neighbors.
- Match on action preserves movement across shots.
- Continuity supports clear geography, time and action.
- Review the edit with adjacent shots and sound, not in isolation.

### 5.12 Practical assignment

- one match-on-action cut;
- one ripple trim;
- one roll edit;
- one slip or slide edit if supported by the NLE;
- dialogue or natural sound kept synchronized;
- a before/after comparison and written explanation of the choices.

# Part IV — Intermediate Editing: Control, Meaning and Design

## Chapter 6 — Transitions, J-Cuts, L-Cuts and Visual Continuity

### 6.1 What a transition does

A transition is the method by which one shot or scene changes to another. The transition affects how the viewer understands time, space, movement and meaning. A transition should have a reason. It may preserve continuity, show a change in time, connect two ideas or deliberately create a visual effect.

The straight cut is the basic transition: one shot ends and the next begins immediately. It is usually the least distracting option. This is why it is the default in many professional edits.

### 6.2 Common picture transitions

**Straight cut:** An immediate change from one shot to another. It is suitable for continuous action, dialogue, changes of angle and most ordinary edits.

**Dissolve:** Two images overlap for a period, with one gradually replacing the other. It can suggest elapsed time, memory, a dreamlike connection or a thematic relationship. It can also create ghosting when movement differs between shots.

**Fade:** The image gradually changes to or from a solid color, often black. A fade may indicate a beginning, ending or substantial transition in time. A fade to black is not automatically necessary at every scene ending.

**Wipe:** One image replaces another through a moving boundary. It has a strong stylistic character and should be used only when it fits the design or story.

**Match cut:** A cut that connects shots through similar shape, movement, framing, sound or idea. The connection can be visual or conceptual.

### 6.3 Audio transitions: J-cuts and L-cuts

Audio does not need to begin and end at the same instant as the picture.

- **J-cut:** Audio from the incoming shot or scene begins before its picture appears. The audience hears the next moment before seeing it.
- **L-cut:** Audio from the outgoing shot continues after the picture has changed. The previous moment's sound carries into the next image.

These terms describe the shape often seen in a timeline: the incoming audio leads the picture in a J-cut; the outgoing audio trails the picture in an L-cut.

**Why they work:** Human perception can accept a sound bridge across a picture change. The sound prepares the viewer for the next scene or preserves continuity while the image changes.

### 6.4 Building a J-cut

1. Identify the incoming dialogue or sound.
2. Place it on a separate audio track or unlink it from its picture if the NLE requires this.
3. Move the incoming audio earlier so it begins under the outgoing picture.
4. Keep enough of the outgoing picture for the audience to understand the current moment.
5. Check that the incoming words are not confusing or premature.
6. Play across the edit at normal speed.
7. Check dialogue sync after the picture changes.

Example: A person is finishing a sentence in a workshop. Before the picture cuts to the next speaker, the next speaker's first words begin. The audience anticipates the change and the scene feels connected.

### 6.5 Building an L-cut

1. Identify the outgoing line or sound that should continue.
2. Extend its audio beyond the picture edit.
3. Place the next shot over the continuing sound.
4. Confirm the sound remains relevant to the new image.
5. Add a suitable fade if the audio ends abruptly.
6. Review the timing and emotional effect.

Example: An interviewee describes a handmade object. The picture cuts to close-ups of the object while the interviewee's voice continues. The words remain connected to the evidence shown.

### 6.6 Continuity of space and screen direction

The viewer builds a mental map of a scene. Editing should help the viewer understand where people and objects are located.

**Eyeline match:** If a subject looks toward screen right, the next shot should usually show the object or person they are looking at in a compatible position.

**Screen direction:** The apparent direction in which a person or object moves across the frame. A subject moving left-to-right in one shot will usually be expected to continue in a compatible direction in the next shot.

**180-degree guideline:** Imagine an axis through the main action or between two subjects. Camera positions on one side of that axis generally preserve left/right relationships. Crossing to the other side may reverse screen direction and confuse the viewer.

The 180-degree guideline is not an absolute law. A visible camera move, neutral shot, new establishing view or deliberate change of perspective can reorient the audience.

### 6.7 Match on action and visual matching

A match on action cuts during a movement so the next shot continues the same action. The action should align in direction, timing and object position.

A visual match cut can connect similar shapes or compositions even when the location or time changes. For example, a circular wheel may cut to a circular plate. The connection is conceptual rather than continuous action.

### 6.8 Choosing a transition

Ask these questions:
1. Is this the same continuous action?
2. Is time passing?
3. Is the location changing?
4. Does the sound need to lead or trail the picture?
5. Is the transition communicating a specific idea?
6. Does the effect distract from the subject?
7. Does the transition create a technical problem such as ghosting or an audio pop?

If a straight cut communicates the idea clearly, do not add an effect merely because the editor offers one.

### 6.9 Worked example: two-person conversation

Scene: Person A asks a question; Person B answers.

- Begin on Person A.
- Let Person B's first word begin before cutting to Person B: J-cut.
- Show Person B delivering the answer.
- Cut to Person A listening while Person B's sentence continues: L-cut.
- Return to Person B for the key point.
- Use an establishing shot if the geography of the conversation is unclear.

The edit uses picture changes to show reactions while audio maintains the continuity of the conversation.

### 6.10 Practical exercise

Create three versions of a 20-second scene:
- Version A: straight cuts only.
- Version B: one dissolve and one fade, each with a written reason.
- Version C: one J-cut and one L-cut.

Compare the versions. Identify which one best communicates time, space and meaning. Do not assume the version with the most effects is best.

### 6.11 Chapter notes

- Straight cuts are the default because they preserve clarity.
- Dissolves can suggest time, memory or association.
- J-cut = incoming audio leads the incoming picture.
- L-cut = outgoing audio continues over the next picture.
- Eyelines and screen direction help the viewer understand space.
- The 180-degree guideline is a continuity aid, not an unbreakable rule.
- Every non-standard transition needs a reason.

### 6.12 Review questions

2. Why is the straight cut commonly used?

4. What is a J-cut?
5. What is an L-cut?
6. What does the 180-degree guideline help preserve?

**Answer guidance:** A transition connects shots and communicates relationships; straight cuts are clear and unobtrusive; dissolves blend images to suggest time or association; J-cut incoming audio first; L-cut outgoing audio continues; 180-degree supports screen geography; reorient with camera movement/neutral or establishing shot; match on action aligns continuous movement.

## Chapter 7 — Pacing, Story Structure, Selects and Stringouts

### 7.1 Pacing is information over time

Pacing is the rate at which the audience receives information, action and emotional change. It is not simply the number of cuts per minute.

A fast sequence can feel slow if it repeats the same information. A long shot can feel compelling if the audience is waiting for an important action or emotional response.

A useful question for every shot is: **What does this shot contribute, and how long does the audience need to understand or feel it?**

### 7.2 Story beats

A **beat** is a meaningful change in information, action, emotion or direction. Beats can be large or small.

Example: In a product demonstration:
- the problem is introduced;
- the tool is revealed;
- the process begins;
- a difficulty appears;
- the process succeeds;
- the result is shown.

Each beat changes what the audience knows or expects.

### 7.3 Selects

**Selects** are chosen portions of source footage that may be useful in the edit. A select should have a source filename and time reference so it can be found again.

A good selects log includes:
- source filename;
- in and out timecode;
- short description;
- story function;
- quality/technical notes;
- rights or consent notes if relevant.

Do not remove factual context simply because a short quote sounds more dramatic. Review the source before and after the selected excerpt to understand what was said and whether the edit could misrepresent the speaker.

### 7.4 Stringouts

A **stringout** is a timeline or document containing selected moments, often kept in source order. It helps the editor review available material before committing to a final story structure.

A stringout is not necessarily a finished edit. It may contain repeated takes, long gaps and material that will later be removed. Its purpose is to make the available content easier to evaluate.

### 7.5 Coverage

Coverage is the range of shots available to construct a scene: wide, medium, close-up, reaction, detail and alternate angles. Good coverage gives the editor options. Poor coverage may force a cut that is visually awkward or unsupported.

When footage lacks coverage:
- use a wider shot for a longer duration if it remains engaging;
- use a relevant cutaway only if it is truthful;
- use a graphic or text explanation if appropriate;
- use a deliberate jump cut when stylistically acceptable;
- request additional footage if possible.

Never use unrelated footage in a way that falsely implies it depicts the event being discussed.

### 7.6 A practical story structure

A simple structure for short-form storytelling:
1. **Hook or opening:** create a reason to pay attention.
2. **Context:** explain who, what, where or why.
3. **Development:** show the process, evidence or conflict.
4. **Turning point:** introduce a meaningful change where appropriate.
5. **Payoff:** deliver the result or answer.
6. **Close:** leave the viewer with a clear final thought or action.

Not every video needs all six parts. A tutorial may use a direct promise, steps and result. A documentary may have an unresolved ending.

### 7.7 Shot duration and emotional meaning

A shot earns its duration by providing information, emotion, geography, rhythm or visual pleasure.

Shortening a shot can:
- remove unnecessary delay;
- increase urgency;
- emphasize a reaction;
- improve clarity.

Lengthening a shot can:
- allow the audience to inspect detail;
- create suspense;
- preserve a natural pause;
- communicate scale or atmosphere.

Do not cut away from a meaningful reaction merely to make the sequence faster.

### 7.8 Montage

A montage arranges multiple shots to compress time, compare actions, show development or build an emotional/visual pattern.

A montage needs enough context for the viewer to understand what is being compressed or compared. If every shot is visually different but there is no clear relationship, the montage may feel random.

### 7.9 Editing for different audiences and platforms

A landscape documentary, vertical social clip and instructional video have different audience expectations. The editor may need to change:
- opening speed;
- amount of context;
- shot duration;
- text size;
- caption placement;
- framing;
- sound mix;
- ending and call to action.

Do not assume that one export can be cropped and reused everywhere without editorial adjustment.

### 7.10 Pacing review procedure

1. Write the central story statement.
2. Watch the sequence without pausing.
3. Record where attention increases, drops or becomes confused.
4. Identify repeated information.
5. Identify moments that need more context or reaction.
6. Check whether the opening establishes a reason to watch.
7. Check whether the ending answers the opening question.
8. Adjust shot duration and order.
9. Watch again at normal speed.
10. Compare against the original story statement.

### 7.11 Worked example: 60-second craft profile

Story statement: “A local maker transforms raw material into a finished object through a careful hand process.”

Selects:
- close-up of finished object;
- wide shot of workspace;
- maker introducing the process;
- hands preparing material;
- close-up of difficult technique;
- tool sound;
- maker inspecting the result;
- final reveal.

Possible structure:
- 0–3 seconds: finished object as a visual hook;
- 3–10 seconds: maker and workspace;
- 10–38 seconds: process and key detail;
- 38–50 seconds: maker's explanation and inspection;
- 50–60 seconds: final reveal and closing text.

The editor must verify that the sequence accurately represents the process and does not imply a false chronology.

### 7.12 Practice assignment

Create a selects reel from at least eight source moments. Record timecodes and story function. Then create:
- a 30-second version;
- a 60-second version.

Explain what information was removed, what was preserved and how the pacing changed.

### 7.13 Chapter notes

- Pacing = rate of meaningful information and emotional change.
- A beat is a meaningful shift in action, information or emotion.
- Selects preserve source references and context.
- A stringout helps review material before final structuring.
- Coverage gives editorial options.
- Shot duration should serve meaning, not a fixed rule.
- Montage compresses or compares material but still needs clarity.

### 7.14 Review questions

1. Why is pacing not the same as cutting speed?
2. What is a select?
3. What information should a selects log contain?
4. What is a stringout?
5. How does coverage help the editor?
6. Why must interview excerpts retain context?
7. Name six possible story stages for a short video.
8. When should a shot be allowed to remain longer?

## Chapter 8 — Titles, Graphics, Typography and Keyframe Animation

### 8.1 Why graphics are used

Titles and graphics can identify a speaker, establish a location, explain a concept, show a chapter, provide a callout or present a final message. They should support the content rather than compete with it.

A graphic is successful when the audience can understand it quickly and return attention to the main subject.

### 8.2 Typography

Typography is the design and arrangement of written language. Important factors include:
- typeface and font choice;
- size and weight;
- line spacing;
- letter spacing;
- alignment;
- contrast;
- hierarchy;
- language and character support.

Use fonts that are licensed for the intended use. Confirm that the selected typeface supports all required characters, accents and scripts. A font that looks attractive in English may not support another language correctly.

### 8.3 Visual hierarchy

Hierarchy tells the viewer what to notice first, second and third.

A lower third might use:
- larger/bolder text for a person's name;
- smaller text for their role;
- a subtle line or shape to group the information.

Do not make every element equally prominent. Too many sizes, colors and weights create visual competition.

### 8.4 Contrast and readability

Text must remain readable over the footage. Check:
- text/background contrast;
- size at the smallest expected display;
- busy image areas;
- placement over faces or important objects;
- platform interface overlays;
- language-specific line breaks.

A shadow or backing panel can improve readability, but should be restrained. Do not solve every contrast issue with thick outlines or large boxes.

### 8.5 Safe placement

Keep essential titles and captions away from frame edges and platform interface areas. Different platforms may cover different parts of a vertical video with buttons, captions, account names or navigation controls.

Test the graphic at the actual output dimensions and on a phone-sized display. A title that is readable on a desktop monitor may be too small on a phone.

### 8.6 Keyframes

A **keyframe** stores a parameter value at a specific time. The software calculates values between keyframes using an interpolation method.

Possible animated properties:
- position;
- scale;
- rotation;
- opacity;
- color;
- effect parameters;
- mask position or shape.

Example: A title begins below its final position and moves upward while fading in. The editor sets an initial position and opacity, then sets the final position and opacity at a later time.

### 8.7 Interpolation and easing

**Linear interpolation** changes a value at a constant rate between keyframes. It can feel mechanical.

**Ease-in** gradually accelerates toward movement. **Ease-out** gradually slows near the end. **Ease-in/ease-out** starts and ends more gently.

Easing should support the visual style. Excessive bounce, overshoot or elastic motion can make serious content feel playful or unprofessional.

### 8.8 Basic title animation procedure

1. Decide what the title must communicate.
2. Write accurate, concise text.
3. Choose a readable typeface and hierarchy.
4. Set the final size and placement first.
5. Check contrast on the actual footage.
6. Add a starting keyframe for position or opacity.
7. Move the playhead to the end time and set the final value.
8. Choose suitable interpolation.
9. Review the animation at normal speed.
10. Check that the title holds long enough to read.
11. Preview at output size and on the intended display.
12. Export a short test before rendering the complete video.

### 8.9 Worked example: speaker lower third

Purpose: identify a speaker after the viewer has seen them.

Design:
- name on the first line;
- role or organization on the second line;
- consistent typeface and color;
- placement in a safe area;
- fade/slide animation that does not distract.

Verification:
- confirm spelling and preferred name;
- check title against bright and dark frames;
- confirm it does not cover the speaker's face;
- read it on a phone;
- keep it on screen long enough for normal reading.

### 8.10 Common graphic failures

- Too many typefaces: reduce to a small consistent system.
- Text too close to edges: move into the safe area.
- Low contrast: simplify the background or use a subtle backing.
- Animation too fast: increase duration and hold.
- Keyframe jumps: inspect values, keyframe positions and interpolation.
- Font missing on another device: package or outline fonts only where licensing and workflow allow; otherwise use a permitted substitute.
- Incorrect spelling: verify names and factual text with the authorized source.

### 8.11 Practice assignment

Create an opening title, a lower third and an end card for a 45-second information video. Submit:
- graphic style sheet;
- typeface and font licensing notes;
- colors and hierarchy;
- placement/safe-area screenshot;
- keyframe timing;
- phone-size readability check.

### 8.12 Chapter notes

- Graphics must support the message.
- Typography includes size, weight, spacing, alignment and language support.
- Hierarchy controls reading order.
- Contrast and safe placement determine readability.
- Keyframes store parameter values over time.
- Interpolation controls the movement between keyframes.
- Test graphics at the actual delivery size.

## Chapter 9 — Dialogue, Music, Sound Design and Audio Mixing

### 9.1 Sound is part of the story

Sound communicates space, emotion, action and meaning. A picture edit can be visually strong but difficult to watch if dialogue is unclear or the music masks important information.

Audio editing includes selecting, synchronizing, cleaning, arranging, balancing and processing sound. The goal is not simply to make every track loud. The goal is to create a controlled and understandable sound experience.

### 9.2 Main sound categories

- **Dialogue:** spoken words from an interview, scene or voice-over.
- **Natural sound:** sound recorded in the environment, such as footsteps, wind or tools.
- **Ambience/room tone:** continuous background sound that represents the acoustic environment.
- **Sound effects (SFX):** designed or selected sounds used to support action or meaning.
- **Foley:** performed and recorded sounds synchronized to picture, such as footsteps or object handling.
- **Music:** composed or licensed musical material used to shape mood and rhythm.

Keep these categories organized on separate tracks or buses where practical.

### 9.3 Synchronization

Audio and picture must correspond in time. Sync may be established by:
- camera-recorded audio;
- a visible and audible clap/slate;
- timecode;
- waveform matching;
- a known event such as a door closing.

Check sync at the beginning and later points in long recordings. A fixed offset can be corrected by shifting the audio. Drift that increases over time may indicate clock or frame-rate mismatch and requires a different diagnosis.

### 9.4 Gain and faders

**Gain** changes signal level. **Clip gain** adjusts an individual audio clip. A **track fader** adjusts the combined signal on a track.

A practical workflow is to correct large differences between dialogue phrases with clip gain before applying compression. This allows the processor to work more consistently.

Avoid using the master fader as the only solution to a single loud clip. Find and correct the source of the problem.

### 9.5 Peaks and loudness

A **peak meter** shows the highest signal level over a short period. **Loudness** measures perceived programme level over time using a defined method. LUFS is a common loudness unit used in broadcast and online delivery specifications.

Peak level and loudness are different. A programme can have low average loudness but contain sharp peaks. A loud programme can still sound poor if it is heavily compressed or distorted.

There is no universal loudness target for every platform and delivery. Follow the written specification. Prevent digital clipping and inspect true peaks if required.

### 9.6 Equalization

**Equalization (EQ)** changes the level of selected frequency ranges.

Common purposes:
- reduce low-frequency rumble;
- reduce an annoying resonance;
- improve speech clarity;
- control harshness;
- shape the tonal balance.

Use EQ to solve an audible problem. Large boosts can make noise more obvious. Always compare processed and bypassed sound at a similar listening level.

### 9.7 Compression

**Compression** reduces dynamic range above a chosen threshold. It can make dialogue more consistent, but excessive compression can sound unnatural.

Important controls commonly include:
- threshold;
- ratio;
- attack;
- release;
- knee;
- makeup gain.

The controls interact. A high ratio or very fast attack can make the voice sound flattened or harsh. A release that is too short can create pumping. Begin with subtle settings and compare.

### 9.8 Noise reduction

Noise reduction can reduce steady hum, hiss or broadband noise, but aggressive processing can create watery or metallic artifacts. It cannot always restore speech buried under loud noise or repair severe clipping.

Try this order:
1. use the cleanest source available;
2. edit out unnecessary noise where possible;
3. use room tone to smooth gaps;
4. apply modest noise reduction if appropriate;
5. listen for artifacts;
6. reduce processing if the voice becomes unnatural.

### 9.9 Music and ducking

Music should support the story without masking dialogue. **Ducking** is the reduction of one source while another is prominent. It can be automated manually or by a side-chain process.

A good mix does not keep music at one fixed level throughout. Important words may require a reduction; a visual montage may allow music to rise.

### 9.10 Fades and edit boundaries

Abruptly cutting an audio waveform can create clicks. Short fades or crossfades can smooth the boundary. The correct duration depends on the source and edit.

Do not fade away meaningful ambience at every cut. Continuous room tone or natural sound may help preserve a sense of space.

### 9.11 Practical mixing order

1. Confirm sync and channel mapping.
2. Separate dialogue, ambience, effects and music.
3. Listen to dialogue alone.
4. Correct clip gain for large level differences.
5. Remove avoidable clicks and unwanted noises.
6. Add room tone where edits create unnatural silence.
7. Establish dialogue as the intelligibility anchor.
8. Add ambience and effects at appropriate levels.
9. Bring music in beneath dialogue.
10. Automate or duck music where words need space.
11. Use EQ/compression only to solve identified problems.
12. Check peaks and the required loudness measurement.
13. Listen on headphones and speakers at a comfortable level.
14. Review the complete programme and exported file.

### 9.12 Worked example: 60-second voice-over

The voice-over has three phrases. Phrase one is quiet, phrase two is much louder and phrase three contains a short click.

Procedure:
- adjust clip gain to reduce the difference between phrases;
- repair the click with an edit or suitable restoration;
- add gentle compression only if the remaining dynamics require it;
- place music on a separate track;
- reduce music during important words;
- check the required peak/loudness specification;
- listen to the full programme on more than one playback system.

### 9.13 Audio quality checklist

- Dialogue is understandable at a comfortable listening level.
- No unintended clipping or distortion.
- No distracting clicks at edits.
- Music does not mask important speech.
- Ambience does not change unnaturally between cuts.
- Stereo channels are correct.
- Sync is checked at multiple points.
- Exported audio matches the approved mix.
- Loudness and peak requirements match the delivery specification.

### 9.14 Chapter notes

- Dialogue is the intelligibility anchor in speech-led content.
- Clip gain and track faders serve different purposes.
- Peak level and loudness are not identical.
- EQ changes frequency balance.
- Compression controls dynamic range.
- Noise reduction can create artifacts.
- Music should support, not compete with, the message.
- Listen to the exported file, not only the editor preview.

9. Why should the mix be checked on multiple playback systems?
10. Why must delivery loudness follow a specification rather than a guessed universal value?

**Answer guidance:** Dialogue/natural sound/ambience/SFX/Foley/music; drift may develop; clip gain affects one clip, fader affects track output; peak is instantaneous maximum, loudness is perceived level over time; EQ adjusts frequencies; compression reduces dynamic range; artifacts; lowering one source under another; translation differs by playback system; requirements vary by platform/client/standard.

# Advanced and Professional Practice Chapters

## Chapter 10 — Multicamera Editing and Complex Sequences

### 10.1 Definition and purpose
Multicamera editing is the process of synchronizing recordings of the same event from multiple cameras and selecting between those angles during editorial review. It is used for interviews, concerts, events, dialogue scenes, lectures and live performances.

### 10.2 Preparation
Before synchronizing, inventory every camera and audio source. Record filename, camera label, frame rate, duration, start timecode, audio channels and known recording interruptions. Preserve originals and create a working copy. Do not assume files with similar creation times are synchronized.

### 10.3 Synchronization methods
1. **Timecode:** useful when devices share a correctly configured clock and timecode source.
2. **Waveform:** useful when sources contain the same distinct sound.
3. **Slate/clap:** provides visible and audible alignment reference.
4. **Manual event alignment:** useful when automatic methods are unavailable, but requires careful visual/audio confirmation.

Every method requires verification. Check the beginning, middle and end to detect drift. A sequence aligned at the first frame can become unsynchronized later because of clock drift, frame-rate mismatch or variable-frame-rate media.

### 10.4 Editorial angle selection
Choose the angle that best serves performance, story, screen direction, eyeline, shot size and emotional emphasis. Avoid cutting merely because another camera is available. A wide shot can clarify geography; a close-up can reveal a reaction; a neutral shot can help repair a continuity problem.

### 10.5 Worked example
Three cameras cover a 10-minute interview. Camera A provides a wide master, B covers the interviewer and C covers the guest. An external recorder supplies clean audio. Align the slate, verify lip sync at three points, create a named multicam source, and cut only when the selected angle improves information or performance.

### 10.6 Practice and review
Create a 2-minute multicamera edit from two or more authorized recordings. Submit a source map, sync method, drift check, angle-choice explanation and export. Review: why is start-only sync insufficient? What makes a cut motivated? How should an editor respond when a source angle is missing?

## Chapter 11 — Professional Workflow, Versioning and Client Revisions

### 11.1 Why version control matters
A professional project changes through review. Without clear versioning, editors can overwrite approved work, implement obsolete notes or deliver the wrong export. A version identifier must connect the project sequence, review file, notes and final deliverable.

### 11.2 Practical naming model
Use a consistent pattern such as:
`Project_Scene_Version_Status_YYYY-MM-DD.ext`
Example: `NorthScene_SC03_v04_REVIEW_2026-11-16.mp4`.

Do not use ambiguous names such as `final-final-new.mp4`. Record the sequence ID and export settings in a manifest.

### 11.3 Review protocol
1. Identify the authorized decision-maker.
2. Confirm review deadline and revision scope.
3. Publish a labeled review copy to an approved destination.
4. Collect notes with version, time reference, author and priority.
5. Resolve contradictions before editing.
6. Record accepted, rejected and deferred notes.
7. Create a new version and preserve the previous one.
8. Obtain explicit approval before calling a version final.

### 11.4 Worked example
A stakeholder requests a shorter opening while another requests more context. The editor should not guess which instruction takes priority. Present two clearly labeled alternatives, explain runtime and story trade-offs, and ask the authorized approver to choose.

### 11.5 Practice
Create a revision log with at least eight notes. Include one conflicting request, one objective correction, one out-of-scope request and one deferred item. Produce a new version and a short decision report.

## Chapter 12 — Editorial Direction, Pipeline Design and Master Capstone

### 12.1 Editorial direction
Editorial direction coordinates story intent, performance, pacing, technical constraints and the work of other departments. A master-level editor can explain not only which cut works, but why it works for the audience and project.

### 12.2 Pipeline design
A pipeline defines how assets move from ingest through editing, review, finishing, delivery and archive. It should identify responsible roles, file formats, naming rules, approval gates, dependencies and recovery procedures.

A useful pipeline map is:
**Brief → Ingest → Media verification → Selects → Assembly → Rough cut → Fine cut → Picture lock → Sound/Color/VFX → QC → Delivery → Archive**

The sequence may vary by production, but dependencies and approval points must be explicit.

### 12.3 Master capstone brief
Create a 3–5 minute short narrative, documentary or process story using original or properly licensed media. Deliver:
- creative brief and audience statement;
- script, outline or paper edit;
- media inventory and rights notes;
- edit decision list or equivalent;
- assembly, rough cut and fine cut version records;
- final picture-locked project;
- approved export and technical specification;
- captions/subtitles where required;
- QC report, credits and archive manifest.

### 12.4 Evaluation
The capstone is judged on story clarity, editorial judgment, continuity, pacing, audio, technical delivery, project organization, accessibility, rights and handoff. A visually impressive video does not pass if it uses unauthorized material or cannot be reopened.

### 12.5 Reflection
Write 500–800 words explaining the strongest editorial decision, one rejected alternative, one technical problem, the evidence used to validate the export and what you would improve in a second edition.

## Chapter 13 — AI-Assisted Editing, Automation and Human Review

### 13.1 Appropriate uses
AI may assist with transcription, translation drafts, shot logging, search, silence detection, rough selects, noise reduction, rotoscoping and repetitive metadata tasks. Its value depends on measurable time saved and output quality.

### 13.2 Risk-based selection
Before using a model, ask:
- Is the task suitable for automation?
- Does the tool upload source media?
- Is the media confidential or personally identifying?
- Are rights and consent sufficient?
- Can the output be checked against the original?
- Is a manual fallback available?

### 13.3 Human review
Check names, numbers, negation, speaker identity, timing, translation, facial/voice changes, visual artifacts and factual context. Retain source timecodes and a record of tool/model version, settings, corrections and approval.

### 13.4 Worked example
An automatic transcript suggests a 15-second quote. Listen to the original question and answer. Correct omissions, confirm the speaker and context, and only then use the selection. A transcript is a search aid, not proof that the quote is accurate.

### 13.5 Practice
Run one AI-assisted task on a non-sensitive sample. Compare the output with a manually verified reference. Report errors, correction time, privacy assumptions and whether the automation was worthwhile.

## Chapter 14 — Professional Delivery, Quality Control and Archive

### 14.1 Delivery specification
A delivery specification must identify the required container, codec, resolution, frame rate, color properties, audio layout, loudness/peak limits if specified, captions, runtime, filename and destination. Never guess a client or platform requirement.

### 14.2 Export validation
Inspect the actual export. Verify metadata, playback, first and last frames, full runtime, sync, black/frozen frames, captions, titles, audio channels and file integrity. A successful export dialog does not prove the file is correct.

### 14.3 Handoff
A complete handoff may include:
- approved master and access copy;
- editable project and required dependencies;
- media manifest and relink instructions;
- captions/subtitles and graphics;
- credits and rights records;
- version/change log;
- QC report and delivery specification;
- archive location and retention instruction.

### 14.4 Archive and restore
Archive only according to authorization and retention rules. Store the project, dependencies and manifest together. Test restoration from the archived copy; a folder that has never been reopened is not proven recoverable.

### 14.5 Practice
Prepare a delivery manifest and QC report for a short edit. Mark unknown requirements as “confirmation required” rather than inventing values. Reopen the project from a separate folder and record the result.

## Chapter 15 — Portfolio, Professional Ethics and Continuous Improvement

### 15.1 Portfolio evidence
A portfolio should demonstrate the editor's actual contribution. Identify role, project context, tools/version where relevant, constraints and permission to show the work. Do not imply ownership of footage, music, graphics or work created by collaborators.

### 15.2 Professional communication
Use concise, version-specific notes. Explain trade-offs without defensiveness. Separate objective technical errors from subjective creative preferences. Confirm scope changes and deadlines in writing.

### 15.3 Ethics and rights
Use media with appropriate rights and consent. Respect privacy, confidentiality, accessibility and accurate representation. Do not fabricate documentary events, misattribute statements or conceal material alterations where disclosure is required.

### 15.4 Continuous improvement
After delivery, review feedback, QC defects, time estimates and workflow bottlenecks. Convert lessons into reusable checklists while protecting confidential project information.

### 15.5 Final knowledge review
1. Why does picture lock need approval and version identity?
2. What evidence proves an export is valid?
3. Why is an AI transcript not authoritative?
4. What belongs in a portable handoff?
5. How should an editor describe work in a portfolio?

**Answer guidance:** Lock stabilizes downstream work; export validity requires metadata and playback/QC; AI output must be checked against source; handoff includes project dependencies, deliverables and documentation; portfolio claims must accurately state role and permissions.
