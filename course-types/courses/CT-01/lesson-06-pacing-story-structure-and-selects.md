# CT-01 — Intermediate: Pacing, Story Structure and Selects

**Course:** Video Editing  
**Pathway stage:** Intermediate

## Learning outcomes
Build a coherent sequence from raw footage; create selects; identify story beats; control pacing through shot duration and information density; use markers and edit decision notes.

## Prerequisites
Lessons 01–05 and a source folder containing multiple takes or angles.

## Key definitions
- Selects: portions of source media chosen as candidates for the edit.
- Stringout: timeline containing selected moments in source order.
- Beat: a meaningful change in information, action or emotion.
- Pace: perceived speed and rhythm of a sequence.
- Montage: sequence of shots compressed or arranged to communicate passage, comparison or development.
- Coverage: range of shots/angles recorded to support scene construction.
- Edit decision list (EDL): structured record of edit events or decisions; format varies.
- Marker: timeline or clip annotation used to identify a moment or issue.

## Why it matters
Pacing is not simply making every clip shorter. It is the rate at which the viewer receives meaningful information. Strong pacing alternates emphasis, clarity, anticipation and release.

## Theory
A scene often contains setup, development and payoff. A shot earns its duration by providing information, emotion or visual pleasure. Remove dead time, but preserve pauses that communicate thought or tension. A montage can compress routine actions, compare moments or show change over time.

Selects should preserve context. A memorable line may be unusable if the question, response or factual context is missing. Keep source timecodes and notes so selections can be found again.

## Tools and alternatives
Use clip markers, bins, labels, transcript notes, stringout sequences and spreadsheets. A paper log with source filename and timecode is a valid alternative.

## Procedure
1. Watch source clips before editing and note useful moments.
2. Create selects with source in/out timecodes and short descriptions.
3. Build a stringout in source order; do not polish transitions yet.
4. Identify the central idea and write a one-sentence story statement.
5. Mark setup, development, turning point and payoff where relevant.
6. Assemble the strongest moments in an order that serves the statement.
7. Review each shot's function: information, emotion, geography, rhythm or transition.
8. Shorten only when the shot has delivered its purpose; retain meaningful reaction and pauses.
9. Use markers for unclear logic, missing coverage, repeated information and pacing problems.
10. Review once without stopping. Note where attention rises, falls or becomes confused.
11. Revise and compare the new cut against the story statement.

## Worked example
A 60-second craft profile may open on the finished object, then show the maker, the process and the final use. The chronology is rearranged to create curiosity, but the process remains truthful and comprehensible.

## Dry run
Create a 30-second sequence with six shots. First use equal 5-second durations, then adjust each duration according to information and emotion. Compare how the rhythm changes without adding effects.

## Guided exercise
Make a selects reel from at least eight source moments. Label each with a timecode and one-sentence purpose. Assemble a 30-second story using only those selects.

## Independent assignment
Deliver a 60-second edit, a selects log, a story statement and a revision note explaining five pacing decisions.

## Common errors and recovery
- Every shot has equal duration: vary duration based on information and emotional emphasis.
- Opening lacks a reason to continue: introduce a question, result or compelling image.
- Montage is confusing: establish context and preserve cause/effect where necessary.
- Selects lose factual context: review the full source around each excerpt.
- Over-editing removes emotion: restore pauses or reaction shots that carry meaning.

## Knowledge check
1. What is a stringout?
2. Why should selects retain source timecodes?
3. Does faster cutting always create better pacing?
4. Name four possible functions of a shot.
5. What is the purpose of a story statement?

## Answer key
1. A timeline of selected moments, often in source order.
2. To locate and verify source material and context.
3. No; pacing depends on information, emotion and clarity.
4. Information, emotion, geography, rhythm, transition or emphasis.
5. To keep the edit focused on its central idea.

## Assessment rubric — 30 points
Selects accuracy 6; story clarity 8; pacing decisions 8; source traceability 4; reflection 4. Pass 24/30.

## Quality-control checklist
- [ ] Selects log includes filenames and timecodes.
- [ ] Sequence has a clear central idea.
- [ ] Each shot contributes a purpose.
- [ ] Pauses and reactions are intentional.
- [ ] Factual context is preserved.
- [ ] Revision notes explain key decisions.
