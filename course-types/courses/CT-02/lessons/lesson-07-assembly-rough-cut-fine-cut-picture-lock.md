# CT-02 Lesson 07 — Assembly, Rough Cut, Fine Cut and Picture Lock

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Expert → Master / Professional editorial workflow
- Estimated time: 6–8 hours, excluding a 3–5 minute capstone edit
- Prerequisites: Lessons 01–06; approved brief or script; organized, rights-cleared footage; basic NLE timeline literacy
- Tools/environment: Any non-linear editor (NLE), media player, written brief, footage log and version/change log. Record application, version, operating system and project frame rate.
- Version/review date: 2.0 — 2026-11-17
- Procedure status: Tool-independent. Interface names and application-specific features are version-dependent and are not prescribed here.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

1. Explain the distinct purpose and exit criteria of assembly, rough cut, fine cut and picture lock, scoring at least 8/10 on the stage-classification check.
2. Build a complete assembly from a beat sheet and selects log while preserving source identity and alternate performances.
3. Diagnose at least five structural or editorial problems in a rough cut and propose evidence-based revisions.
4. Refine performance, continuity, rhythm, transitions and audio relationships in a fine cut without losing story clarity.
5. Create traceable versions and a feedback disposition log in which every note has a recorded status.
6. Prepare a picture-lock package identifying the exact approved sequence, runtime, frame rate, approver, approval date, exceptions and downstream dependencies.
7. Assess the impact of a post-lock change and issue a controlled change request before altering the approved picture.

**Evidence standard:** Demonstrate competence through editable project/sequence evidence, version records, a written review log, a lock record (or explicitly unapproved proposed lock), and a short rationale explaining major decisions.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Practical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Assembly | First broad arrangement of selected material in intended story order | Tests whether material can form a complete scene or programme |
| String-out | Organized sequence of selected takes or source ranges | Keeps editorial options visible before structural decisions |
| Radio cut | Dialogue- or audio-led structural version | Tests spoken content and timing without relying on picture polish |
| Rough cut | Complete early version focused on story, structure, scene order and duration | Makes large editorial questions reviewable |
| Fine cut | Refined version with precise performance, timing, continuity and transitions | Resolves smaller editorial choices after structure is stable |
| Picture lock | Explicitly approved picture version whose timing is frozen for downstream work | Provides a dependable reference for sound, VFX, titles, subtitles, color and mastering |
| Handle | Extra source frames before or after an edit | Preserves trimming and transition flexibility |
| Trim | Adjusting an edit point or clip boundary | Changes timing without necessarily changing story order |
| Slip edit | Changing the source range while preserving timeline position and duration | Selects a better moment without moving neighboring edits |
| Slide edit | Moving an edit point while preserving the duration of affected clips | Redistributes timing between adjacent shots |
| Temp track | Temporary music or effects used to communicate intent | Must be identified as temporary and rights-safe |
| Turnover | Organized handoff of locked picture, references, notes and dependencies | Reduces ambiguity during finishing |
| Change request | Recorded proposal to alter an approved or frozen element | Makes authorization and downstream impact visible |
| EDL | Edit Decision List recording edit decisions and source references | One interchange or audit aid; not a complete project handoff |
| OMF / AAF / XML | Interchange formats used in some post-production workflows | Support varies by application, version, media and effect |
| Picture version ID | Stable identifier for an approved or review picture state | Connects notes, exports and downstream work to the same cut |

## Purpose and professional relevance

An editor does not move directly from raw footage to a polished master in one uninterrupted pass. Stages of editorial refinement separate different decisions so that teams solve the largest problems before investing time in detail.

- **Assembly asks:** Can the recorded material be arranged into a complete, understandable version?
- **Rough cut asks:** Does the scene or programme work as a story, and what must change?
- **Fine cut asks:** Are the selected performances, cut points, timing and transitions the strongest available choices?
- **Picture lock asks:** Has the authorized decision-maker approved this exact picture timing for downstream work?

These stages are workflow conventions, not universal legal definitions. Productions may use different labels, combine stages or require additional approvals. The brief, production policy and named decision owner govern the project.

A picture lock is operationally significant. A one-frame trim may move a music cue, subtitle, visual effect, sound effect, transition, credit or delivery duration. Lock therefore requires version identity and change control, not merely an editor's belief that the timeline looks finished.

## Theory and editorial principles

### 1. Progressive commitment

Early stages should preserve options; later stages should resolve them. Committing to one take too early can hide a stronger performance or structural alternative. Keeping every option indefinitely makes review and finishing difficult.

1. Preserve source media and record its identity.
2. Select usable ranges without destroying alternatives.
3. Assemble enough material to test the complete story.
4. Make structural choices in the rough cut.
5. Refine exact timing and continuity in the fine cut.
6. Freeze an approved picture version and document exceptions.

### 2. Structure before polish

A scene can contain beautiful shots and still fail because the audience cannot understand the character's objective, obstacle or change by the ending. During assembly and rough cut, prioritize story completeness, cause and effect, audience knowledge, point of view, geography, action clarity, performance intention, emotional progression and target runtime.

Do not spend substantial time on decorative transitions, detailed effects or final color while fundamental story questions remain unresolved. Temporary elements may communicate intent, but label them clearly.

### 3. Editorial evidence over personal attachment

A shot should remain because it performs a function: reveals information, clarifies geography, carries performance, creates a reaction, advances action, establishes atmosphere or gives the audience necessary time. It should not remain solely because it was difficult or expensive to capture.

When proposing a removal, explain what information or emotional effect is lost. When proposing a hold, identify what the audience is meant to notice or feel during that time.

### 4. Review is a structured activity

A review copy should be identifiable and accompanied by a clear question. Different review rounds should not ask every stakeholder to judge every aspect simultaneously.

- Structural review: Is the story complete and understandable?
- Performance review: Are the emotional choices and reactions working?
- Continuity review: Are geography, action, eyelines and props coherent?
- Fine-cut review: Are timing and transitions intentional?
- Lock review: Is this exact picture version approved for finishing?

Notes should be tied to a specific version and, where practical, timecode. “Make it stronger” is not yet executable. Ask what audience response or story problem the reviewer means.

### 5. Separate types of feedback

Classify notes before changing the edit:

- **Objective defect:** missing media, flash frame, sync error, spelling error or mismatch with an agreed specification.
- **Brief requirement:** required shot, runtime, credit, language or narrative element.
- **Creative preference:** alternate choice about timing, emphasis, tone or performance.
- **Unclear note:** insufficient detail to identify the requested change.
- **Conflicting note:** incompatible instructions from different reviewers or decision owners.
- **Out-of-scope request:** new requirement that changes the approved brief, schedule or budget.

Do not treat every comment as an instruction. Confirm the decision owner and resolve conflicts transparently.

### 6. Picture lock is a controlled state

Picture lock normally freezes editorial timing for the agreed downstream workflow. It does not necessarily mean sound, color, VFX, captions, titles or mastering are complete. Some productions permit controlled exceptions; others require a new approval round.

A post-lock change must identify the reason, exact location, old and new state, approving authority, affected departments, new version ID and re-QC requirements. Never silently replace the locked version.

## Reproducible procedure

### Phase A — Intake and editorial readiness

1. Read the brief and identify audience, format, target runtime, story requirements and review authority.
2. Record the current script or paper-edit revision. If no script exists, use the approved beat sheet or documentary structure.
3. Inventory picture, production sound, music, graphics, titles, VFX references and proxy media.
4. Verify that each asset has a stable identifier and source path or relink information.
5. Confirm rights, releases, consent and restrictions for all media.
6. Confirm project frame rate, audio sample rate, resolution and established technical assumptions. Do not change project settings casually after editing begins.
7. Record missing coverage, unavailable media, uncertain rights and unresolved creative questions.
8. Create a protected working copy or recoverable project checkpoint before major structural editing.

**Exit evidence:** brief/revision ID, asset inventory, rights status, project settings, known gaps and recoverable working project.

### Phase B — Build the assembly

1. Group media by scene, setup, camera, take or subject using consistent naming.
2. Review relevant material before selecting. Record usable source in/out points and performance notes.
3. Build a string-out or selects sequence when it helps compare alternatives.
4. Arrange selected material in intended story order, guided by the beat sheet or approved structure.
5. Include sufficient coverage to preserve plausible options; do not automatically include every recorded take.
6. Retain handles where available and avoid trimming originals.
7. Use temporary slugs or clearly labeled placeholders for missing elements. Never make a placeholder appear to be approved final content.
8. Review the complete assembly once without stopping to polish individual frames.
9. Write a structural report: missing beats, repeated information, unclear motivation, pacing problems, continuity risks and estimated runtime.

**Assembly exit criteria:** the complete intended scene or programme can be followed from beginning to end; missing material and structural uncertainty are visible; source identity and alternatives remain recoverable.

### Phase C — Develop the rough cut

1. Duplicate or otherwise preserve the assembly as a new version.
2. Establish the strongest available beginning, progression and ending.
3. Remove material that repeats information without adding a new reaction, perspective or dramatic function.
4. Test alternate scene or beat order only where story logic supports it.
5. Compare performances across takes. Avoid combining fragments in a way that changes meaning or creates misleading continuity.
6. Check geography, screen direction, eyelines, props and match on action.
7. Shape broad pacing. Give important decisions and reactions enough time; compress only where the audience can still understand the action.
8. Use temporary music or effects only when they clarify intent. Mark temp media, ownership and replacement needs.
9. Review the complete cut at normal speed and note exact timecodes.
10. Compare runtime with the brief. If over or under target, identify structural causes before arbitrary trims.
11. Prepare a review copy with a unique version ID and review brief.
12. Collect notes and enter each into the feedback disposition log.

**Rough-cut exit criteria:** a complete, reviewable version exists; major story and structure questions have been addressed or listed; feedback is tied to the correct version.

### Phase D — Resolve feedback and make the fine cut

1. Sort notes by type: defect, brief requirement, creative preference, unclear, conflicting or out-of-scope.
2. Confirm the authorized decision owner where notes conflict.
3. Record each response: accept, reject with rationale, defer, request clarification or mark duplicate.
4. Preserve the reviewed version before implementing a new round.
5. Refine source in/out points to improve performance, action and emotional timing.
6. Inspect adjacent shots for continuity, eyelines, screen direction, prop state, movement and changes in light or camera position.
7. Refine dialogue overlaps, J-cuts, L-cuts, room tone and audio transitions without implying a temporary mix is final.
8. Review transitions at normal speed and in context. Avoid effects that hide structural problems.
9. Check the start and end of every scene, including scene transitions.
10. Review the full programme after local changes. A trim that improves one moment may damage the next.
11. Verify runtime, sequence identity and outstanding placeholders.
12. Produce a fine-cut review copy and updated change log.

**Fine-cut exit criteria:** structure and performance choices are stable; continuity and timing have been reviewed; remaining issues are listed and have an owner or disposition.

### Phase E — Picture-lock approval

1. Confirm that all required picture decisions and stakeholder notes have a recorded disposition.
2. Confirm required shots, titles, graphics and VFX placeholders are approved or explicitly listed as exceptions.
3. Confirm the exact sequence/project version and runtime.
4. Review the full picture at normal speed, including first frame, last frame, scene transitions and known risk areas.
5. Confirm the project opens and required source media is online or relink instructions are documented.
6. Prepare a lock record with project name, sequence ID, version, date, runtime, frame rate, approver, approval evidence and exceptions.
7. Obtain explicit approval from the authorized person. If unavailable, label status **Proposed lock — not approved**.
8. Preserve the approved state using the production's backup, access-control or archive process.
9. Prepare turnover notes and notify downstream departments of the lock version and exceptions.
10. Route every subsequent picture change through a change request and affected-department notification.

**Picture-lock exit criteria:** approval is traceable to an exact version; timing and exceptions are documented; downstream teams know the authoritative version.

## Worked example — 3-minute dramatic scene

**Brief:** A courier arrives at a small office to deliver an envelope. The recipient recognizes the sender's name, hesitates and decides not to open it. Target runtime: approximately 3 minutes. Available material: one master, two medium angles, two close-ups, an envelope insert, three performance takes and production sound.

### Step 1: Assembly decisions

The editor places the scene in story order: office master and courier entrance; courier offers the envelope; recipient asks who sent it; courier gives the name; recipient reacts; recipient accepts the envelope; recipient places it unopened on the desk; courier exits while the recipient remains.

All three reaction takes remain available in the selects record. The assembly is 3:42 because it preserves pauses, repeated dialogue and alternate coverage. It is complete but not yet shaped.

### Step 2: Rough-cut decisions

Review reveals that the courier repeats the sender's name and the master shot holds after the courier has left. The editor removes the repeated line, selects the clearest performance and tests a shorter exit. The reaction remains long enough for recognition to register.

| Section | Assembly | Rough cut | Reason |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Entrance and offer | 0:48 | 0:39 | Remove repeated approach coverage |
| Sender reveal | 0:56 | 0:52 | Preserve reveal and reaction |
| Acceptance | 0:51 | 0:43 | Remove duplicated hand movement |
| Exit and ending | 1:07 | 0:46 | Keep final decision, shorten redundant tail |
| **Total** | **3:42** | **3:00** | Structural compression, not arbitrary speed |

These are hypothetical teaching values, not measured footage timings.

### Step 3: Fine-cut decisions

The editor compares reaction takes. Take B has the most believable recognition; Take C has a useful hand movement but a continuity mismatch in envelope position. The editor uses Take B for the face reaction and a compatible insert for the envelope. A short sound bridge preserves office ambience across the courier's exit.

The editor checks the cut at normal speed and inspects the envelope handoff frame-by-frame. A one-frame prop jump is found and corrected using a better source range.

### Step 4: Lock decision

The director approves sequence OFFICE_SC03_FINE_v006, runtime 03:00, at the project's recorded frame rate. The lock record names the approver and date and notes one temporary VFX marker that must be replaced downstream. The picture is approved with that exception; the VFX team receives the exact reference and timecode.

**Expected result:** three distinguishable editorial stages, a traceable fine cut, a complete feedback log and an explicit lock state. The example does not imply that an actual project or export has been executed.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

Use a fictional 90-second scene with six story beats:

| Beat | Description | Initial duration | Editorial question |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Character enters | 12 s | Is geography clear? |
| 2 | Notices a damaged photograph | 15 s | Does the audience see what changed? |
| 3 | Picks it up | 18 s | Is the action continuous? |
| 4 | Recognizes a person | 20 s | Is the reaction readable? |
| 5 | Hides the photograph | 15 s | Is the decision clear? |
| 6 | Leaves frame | 10 s | Does the ending land? |
| | **Total** | **90 s** | |

**Assembly trace**

- Put all six beats in order.
- Include the best available coverage and retain alternate reactions in a separate selects record.
- Mark the damaged photograph insert as essential.
- Record any missing reverse angle as a gap, not as a shot that exists.

**Rough-cut trace**

- Review whether Beat 2 reveals the damage before the reaction in Beat 4.
- Test whether Beat 3 can be shortened without losing the hand action.
- Check whether Beat 5 communicates deliberate concealment rather than accidental movement.
- If the cut runs 82 seconds, record which eight seconds were removed and why. Do not assume 82 seconds is automatically better than 90.

**Fine-cut trace**

- Compare two reaction performances.
- Match hand position and photograph orientation across the insert.
- Review the sound of the photograph being handled and room ambience.
- Watch the whole 82-second version and check that the ending still communicates the character's choice.

**Lock trace**

- Create a hypothetical lock record only after the authorized reviewer approves the exact version.
- If approval has not been obtained, set status to “Proposed lock — not approved.”
- If a later request changes the photograph insert, record old and new timecodes and notify any department whose work depends on that shot.

## Guided practice

### Exercise A — Stage identification (30 minutes)

Label these actions by their primary stage: selecting all usable takes; testing story order; refining a reaction by frames; recording approval of an exact sequence; issuing a post-lock change request. Explain why each action belongs there. Some tasks may recur at multiple stages; state the dominant purpose.

### Exercise B — Rough-cut diagnosis (60 minutes)

Use a 1–2 minute dialogue scene you created or are licensed to edit.

1. Write the scene objective and six to ten beats.
2. Build a complete assembly.
3. Watch without stopping and write at least five observations.
4. Create a rough cut addressing at least three structural issues.
5. Record before/after runtime and identify removed beats or shots.
6. Ask a peer to summarize what happened without reading the script.
7. Compare the peer's understanding with the intended scene meaning.

**Success criteria:** the scene is complete, the intended change is understandable, the peer can identify the main conflict, and all source media remains recoverable.

### Exercise C — Fine-cut and lock simulation (60 minutes)

1. Select two adjacent edits that need refinement.
2. Record original and revised source ranges.
3. Explain the performance, continuity or rhythm improvement.
4. Review the full sequence after each change.
5. Complete a lock record with approval marked confirmed or not yet obtained.
6. Create a hypothetical change request for one proposed post-lock alteration.

**Success criteria:** changes are traceable, no prior approved version is silently overwritten, and lock status is honest.

## Independent assignment — Editorial progression portfolio

Create a 3–5 minute narrative scene using original or properly licensed footage. A documentary exercise may be used only if the edit preserves factual context and does not create misleading chronology or causation.

### Required deliverables

1. One-page brief, intended audience and target runtime.
2. Script revision or beat sheet.
3. Source-media inventory and rights/consent record.
4. Selects log with source in/out points and take notes.
5. Assembly version and short structural report.
6. Rough-cut version and review copy.
7. Feedback disposition log with at least eight notes or clearly identified simulated notes.
8. Fine-cut version and rationale for at least five meaningful refinements.
9. Proposed or approved picture-lock record.
10. Change request showing how a post-lock alteration would be controlled.
11. Short reflection (500–800 words) describing what was removed, what was preserved and why.

### Submission rules

- Keep originals untouched.
- Label simulated feedback as simulated.
- Do not invent approver names or claim approval that did not occur.
- If no external reviewer is available, record “self-review only; independent approval not obtained.”
- Include application and version used, but do not claim software features were tested unless they were.
- If a deliverable cannot be created, explain the blocker and provide available evidence.

## Common errors, diagnosis and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assembly is incomplete | Missing beats or unreviewed media | Compare beat sheet with timeline and inventory | Locate valid coverage or record a missing-shot exception |
| Assembly is polished but confusing | Effects prioritized before structure | Review story without temporary polish | Return to beat order, motivation and audience knowledge |
| Rough cut is still too long | Repetition or redundant coverage remains | Mark each shot's dramatic function | Remove repetition only after checking information and performance loss |
| Rough cut is too short and unclear | Compression removed necessary action or reaction | Ask an uninformed viewer to summarize the scene | Restore minimum visual or dialogue information needed |
| Performance feels inconsistent | Takes combined without checking emotional progression | Compare delivery, eyelines, body position and intention | Select a coherent performance or motivated cutaway |
| Fine cut has many tiny edits but no improvement | Local trimming without whole-scene review | Compare full-scene playback and beat progression | Restore stronger version; refine only edits with a clear purpose |
| Reviewer notes conflict | No single decision owner | Inspect review brief and stakeholder roles | Request an authorized decision and document resolution |
| Wrong version was reviewed | Export and sequence IDs do not match | Compare filename, slate/metadata and sequence record | Reissue correct review copy and invalidate ambiguous notes |
| Project fails to relink | Media paths or identifiers changed | Compare inventory and project references | Restore known paths or relink by verified media identity |
| Post-lock change breaks sound or VFX timing | Change control was bypassed | Compare locked and new versions | Issue change request, notify teams and repeat impacted QC |
| Picture lock claimed without approval | Editorial completion confused with approval | Inspect lock record and approval evidence | Mark proposed/unapproved until authorization exists |
| Archive cannot be reopened | Dependencies or project version undocumented | Test a copy in intended environment | Gather missing media, fonts, plugins or version notes; record gaps |

## Knowledge check

1. What question does an assembly answer that a fine cut does not primarily answer?
2. Why preserve alternate takes during early editorial stages?
3. Name four structural questions to ask when reviewing a rough cut.
4. Explain the difference between an objective defect and a creative preference.
5. Why should feedback be tied to a version ID and timecode?
6. What is the purpose of a feedback disposition log?
7. Give three examples of downstream work affected by a picture change.
8. What information must a picture-lock record contain?
9. What should an editor do if approval has not been obtained?
10. Why is a post-lock change request necessary even when the trim is only one frame?
11. What is the difference between a temporary placeholder and approved final content?
12. Why review the full programme after local fine-cut changes?

## Answer key and instructor guidance

1. Assembly tests whether selected material can form a complete, followable structure; fine cut refines exact editorial choices.
2. Alternatives may contain stronger performance, continuity or story information. Early commitment can remove useful options.
3. Story completeness, cause/effect, point of view, geography, motivation, duplicated information, emotional progression and runtime are valid examples.
4. An objective defect can be checked against an agreed fact or specification; a creative preference expresses a desired interpretation or emphasis.
5. It ensures the note refers to the same editorial state and lets the editor locate the intended moment.
6. It records whether each note was accepted, rejected, deferred, clarified or treated as duplicate, including rationale where needed.
7. Sound/music cues, VFX, titles, subtitles/captions, color, credits and mastering/delivery timing.
8. Project/sequence identity, version ID, runtime, timing basis, approver, approval date/evidence, status and exceptions.
9. Mark the version proposed or awaiting approval; do not claim picture lock.
10. One frame can shift sync, cue timing, subtitles, effects or runtime. Assess and communicate the change.
11. A placeholder is provisional and clearly labeled; final content is approved for intended use.
12. Local changes can alter scene rhythm, transitions, continuity and the meaning of adjacent beats.

**Instructor guidance:** Accept equivalent answers when reasoning is accurate. For Questions 8–10, require traceability and downstream impact. Fabricated approval, overwritten source material or ignored rights/consent fails the professional-process gate regardless of score.

## Assessment rubric (100 points)

| Criterion | Points | Full-credit evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Stage understanding and editorial reasoning | 15 | Distinguishes assembly, rough, fine and lock; explains decisions in story terms |
| Source organization and preservation | 10 | Accurate inventory, stable IDs, rights notes and recoverable project |
| Assembly completeness | 10 | Intended beats represented; missing coverage and alternatives recorded |
| Rough-cut structure and diagnosis | 15 | Complete reviewable version; structural improvements supported by observations |
| Fine-cut performance and continuity | 15 | Refined timing, performance, geography, action and sound; full-sequence review |
| Feedback and version management | 10 | Notes tied to versions/timecodes and transparently dispositioned |
| Picture-lock/change-control evidence | 15 | Accurate lock state, approval evidence or honest unapproved status, exceptions and change request |
| Professional reflection and handoff | 10 | Clear rationale, limitations, downstream dependencies and organized submission |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Pass threshold:** 75/100, with no critical failure in source preservation, rights/consent, version identity or truthful approval reporting. A numerical pass does not convert an unapproved proposed lock into an approved lock.

**Remediation:** If below 75, revise the lowest-scoring stage and resubmit its evidence. If failure concerns source loss, rights, fabricated approval or an untraceable version, correct the process and repeat the relevant quality gate.

## Quality-control checklist

### Editorial
- [ ] Brief, audience and target runtime are recorded.
- [ ] Scene beats and required information are present.
- [ ] Assembly, rough and fine versions are distinguishable.
- [ ] Performance choices have been compared.
- [ ] Geography, eyelines, screen direction and action continuity are checked.
- [ ] Pacing and scene-to-scene flow have been reviewed at normal speed.
- [ ] Temporary elements and missing shots are clearly identified.

### Review and version control
- [ ] Every review copy has a unique version ID.
- [ ] Notes identify the reviewed version and relevant timecode.
- [ ] Each note has a disposition and, where needed, rationale.
- [ ] Conflicting or ambiguous notes are escalated rather than silently guessed.
- [ ] Previous versions and source media remain recoverable.

### Picture lock and turnover
- [ ] Exact project and sequence identity are recorded.
- [ ] Runtime and timing basis are recorded.
- [ ] Approval authority, date and evidence are present, or status is explicitly unapproved.
- [ ] Exceptions and placeholders are listed.
- [ ] Post-lock change-control route is documented.
- [ ] Sound, VFX, titles, subtitles, color and delivery dependencies are identified.
- [ ] Handoff package and relink instructions are understandable.

### Professional responsibility
- [ ] Rights, releases and consent are accounted for.
- [ ] Confidential material is not shared beyond authorization.
- [ ] No fabricated approval, execution or validation claim is present.
- [ ] Accessibility and language requirements in the brief are considered.
- [ ] Limitations and unresolved issues are reported.
