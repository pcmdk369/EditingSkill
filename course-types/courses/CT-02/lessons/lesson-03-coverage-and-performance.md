# CT-02 Lesson 03 — Shot Coverage, Performance and Editorial Choice

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Beginner → Intermediate
- Estimated time: 4–5 hours, including source review, logging, two edit comparisons and reflection
- Prerequisites: Lessons 01–02 recommended; ability to identify a dramatic beat, describe scene geography, read source timecode and distinguish a shot from a take
- Tools/environment: Authorized source footage; source inventory; paper, spreadsheet or database selects log; any playback or NLE environment capable of reviewing the source. This lesson is tool-independent.
- Version/review date: 2.0 — 2026-11-21
- Status: Expanded learner-facing lesson; independent review and practical validation pending

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

1. Classify at least eight common coverage forms and explain the dramatic or informational function each candidate shot could serve in a named beat.
2. Inventory all in-scope takes with stable source IDs, source ranges, camera/angle, take number, usable handles and known restrictions.
3. Record observable performance evidence separately from interpretation, including gesture, breath, pause, gaze, vocal delivery, listening response and emotional progression.
4. Compare at least three candidate takes against five editorial dimensions: dramatic value, performance, continuity, technical usability and editorial function.
5. Apply an anchored 1–5 rating scale and a documented weighting model as a decision aid, then identify critical gates that override a numeric score.
6. Build a beat-to-coverage map that identifies primary coverage, alternatives, missing information and continuity risks for every major beat.
7. Construct two short edit plans: one that prioritizes performance continuity and one that tests purposeful visual variety, then justify the selected version with source evidence.
8. Explain why a reaction, insert, close-up, master or cutaway is included, deferred or rejected without inventing an event or changing the meaning of a performance.
9. Submit a traceable selects reel or paper equivalent, editorial decision log, QC record and limitations statement.

## Prerequisites and readiness check

Review the following before starting:

- Lesson 01: dramatic beats, audience knowledge, source-linked paper edits and the difference between an action and a meaningful change.
- Lesson 02: scene geography, axis of action, screen position, eyelines and spatial continuity.
- Basic source-media vocabulary: scene, camera, angle, shot, take, source in/out, timeline position and handles.

**Readiness check:** Given a short dialogue scene, write one sentence describing its dramatic objective, identify three meaningful beats and sketch the spatial relationship between the participants. Then identify one shot that could communicate each beat. If the proposed shots do not exist in the source inventory, record a coverage gap rather than assuming that the footage contains them.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Editorial context |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage | The set of recorded shots and takes that provides options for constructing a scene | Useful coverage offers choices that support story, performance, geography and action |
| Master shot | A shot that covers the whole scene or a substantial continuous portion | Establishes relationships and may preserve simultaneous performances |
| Two-shot | A composition containing two subjects | Shows interaction, distance, power or shared reaction |
| Single | A shot that isolates one subject | Focuses attention on one participant without automatically proving a reaction |
| Over-the-shoulder (OTS) | A shot framed past or beside one subject toward another | Maintains a visible relationship in dialogue |
| Close-up | A tight view of a face, object or detail | Emphasizes a response, discovery or detail |
| Insert | A close view of a specific object or action | Clarifies an object state or a meaningful physical action |
| Cutaway | A view away from the principal action | May provide context, contrast, transition or a truthful pause |
| Reaction shot | A shot that shows a subject responding to an event or information | Can change audience interpretation; must be supported by the recorded performance and chronology |
| Point-of-view (POV) shot | A view presented as what a character sees | Requires a plausible relationship to the character's position and gaze |
| Take | One recorded attempt at a shot or performance | Alternate takes may differ in timing, behavior, framing and technical quality |
| Select | A source range considered useful for an edit | Must retain source ID and in/out information |
| Selects reel | An ordered review sequence of candidate ranges | Makes alternatives easier to compare; it is not necessarily a finished scene |
| Coverage map | A record linking beats or information needs to available shots and takes | Reveals options and missing coverage before detailed cutting |
| Handles | Extra source frames before or after the intended action/range | Allow timing adjustments; no universal handle length applies to every scene |
| Performance continuity | The coherence of behavior, timing, gaze, gesture, voice and emotional progression across selected material | A cut can be technically smooth yet interrupt the performance |
| Performance observation | A description of what is visibly or audibly present in the source | Example: “the actor pauses, looks down, then exhales” |
| Performance interpretation | A cautious explanation of what an observed behavior may communicate | Example: “the pause may suggest hesitation”; it is not a fact about the actor's internal state |
| Editorial function | The specific information or dramatic task a shot performs in the edit | Establish, reveal, connect, contrast, clarify, emphasize or allow a response |
| Coverage gap | Information or action needed by the edit that is not adequately present in available footage | Must be disclosed; it cannot be repaired by inventing source evidence |
| Selects decision | A recorded choice to include, reject, defer or hold a source range | Includes rationale, evidence, risk and version |

## Purpose and professional relevance

Coverage gives the editor options, but the number of recorded shots is not a measure of their usefulness. The professional task is to find the minimum set of shots that communicates the scene's necessary information while preserving meaningful performance, coherent space and truthful chronology.

Editors regularly work with uneven coverage: a master may be complete but emotionally distant; a close-up may contain the only convincing pause but have a technical defect; a reaction may be strong but belong to a different moment; an insert may be sharp but show the wrong hand or prop state. Editorial judgment is the process of evaluating these competing needs and documenting the trade-off.

A coverage and performance analysis supports:

- identifying the shot that best communicates each dramatic beat;
- protecting listening, silence, hesitation and non-verbal behavior;
- comparing alternate takes without relying only on technical polish;
- avoiding excessive cutting when a two-shot or master already carries the scene;
- identifying gaps before a scene is assembled;
- explaining rejected shots and accepted compromises to a director, producer or reviewer;
- preserving source traceability and the integrity of documentary or factual material.

**Professional principle:** A rating sheet can structure discussion; it cannot determine artistic truth. A lower-scoring shot may be necessary if it contains a unique, essential performance moment, but the accepted risk must be explicit and must not violate a critical rights, safety, factual or continuity requirement.

## Theory and principles

### 1. Coverage is a set of editorial options, not a shot-count target

A scene may be adequately covered by a continuous master, or it may need multiple angles to communicate changes in attention, action and point of view. More coverage can create more choices, but also more continuity problems and more review time.

Ask three questions for every candidate:
1. What information or performance does this shot add?
2. Is that information already communicated more clearly elsewhere?
3. Does the shot create a new continuity, chronology, rights or interpretation risk?

If a shot adds no useful information and weakens the scene's rhythm or performance, do not include it merely because it exists.

### 2. Begin with the dramatic beat, then map the coverage

The beat map identifies what changes. The coverage map identifies which recorded material can communicate that change.

For each beat, record:
- the audience's information need;
- the subject whose behavior matters;
- the action or response that carries the change;
- candidate shot IDs and source ranges;
- primary and alternate coverage;
- spatial, temporal, performance and technical risks;
- the reason for the final choice.

This prevents the editor from starting with a favorite close-up and forcing the scene around it. It also makes missing coverage visible before the edit is committed.

### 3. Understand what each coverage type can and cannot do

- **Master:** can preserve the timing and interaction of several performers. It may be less intimate, but it can protect the integrity of simultaneous behavior.
- **Two-shot:** can show the relationship between people and allow the viewer to compare their behavior in the same moment.
- **Single:** can direct attention to one person. It does not automatically establish what that person knows or when a response occurred.
- **OTS:** can preserve a visual relationship and useful eyeline context. It may be less suitable if the foreground blocks important action.
- **Close-up:** can reveal subtle behavior or detail. It can also remove context, overstate emotion or create an unsupported emphasis.
- **Insert:** can clarify a meaningful object or action. Verify the hand, orientation, state and chronology against the source.
- **Cutaway:** can provide context or a pause. Do not use an unrelated object or reaction to imply a fact that the source does not support.
- **Reaction:** can show listening and response. Its timing is critical: a reaction placed before the information is delivered may falsely imply prior knowledge.
- **POV:** can align the viewer with a character's perception. Check the preceding gaze, camera position and plausible view.
- **Tracking or moving coverage:** can preserve movement and spatial relationships, but may be harder to combine with static coverage if the movement path changes.

These are functional descriptions, not mandatory rules. A shot's dramatic use depends on framing, performance, blocking, sound, context and the edit.

### 4. Evaluate performance through observable evidence

Avoid vague notes such as “best acting” or “more emotional” without evidence. Separate observation from interpretation.

| Observable evidence | Possible editorial relevance |
|---|---|
| Pause before a line | May allow a decision or realization to register |
| Breath or exhale | May preserve tension, effort or release |
| Change in gaze | May reveal attention, avoidance or a new target |
| Gesture begins or stops | May support a beat or create a continuity issue |
| Listening response | May show that information has landed |
| Vocal pace or emphasis | May change intention, clarity or relationship |
| Body orientation | May preserve geography and power relationship |
| Overlapping dialogue | May communicate interruption or conflict |
| Silence after a line | May preserve a response that dialogue alone does not express |
| Background movement | May affect continuity or reveal another event |

Record what can be seen or heard. Then write a separate interpretation using cautious language such as “may indicate,” “appears to,” or “supports the reading that.” Do not claim to know an actor's private intention from a facial expression.

### 5. Performance continuity is more than matching gestures

When combining takes, compare the full performance arc:

1. **Preparation:** posture, breath, gaze and emotional state before the line/action.
2. **Trigger:** the event or information that changes the behavior.
3. **Response:** vocal, facial or physical reaction.
4. **Adjustment:** a pause, changed tactic, movement or decision.
5. **After-state:** the behavior that remains after the beat.

A cut may match hand position but interrupt the breath or remove the moment of listening. Conversely, a small gesture mismatch may be acceptable if the emotional and spatial progression remains clear. Evaluate the whole performance, not a single frame in isolation.

### 6. A weighted score is a discussion aid

The original lesson's five dimensions remain useful:

| Dimension | Suggested weight | What to examine |
|---|---:|---|
| Dramatic value | 30% | Does the shot communicate the beat and character objective? |
| Performance | 25% | Is the behavior specific, coherent and useful to the scene? |
| Continuity | 20% | Can it connect without an unintended spatial, action or performance jump? |
| Technical usability | 15% | Are focus, exposure, movement, sound and artifacts acceptable for the intended use? |
| Editorial function | 10% | Does it establish, reveal, emphasize, connect or provide needed context? |
| **Total** | **100%** | |

Use a 1–5 anchored scale for each dimension. Convert a rating to weighted points using:

\[
\text{Weighted points}=\frac{\text{rating}}{5}\times\text{dimension weight}
\]

A score is not a universal industry standard. The suggested weights are a teaching model. An instructor or production may adjust them, but must record the chosen model and use it consistently within one comparison.

**Critical gates override the total.** Do not select a shot that violates source authorization, participant consent, factual integrity or a non-negotiable delivery requirement. A critical continuity or chronology defect also requires a documented resolution or an explicit decision to omit the shot. Do not hide a serious defect inside a high average.

### 7. Technical quality is contextual, not a beauty contest

Technical usability depends on the intended delivery and the defect's visibility and impact. A soft-focus frame may be acceptable for a brief, motivated transition but unacceptable for a long close-up that carries essential information. A noisy audio take may be unusable if the words cannot be verified, even when the picture is strong.

Record:
- the exact source range affected;
- whether the defect is constant or momentary;
- whether it affects comprehension or only polish;
- whether an authorized repair is possible;
- whether the repair introduces another risk;
- whether the accepted compromise is approved.

Do not claim that software can recover detail or repair an artifact unless that operation is tested in the named environment. Enhancement can create plausible but false detail.

### 8. Selects reels and source traceability

A selects reel is a review aid. Each entry must retain a stable source ID and source in/out, and should include a brief note about its purpose. A timeline position alone is not enough: it changes when the edit is rearranged.

Keep separate:
- **source range:** the in/out from the original media;
- **select ID:** the chosen candidate range;
- **timeline position:** where it is used in one edit version;
- **version ID:** the edit state in which the decision appears.

If a source range changes, update the select record rather than silently reusing an outdated note.

### 9. Reaction shots and editorial truth

A reaction shot is powerful because it changes the audience's access to information. It can suggest agreement, doubt, shock, indifference or knowledge. That power creates responsibility.

Before using a reaction, verify:
- the recorded event that the person is responding to;
- the source chronology and take relationship;
- whether the reaction belongs to the same scene moment;
- whether the eyeline and spatial relation are plausible;
- whether the edit changes the apparent meaning of the person's behavior.

In fictional editing, a constructed reaction may be part of the intended storytelling if it remains consistent with the scene's dramatic truth. In documentary, journalism, factual, educational or evidentiary contexts, moving a reaction can misrepresent what occurred. Follow the applicable editorial policy and disclose constructed chronology where required.

### 10. Build alternatives before committing

A useful comparison is not “many cuts versus few cuts” in the abstract. Create two versions with a stated hypothesis:

- **Performance-led version:** prioritize the strongest continuous behavior and listening relationships; use a master or two-shot when it carries simultaneous performance.
- **Coverage-led version:** test close-ups, inserts and reactions where they add specific information or emphasis.

Keep the source material and sequence boundaries consistent where possible. Record every intentional difference. Compare the versions against the scene objective, not only personal preference.

### 11. Accessibility and fair evaluation

Use text labels and stable IDs rather than color alone. Provide written descriptions of relevant visual behavior where a reviewer cannot access the footage. When evaluating performances, focus on the scene's requirements and observable communication, not a learner's assumptions about identity, disability, accent, age or cultural mannerisms. Do not treat one style of expression as the universal definition of truthful performance.

## Reproducible procedure

### Phase A — Define scope and protect the source

1. Record the project/scene ID, intended audience, dramatic objective and required deliverable.
2. Confirm source ownership, license/permission, participant consent and any restrictions on use or cloud processing.
3. Identify the exact media and takes in scope. Preserve camera originals and work from authorized copies or references.
4. Confirm the scene's beat map and geography map. Record unresolved questions instead of assuming an answer.
5. Define the review boundary: which takes, camera angles, source ranges and languages are included.

### Phase B — Inventory and review

6. Assign or preserve a stable source ID for every clip/take. Do not rename source media in a way that breaks its identity.
7. Record scene, camera, angle, shot size, take, source duration, frame rate/timebase if known and any transform or proxy relationship.
8. Watch every in-scope take in full at normal speed before selecting short ranges.
9. Make a first-pass note about dramatic function and performance. Do not rate a take solely from a thumbnail or isolated frame.
10. Mark candidate source in/out ranges. Include enough context to understand the preparation and aftermath of the action.
11. Record usable handles and any point where the take begins mid-action or ends before the performance resolves.
12. Note technical defects by exact source range and describe whether they are constant, intermittent or uncertain.

### Phase C — Analyze performance and coverage

13. For each candidate, record observable behavior separately from interpretation: pause, breath, gaze, gesture, vocal pace, overlap, listening response and body position.
14. Link each candidate to one or more dramatic beats and state its proposed editorial function.
15. Complete the five-dimension comparison using the anchored rating scale or a qualitative explanation.
16. Apply critical gates before comparing totals. Hold any candidate with unresolved authorization, chronology, factual or critical continuity concerns.
17. Build the beat-to-coverage map. Identify primary shot, alternate, unique performance information, coverage gap and risk for each beat.
18. Compare master/two-shot options against the proposed single/close-up construction. Check whether simultaneous performance is lost when the scene is fragmented.

### Phase D — Build and compare

19. Assemble a performance-led paper edit or sequence using the strongest coherent performance and the least disruptive coverage.
20. Create a second coverage-led alternative with a stated hypothesis about the information or emotion each additional cut should provide.
21. Add reaction shots, inserts and cutaways only when their source, timing, eyeline and function are defensible.
22. Watch both versions uninterrupted, then review the critical adjacent cuts. Record any performance, geography, chronology or technical discontinuity.
23. Ask an unbriefed reviewer to describe the scene's objective, the key change and any moment that felt unclear or emotionally interrupted. Do not explain the intended reading first.
24. Revise only after identifying the shot relationship or performance detail that caused the problem. Record the reason and retain the prior version.

### Phase E — Document and hand off

25. Save the selects log, coverage map, two version IDs, decision log, viewer/reviewer notes and QC checklist.
26. Record rejected alternatives and accepted technical compromises.
27. Label paper-only or simulated work clearly. Do not claim a timeline, selects reel, render, export or viewer test was executed unless it was.
28. Preserve source identity and earlier edit versions. Provide a concise handoff note listing open gaps and decisions requiring director/client approval.

## Worked example

**Brief/input — fictional dialogue scene:** Mira tells Arun that she has accepted a job in another city. Arun tries to remain calm. The scene's objective is to reveal a change in their relationship without making Arun's response melodramatic.

**Fictional source inventory:**

| Source ID | Coverage | Take | Source range | Observation |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| CAM-A-T01 | Two-shot/master | 1 | 00:00–00:42 | Both performers visible; timing is continuous |
| CAM-B-T02 | Mira single | 2 | 00:08–00:31 | Clear line delivery; quick glance before final phrase |
| CAM-C-T01 | Arun close-up | 1 | 00:12–00:35 | Restrained pause, looks down, then returns gaze |
| CAM-D-T03 | Hand insert | 3 | 00:20–00:24 | Fingers tighten around a paper cup |
| CAM-E-T01 | Arun alternate close-up | 1 | 00:11–00:33 | Strong vocal breath; focus softens near end |
| CAM-F-T02 | Mira reaction | 2 | 00:25–00:39 | Smile begins before the line finishes; chronology requires checking |

All IDs, ranges and observations above are simulated teaching data, not actual media.

### Candidate score comparison

Ratings are illustrative, on a 1–5 scale. The suggested weights are dramatic value 30%, performance 25%, continuity 20%, technical usability 15% and editorial function 10%.

| Candidate | Dramatic | Performance | Continuity | Technical | Function |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| CAM-A-T01 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 4 |
| CAM-C-T01 | 5 | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 |
| CAM-E-T01 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| CAM-D-T03 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 5 | 3 |

For CAM-C-T01: (5/5 × 30) + (5/5 × 25) + (4/5 × 20) + (4/5 × 15) + (5/5 × 10) = 30 + 25 + 16 + 12 + 10 = **93/100**.

The score helps compare the candidate, but it does not authorize the cut. The source chronology, eyeline and action state still need verification.

### Decision process

1. Use the master/two-shot to establish the relationship and preserve both performers during the reveal.
2. Test CAM-B-T02 for the line, but retain the full phrase and enough context to see Mira's glance.
3. Use CAM-C-T01 after the information lands if the recorded pause belongs to that moment and the eyeline is compatible.
4. Hold CAM-E-T01 as an alternate. Its performance may be valuable, but the focus softness must be reviewed in context and against delivery requirements.
5. Use CAM-D-T03 only if the cup action is part of the same chronology and communicates a meaningful physical response. Do not insert it simply to create visual variety.
6. Do not use CAM-F-T02 until its smile timing is verified. If the smile precedes the information, placing it after the line could create a false reaction.
7. Compare a version that stays in the master through the reveal with a version that cuts to Arun's close-up. Ask which preserves the relationship and makes the emotional change understandable.

**Expected output:** A traceable selects log and a 30–60 second scene edit or paper edit, plus a written rationale for the chosen performance, the rejected reaction and any accepted technical compromise.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

The following is a simulated paper exercise. It does not represent footage that has been edited or reviewed in an NLE.

### A. Beat-to-coverage map

| Beat | Audience needs to understand | Primary coverage | Alternate | Gap/risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Mira prepares to speak | Both people are together | CAM-A-T01 | CAM-B-T02 | Confirm both are in the same time window |
| 2. Mira reveals the move | Information and delivery | CAM-A-T01 | CAM-B-T02 | Do not cut before the key phrase completes |
| 3. Arun receives the news | A response occurs | CAM-C-T01 | CAM-E-T01 | Verify reaction chronology and focus |
| 4. Arun tries to respond | Effort to remain calm | CAM-A-T01 | CAM-C-T01 | Avoid removing the breath/pause |
| 5. Physical tension | Cup handling may reveal behavior | CAM-D-T03 | Master | Confirm hand and cup state |
| 6. Mira waits | Listening and relationship | CAM-A-T01 | CAM-F-T02 | Verify smile timing; possible false reaction |

### B. Annotated paper-edit trace

| Order | Shot/source range | In-edit purpose | Check before approval |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | CAM-A-T01, 00:00–00:13 | Establish both performers and the conversation | Geography and full context |
| 2 | CAM-B-T02, 00:08–00:20 | Carry Mira's reveal | Phrase continuity and source chronology |
| 3 | CAM-C-T01, 00:12–00:23 | Show Arun's listening response | Eyeline, breath and reaction timing |
| 4 | CAM-A-T01, 00:20–00:31 | Return to both performers | Does the master preserve simultaneous behavior? |
| 5 | CAM-D-T03, 00:20–00:24 | Optional cup insert | Hand/cup state and factual timing |
| 6 | CAM-A-T01, 00:31–00:42 | Hold the aftermath | Does the ending allow the response to register? |

The ranges are not instructions to overlap all source durations literally; they are source references for a paper plan. A real timeline must be built from actual frame-accurate source material and the chosen project timebase.

### C. Dry-run decision branches

- **If the master carries the reveal and both reactions:** retain it longer; do not add singles just to increase cut count.
- **If Arun's close-up contains a distinct listening beat:** cut to it only after the information is delivered and the gaze relationship is compatible.
- **If the alternate close-up is soft:** assess the defect at intended display size and duration. If it harms essential information, reject it or seek an approved alternative.
- **If the cup insert is from another take/action state:** omit it or clearly treat it as a separate temporal construction only where the genre and source integrity permit.
- **If the reaction's chronology is uncertain:** do not present it as proof of a response to that line. Record the uncertainty and use the master or another verified option.
- **If the scene feels overcut:** compare a version with fewer transitions and identify which information each removed cut had been adding.

**Dry-run stop condition:** If a shot's source identity, chronology, rights or essential action state cannot be verified, do not present the proposed edit as a factual representation of that event.

## Guided practice

### Exercise 1 — Coverage classification and function (20 minutes)

Use an authorized scene inventory containing at least eight shot types. Classify each shot and write the beat or information need it can serve. Identify one shot whose framing suggests one function but whose performance supports another.

**Hint:** Describe what the viewer can see and hear before writing what the shot means.

**Success criteria:** At least eight correct classifications; each has a specific function; assumptions and missing information are marked.

### Exercise 2 — Performance observation versus interpretation (25 minutes)

Review three short takes of the same line or action. Write two columns: observable behavior and possible interpretation. Include at least five observations per take.

**Hint:** “Looks down for two seconds” is an observation; “feels guilty” is an interpretation.

**Success criteria:** The observation column contains verifiable behavior; interpretations are cautious and linked to evidence.

### Exercise 3 — Weighted comparison and critical gates (25 minutes)

Rate three candidate takes using the five dimensions and the suggested weights. Then identify any critical gate that should override the score.

**Hint:** A high score cannot cure missing permission or a false chronology.

**Success criteria:** Arithmetic is correct, the rating model is stated, and at least one limitation of numerical scoring is explained.

### Exercise 4 — Two-version edit comparison (45–60 minutes)

Create a performance-led and a coverage-led edit of the same short scene. Keep source ranges traceable. Write a hypothesis for each version and compare them with an unbriefed reviewer.

**Hint:** Change one primary editorial variable at a time where feasible.

**Success criteria:** Two distinct version IDs; a decision log; reviewer prompt and response; a reasoned final selection.

## Common errors and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scene feels visually repetitive | Shots selected by size or technical quality rather than beat function | Map each cut to the information it adds | Use a purposeful master, two-shot, insert or reaction only where it contributes |
| Performance jumps between cuts | Gesture, breath, gaze or emotional state differs across takes | Compare the action arc and adjacent source ranges | Choose a compatible take or restructure around the master |
| Reaction feels artificial | Reaction is mistimed or from an unrelated moment | Verify chronology, eyeline and source context | Use an authentic reaction, retain the master or omit it |
| Only one take is considered | Incomplete source review | Compare inventory against all in-scope media | Review every in-scope take before final selects |
| Sharpest take feels emotionally weak | Technical polish dominates selection | Rate dramatic and performance value separately | Consider a truthful take if the defect is acceptable and approved |
| Selects cannot be found again | Timeline positions used instead of stable source IDs/ranges | Inspect the log for source references | Restore source IDs, source in/out and version identifiers |
| Too many inserts interrupt the scene | Inserts chosen for variety rather than information | Ask what each insert changes for the audience | Remove redundant inserts or reserve them for a meaningful beat |
| Master is abandoned too early | Assumption that close-ups are always more cinematic | Compare simultaneous behavior and scene rhythm | Retain the master where it carries both performances |
| Close-up implies unsupported emotion | Interpretation treated as fact | Separate observed behavior from inferred meaning | Use cautious language and test the cut in context |
| Accepted technical compromise is hidden | No defect/exception record | Inspect technical notes and review copy | Document source range, impact, approval and limitation |
| Weighted scores disagree with editorial judgment | Model is incomplete or context differs | Review ratings, weights and critical gates | Record the exception and explain the reason; do not manipulate scores to justify a preference |
| Reaction appears to precede the cause | Source chronology is unclear or edit order is misleading | Trace source time and dialogue/action sequence | Restore chronology or label the construction as intentional where permitted |
| Shot variety damages geography | Coverage is selected without the Lesson 02 map | Compare camera side, eyeline and screen position | Use compatible coverage or re-establish geography |
| Performance note is subjective or biased | Vague judgment replaces observable evidence | Re-write the note using visible/audible behavior | Describe behavior, context and editorial effect without identity-based assumptions |
| Missing coverage is concealed | Editor assumes a repair can be invented | Check whether the required action exists in source | Disclose the gap, use available truthful coverage or request authorized pickup material |

## Knowledge check

1. Define coverage. Why is the number of shots not a reliable measure of coverage quality?
2. Distinguish a master, two-shot, single, OTS, insert and cutaway.
3. What is the difference between an observable performance note and an interpretation?
4. Name the five editorial dimensions used in the suggested comparison model.
5. Calculate the weighted points for a rating of 4/5 in a dimension weighted at 25%.
6. Why must critical gates be checked before comparing weighted totals?
7. What information should a selects log retain so a range can be found again?
8. Give three reasons to retain a master or two-shot instead of cutting to a close-up.
9. What must be verified before using a reaction shot?
10. Explain why a technically imperfect take may be preferable to a technically clean take.
11. How can an insert create a continuity or factual problem?
12. What is a coverage gap, and how should it be documented?
13. Why should performance continuity include breath, gaze and listening behavior?
14. Describe a fair comparison between a performance-led edit and a coverage-led edit.
15. What is the limitation of a small unbriefed viewer test?
16. In what circumstances can rearranging a reaction shot misrepresent an event?

## Answer key / instructor guidance

1. Coverage is the set of shots/takes available to construct a scene. Quality depends on useful options for story, performance, geography and action, not volume.
2. A master covers a substantial scene; a two-shot frames two subjects; a single isolates one; OTS frames past/beside one subject toward another; an insert isolates an object/action; a cutaway moves away from the principal action for context or another function.
3. An observation describes visible/audible evidence; an interpretation proposes what it may mean and should be qualified.
4. Dramatic value, performance, continuity, technical usability and editorial function.
5. (4/5 × 25) = 20 weighted points.
6. Rights, consent, factual integrity or critical continuity requirements cannot be offset by a high average.
7. Stable source ID, take/camera/angle, source in/out, shot type, handles, notes and edit version/timeline reference where relevant.
8. The master may preserve simultaneous performance, geography, timing and relationship; it may avoid false reaction timing or excessive fragmentation.
9. Source chronology, triggering event, eyeline/spatial relationship, take context and whether the reaction changes the apparent meaning.
10. The clean take may lack the essential performance moment; the defect may be minor or acceptable in context, subject to delivery and approval.
11. The insert may show a different hand, prop orientation, state, time or take; it can imply an action that did not occur at that moment.
12. Needed information/action is absent or inadequate in available footage. Record the missing beat, source search performed, impact and possible authorized remedy.
13. They are part of the performance arc and can carry intention, timing and listening; matching only a hand position can still interrupt the scene.
14. State a hypothesis for each, keep source and sequence boundaries consistent where possible, record changed variables, compare against the scene objective and collect unbriefed feedback.
15. It is formative feedback from a small sample, not statistically representative audience research.
16. When it implies a response to information the person had not yet received, or otherwise changes factual chronology/meaning, especially in documentary or evidentiary contexts.

## Assessment rubric

| Criterion | Points | Evidence required |
|---|---:|---|
| Source authorization and inventory | 10 | Stable IDs, in-scope takes, permission/consent and preservation record |
| Beat and geography mapping | 10 | Clear beat objectives and usable spatial reference |
| Coverage classification and function | 10 | Correct shot types and specific editorial purposes |
| Performance observation and analysis | 15 | Observable evidence separated from cautious interpretation |
| Take comparison and decision model | 15 | Correct ratings/calculation, limitations and critical gates |
| Selects and two-version edit | 15 | Traceable ranges, distinct hypotheses and coherent edit plans |
| Editorial rationale and trade-offs | 10 | Defensible choices, rejected shot and accepted compromise |
| Viewer/reviewer evidence | 5 | Neutral prompt, response or explicit limitation |
| QC, accessibility and documentation | 10 | Complete checklist, versions, source traceability and integrity |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Pass threshold:** 70/100.

**Mandatory gates:** No unauthorized source use, fabricated source evidence, knowingly misleading reaction/chronology, undisclosed critical defect or unpreserved original. A paper-only simulation can demonstrate planning and reasoning but cannot be represented as an executed edit or learner-tested result.

### Remediation guidance

- If source inventory or traceability is below half the available points, rebuild the inventory and re-check all in-scope takes before selecting.
- If performance analysis is weak, repeat Exercise 2 with observation and interpretation in separate columns.
- If the weighted comparison is inaccurate, recalculate by hand and explain the model's limitations.
- If a reaction or insert is misleading, trace its source chronology and rebuild the edit around verified material.
- If viewer evidence is absent, disclose the limitation; do not invent responses.
- If authorization is uncertain, stop use until permission is confirmed.

## Quality-control checklist

### Source integrity and preparation
- [ ] Source ownership, license/permission and consent are recorded.
- [ ] Originals are preserved and source IDs remain stable.
- [ ] All in-scope takes were reviewed in full before selects were finalized.
- [ ] Scene objective, beat map and geography reference are available.
- [ ] Missing or uncertain coverage is disclosed.

### Coverage and performance
- [ ] Each selected shot has a specific beat/information function.
- [ ] Observable performance evidence is separate from interpretation.
- [ ] Performance arc includes preparation, trigger, response and after-state.
- [ ] Breath, gaze, gesture, voice and listening behavior were reviewed where relevant.
- [ ] Master/two-shot alternatives were considered.
- [ ] Reactions are chronologically and spatially defensible.
- [ ] Inserts match action and prop state or are explicitly justified.

### Technical and editorial decisions
- [ ] Technical defects are recorded by source range.
- [ ] Weighted scores use a stated model and correct arithmetic.
- [ ] Critical gates were applied before accepting a candidate.
- [ ] Accepted compromises have rationale and approval where required.
- [ ] Selects reel and two edit versions retain traceable source ranges.
- [ ] Prior versions and rejected alternatives are recoverable.

### Review, accessibility and handoff
- [ ] Reviewer prompt is neutral and does not reveal the intended interpretation.
- [ ] Viewer-test sample limitations are stated.
- [ ] Labels and tables do not rely on color alone.
- [ ] Rights, privacy, consent and factual integrity are addressed.
- [ ] Simulated, paper-only, unexecuted and untested evidence is labeled.
- [ ] Handoff lists open decisions and coverage gaps.
