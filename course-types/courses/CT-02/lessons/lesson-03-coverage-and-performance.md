# CT-02 Lesson 03 — Shot Coverage, Performance and Editorial Choice

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Beginner → Intermediate
- Estimated time: 3 hours
- Prerequisites: CT-02 Lessons 01–02 recommended; familiarity with source clips and shot types
- Tools/environment: NLE with source viewer and bins; spreadsheet or paper selects log
- Version/review date: 1.0 — 2026-11-16

## Learning outcomes
Learners can:
- identify common coverage types and their narrative functions;
- compare takes for performance, clarity, continuity and technical usability;
- select a shot based on dramatic purpose rather than visual novelty;
- build a selects reel and coverage map;
- explain when a reaction, insert, master or close-up strengthens the scene.

## Key definitions and terminology
| Term | Definition | Context/example |
|---|---|---|
| Master shot | A shot covering the whole scene or a substantial portion | Wide two-person scene |
| Two-shot | A composition showing two subjects together | Establishes relationship |
| Single | A shot isolating one subject | Character A's response |
| Over-the-shoulder (OTS) | Shot framed past one subject toward another | Dialogue coverage |
| Close-up | Tight framing emphasizing a face or detail | Recognition or emotional change |
| Insert | Close detail of a specific object/action | Hand turns a key |
| Cutaway | Shot away from the main action | Clock, room detail, listener |
| Reaction shot | Shot showing response to an event or line | Listener absorbs news |
| Selects reel | Sequence of chosen candidate ranges | Best takes assembled for review |
| Performance continuity | Consistency of gesture, expression, timing and emotional progression | Hand position across angles |

## Purpose and professional relevance
Coverage gives the editor choices, but too many choices can slow the edit if they are not organized. Professional selection balances story information, performance truth, continuity, image/sound quality and shot variety. A technically imperfect take may contain the only honest emotional moment; a technically pristine take may not serve the scene.

## Theory and principles
Evaluate each take across five dimensions:
1. **Dramatic value:** does it communicate the beat and character objective?
2. **Performance:** is the behavior believable, specific and consistent with the scene?
3. **Continuity:** can it connect to adjacent shots without an accidental jump?
4. **Technical quality:** focus, exposure, movement, sound and artifacts.
5. **Editorial function:** does it establish, reveal, emphasize, connect or provide a useful reaction?

Do not choose shots only because they are close-ups or visually impressive. A master may preserve timing and interaction that isolated singles cannot. A reaction shot can change the audience's interpretation, so it must be temporally and emotionally truthful.

Use a simple weighted score only as a decision aid, not as a substitute for judgment. Example weights: dramatic value 30%, performance 25%, continuity 20%, technical quality 15%, editorial function 10%. Record exceptions where a lower technical score is accepted for a uniquely valuable performance.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives
- **Required:** authorized source footage, clip/take IDs, selects log.
- **Optional:** NLE bins, markers, source metadata, waveform and scopes.
- **Alternatives:** contact sheet, spreadsheet, paper shot cards.
- **Accessibility:** provide text descriptions for visual selects and label takes consistently.

## Procedure
1. Inventory all source clips and group by scene, camera, shot size and take.
2. Watch each take in full before marking a short range.
3. Record clip ID, take, in/out timecode, shot type and notable performance moment.
4. Score the five selection dimensions or write a short qualitative note.
5. Flag technical defects and continuity differences; do not silently discard unique story information.
6. Build a selects reel grouped by dramatic beat, not only by camera or shot size.
7. Compare the master against the proposed close-up/reverse-shot construction.
8. Place the best performance moment first, then test whether coverage can support the cut.
9. Add reactions only where they reveal a real response or improve comprehension.
10. Watch the sequence for performance continuity: gesture, breath, eye movement, object handling and emotional progression.
11. Save a versioned selects log and timeline.

## Worked example
**Brief/input:** A character tells a friend they are leaving town. Three takes are available:
- Take A: clean sound and stable framing, but the delivery is emotionally flat.
- Take B: slight focus softness at the end, but contains a restrained pause and a convincing reaction.
- Take C: energetic delivery, but the hand position changes between the line and reverse angle.

**Decision process:** Take B may be the strongest dramatic choice if the focus issue is not distracting and the pause is essential. Use Take A as an alternate or for a line section. Avoid cutting to Take C's mismatched hand position unless the action can be connected honestly. Compare the master for natural timing before constructing the scene from singles.

**Expected output:** A selects reel with at least two viable options for key beats and a written rationale for any technical compromise.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough
| Decision | Candidate 1 | Candidate 2 | Editorial test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main line | Clear but flat | Slightly soft, truthful | Which conveys the character's change? |
| Reaction | Looking down | Looks up after pause | Which response belongs to the actual moment? |
| Object insert | Hand steady | Hand position differs | Does the insert preserve action state? |
| Scene rhythm | Master uninterrupted | Singles assembled | Which preserves timing and geography? |

A scoring sheet may use 1–5 per dimension. Multiply each score by its weight and compare totals. If a candidate wins numerically but fails a critical continuity or rights requirement, it is not an acceptable selection.

## Guided practice
Select a 30–60 second dialogue scene with at least two takes per angle. Build a selects reel and score each candidate. Then make two edits: one favoring visual variety and one favoring performance continuity. Ask a viewer which version communicates the relationship more clearly and why.

**Success criteria:** selections are traceable, reasons are specific, and the chosen edit preserves performance and geography.

## Independent assignment
Prepare a coverage and performance report for a 1–2 minute scene. Submit:
- source inventory;
- shot/take selects table with timecodes;
- weighted or qualitative comparison;
- selects reel;
- assembled scene;
- 200-word rationale discussing at least one rejected shot and one accepted technical compromise.

## Common errors and recovery
| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scene feels visually repetitive | Selects chosen only by performance or shot size | Review shot function across beats | Add purposeful master/insert/reaction coverage |
| Performance jumps between cuts | Gestures or emotional state differ | Compare adjacent takes frame-by-frame | Choose matching action or restructure |
| Reaction feels artificial | Reaction is out of context | Verify source chronology and eyeline | Use authentic reaction or omit it |
| Only one take is considered | Incomplete source review | Check inventory against recorded media | Review all takes before locking selects |
| Sharpest take feels emotionally weak | Technical quality dominates decision | Evaluate dramatic value separately | Choose truthful performance if defect is acceptable |

## Knowledge check
1. What is the difference between a master shot and an insert?
2. Why is a reaction shot a powerful editorial choice?
3. Name five dimensions for evaluating a take.
4. Why should a weighted score not make the final decision automatically?

## Answer key / instructor guidance
1. A master covers the scene broadly; an insert isolates a detail or action.
2. It controls how the audience interprets an event and reveals response.
3. Dramatic value, performance, continuity, technical quality and editorial function.
4. Context, critical continuity, rights and unique performance value may outweigh a numeric score.
