- Tools/environment: Source-footage inventory, top-down scene diagram, shot/continuity log, paper edit or any NLE; no application-specific UI steps are assumed

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

1. Construct a top-down geography diagram that identifies at least 90% of the relevant people, props, entrances, exits, landmarks and camera positions in a supplied scene.
2. Identify the primary axis of action and classify each camera setup as side A, side B, neutral/on-axis or moving across the axis, with a written reason.
3. Complete a shot-by-shot continuity matrix recording screen position, facing direction, eyeline target, movement vector and action/prop state.
4. Diagnose at least five spatial or directional mismatches and distinguish a true continuity error from a motivated change in geography.
5. Build a continuity-safe edit and a second intentionally crossing version, each with a documented transition strategy.
6. Explain how an establishing shot, neutral shot, continuous camera move, subject movement or re-establishing shot changes the viewer's spatial model.
7. Conduct an unprompted viewer-comprehension test and revise the edit based on observed misunderstanding rather than preference alone.

## Prerequisites and readiness check

Before beginning, learners should be able to:

- identify a wide/master, medium, close-up and insert;
- describe what happens immediately before and after a selected action;
- distinguish what is visible in the frame from what is inferred off-screen;
- label screen-left and screen-right from the viewer's perspective;
- preserve source IDs and avoid overwriting original media.

**Readiness check:** Given a still frame, mark the visible subject's screen position and facing direction. Then describe one plausible off-screen eyeline target without claiming it is proven by the image. If these two tasks are difficult, review Lesson 01 terminology and Chapter 2 before proceeding.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Editorial use |
|---|---|---|
| Scene geography | The viewer's mental model of people, objects, boundaries and movement in a location | Helps the audience understand where action occurs |
| Axis of action | An imaginary line defined by the main interaction or direction of movement | Provides a reference for camera-side and screen-direction decisions |
| 180-degree guideline | A continuity convention in which related camera positions generally remain on one side of the axis | Preserves a stable left/right relationship |
| Camera side | The side of the axis from which a camera records the action | Helps classify coverage before editing |
| Screen position | The apparent location of a subject in the frame: left, center or right | Tracks spatial relationships across shots |
| Eyeline | The direction a subject's eyes or head are directed toward | Connects a person to an off-screen partner or object |
| Eyeline match | A shot relationship in which a look is followed by a plausible view of its target, or reverse looks remain spatially compatible | Supports cause-and-effect and spatial comprehension |
| Screen direction | The apparent direction of travel or attention across the image | Helps preserve movement continuity |
| Neutral / on-axis shot | A view aligned with or close to the axis, often reducing the left/right bias of side angles | Can help reveal or transition between spatial sides |
| Establishing shot | A view that introduces the location and the relevant spatial relationships | Gives the audience an initial map |
| Re-establishing shot | A later view that updates or restores the spatial map after movement or a change | Clarifies changed positions or a new area |
| Motivated crossing | A camera or subject movement that makes a change of axis understandable within the scene | Allows a deliberate shift in screen relationships |
| Mirroring | Reversing an image horizontally or vertically in post | Can disguise a mismatch but may reverse text, marks, handedness or real geography |
| Action state | The exact stage of a movement or object interaction | Prevents jumps in hand, prop, door or body position |
| Spatial continuity | Consistency or intentional legibility of position, direction, orientation and distance | The larger system that an edit should preserve or deliberately reshape |

**Important distinction:** The 180-degree guideline is a convention, not a physical law. A technically compliant edit can still be confusing if it withholds essential geography. A line crossing can be clear when the audience sees the change. Judge the viewer's understanding, not rule compliance alone.

## Purpose and professional relevance

Spatial continuity is the craft of helping viewers understand where people are, where they are looking, how they move and how one shot relates to the next. It matters in dialogue, action, comedy, sport, dance, documentary observation, interviews and product demonstrations. In each case, the audience forms a spatial model from incomplete views.

A cut can preserve screen direction but still be misleading if the source action did not happen in that order. Conversely, an intentional reversal may be dramatically useful if it signals a turn, a new viewpoint, confusion or a power shift. The professional task is to make the spatial design intentional and supported by the source.

Editors use geography analysis to:

- select compatible takes and camera angles;
- plan shot order before detailed trimming;
- detect mismatched eyelines and movement direction;
- maintain the relationship between hands, props, doors and other landmarks;
- identify missing coverage before attempting a repair;
- explain why a shot is omitted, repositioned or used as a transition;
- communicate continuity risks to directors, assistant editors and reviewers.

## Theory and principles

### 1. The audience builds a spatial model

A viewer does not see the whole set. The viewer infers it from each frame, camera movement, sound cue, eyeline and action. The edit should provide enough stable information for the intended scene.

Think of spatial information in five layers:

1. **Global map:** room shape, entrances, exits, windows, doors and major landmarks.
2. **Relative position:** who is near, far, left, right, in front or behind.
3. **Orientation:** where bodies and faces are directed.
4. **Vector:** where a person, vehicle or object travels.
5. **Interaction state:** what hand holds an object, whether a door is open, and what stage of an action has occurred.

A cut can preserve four layers while breaking the fifth. For example, a character may remain screen-left and look screen-right, but the envelope may jump from the left hand to the right hand. A geography review must therefore include both spatial and action-state checks.

### 2. Define the axis from the action, not from the frame

The axis is established by the primary interaction or movement, not simply by the edge of the camera frame. For a conversation, draw a line between the principal participants. For a moving subject, use the dominant path of travel. For a group, identify the primary relationship that the scene asks the viewer to follow; secondary axes may arise as the action changes.

A scene can contain more than one useful axis. Record when the primary action changes and explain whether the edit establishes a new axis or intentionally abandons the previous one.

### 3. Camera-side logic and screen relationships

For a simple two-person exchange:

- A and B occupy opposite sides of the interaction line.
- Cameras on one side of that line tend to preserve a stable left/right arrangement.
- If A is framed screen-left and looks screen-right toward B, B's compatible reverse generally looks screen-left.
- A camera placed across the line may reverse the apparent arrangement.

These are expected relationships, not universal framing laws. Lens choice, camera movement, actor blocking, a curved path, an intentionally frontal shot, reflections and complex staging can change the result. Always inspect the recorded scene diagram and actual frames.

### 4. Eyelines are vectors, not just left/right labels

An eyeline includes horizontal direction, vertical height, distance and the implied target. Two actors may both look screen-left yet appear not to look at one another if one looks too high, too low or too close to camera.

Record:
- target identity or a cautious label such as “off-screen target 1”;
- approximate horizontal direction;
- approximate vertical angle;
- whether the look is eye-only, head-led or full-body;
- whether the target's shot supports the look.

Do not invent an off-screen object or person. If the target is not shown or established, describe the inference as uncertain.

### 5. Screen direction and movement

A subject travelling left-to-right in one shot will usually be expected to continue in a compatible direction in the next shot when the route is continuous. A reversal can indicate a turn, a return journey, a change in viewpoint or a new axis. The edit must supply enough evidence for the intended interpretation.

Trace movement as a sequence: **start position → path → turn/interaction → end position**. A single arrow on a diagram is insufficient when the subject changes direction or passes behind an obstacle.

### 6. Establishing, neutral and re-establishing strategies

- **Establishing view:** introduces the initial map before detailed coverage.
- **Neutral/on-axis view:** reduces the directional bias of a side angle and may prepare a crossing.
- **Continuous camera move:** lets the viewer experience the camera crossing the line rather than discovering a reversed setup after a cut.
- **Motivated subject movement:** a person walks around, changes seats or otherwise changes the relationship that defines the axis.
- **Re-establishing view:** shows the changed geography after movement or a new beat.

No device automatically repairs a scene. A neutral shot can be visually awkward; a wide can interrupt emotional timing; a continuous move may not exist in the footage. Choose the least misleading solution that serves the dramatic purpose.

### 7. Spatial clarity versus expressive disorientation

Disorientation can be an intentional effect. If it is the goal, document what the audience should feel and what information remains understandable. For example, the viewer may be uncertain which corridor a character entered but should still understand that the character is alone and being followed. Intentional ambiguity should be bounded, not accidental.

### 8. Mirroring is not a default repair

Horizontal mirroring can make screen directions appear compatible, but it can also reverse readable signs, logos, uniforms, handedness, watch placement, vehicle controls, screen graphics, scars, costume details and real-world orientation. It may also violate documentary integrity. Consider selecting another take, changing shot order, using a transition, or acknowledging an intentional crossing first. If mirroring is considered, document authorization, inspect the whole frame and verify that no factual or visual detail is falsely reversed.

## Reproducible procedure

### Phase A — Inspect and map the scene

1. **Confirm authorization and source identity.** Record project ID, media IDs, permitted use, consent status and any restrictions. Preserve camera originals.
2. **Watch the complete scene once.** Do not begin by selecting attractive close-ups. Identify the principal interaction, movement path and dramatic changes.
3. **Choose the primary axis.** Draw it through the principal interaction or along the dominant movement path. Write one sentence explaining why this is the useful axis.
4. **Create the top-down map.** Mark fixed landmarks, doors, windows, table edges, entrances, exits, character start/end positions and important props.
5. **Plot every camera.** Use stable camera IDs and mark which side of the axis each camera occupies. Mark on-axis/neutral positions separately.
6. **Record movement and blocking.** Add arrows for each subject's route, turns, pauses and object exchanges. Use separate arrows for different subjects.
7. **Create a shot inventory.** Give every usable shot/take a unique ID. Record source range, shot scale, camera position and any known mirrored/rotated state.

### Phase B — Analyze each shot

8. For each shot, record each relevant subject's screen position: left, center, right, off-screen or changing.
9. Record body orientation and gaze separately. A body may face camera while eyes look off-screen.
10. Record the eyeline target and horizontal/vertical direction. Use “unknown” when the target is not established.
11. Record movement direction and path. Note any turn or change of axis.
12. Record action state: hand, prop, door, seat, bag, vehicle or other continuity-critical detail.
13. Compare the shot against the top-down map. If the footage contradicts the diagram, revise the diagram rather than forcing the footage to fit an assumption.

### Phase C — Build and test the edit

14. Arrange a continuity-safe sequence using compatible camera sides and action states.
15. Check each adjacent pair in order. Do not inspect only isolated shots; continuity is relational.
16. Flag all crossings and direction reversals. For each, state whether it is accidental, motivated, neutralized or intentionally disorienting.
17. Build a second version that includes one deliberate crossing. Use only a transition supported by the available footage: continuous camera movement, subject movement, neutral view or re-establishing shot.
18. Watch each version uninterrupted, once with sound and once without sound where appropriate. The silent pass can expose visual geography problems but does not replace the full sound review.
19. Ask a viewer who has not seen the diagram to describe who is where, who looks at whom and how the subject moved. Do not coach the viewer.
20. Record the viewer's exact misunderstanding, if any. Revise only after identifying the shot relationship that caused it.
21. Save both versions, the map, continuity matrix, source references and revision note. Keep original media and earlier edit versions unchanged.

## Worked example

**Brief/input:** A courier enters a small office, approaches a desk, places a sealed envelope on the desk and steps back. The recipient sits behind the desk. A window is behind the recipient; the door is behind the courier.

**Fictional source inventory:**

| Shot ID | Coverage | Camera side | Recorded action |
|---|---|---|---|
| S01 | Office master | Side A | Courier enters; recipient seated |
| S02 | Courier medium | Side A | Courier approaches desk |
| S03 | Recipient single | Side A | Recipient watches courier |
| S04 | Envelope insert | Side A | Courier places envelope |
| S05 | Courier alternate | Side B | Same approach, alternate setup |
| S06 | Neutral wide | On/near axis | Both people and desk visible |
| S07 | Recipient close-up | Side A | Recipient looks down at envelope |

**Decision process:**

1. Draw the line from courier to recipient across the desk.
2. Establish the master: courier begins near the door and recipient remains near the window.
3. Use S02 for the approach and S03 for the recipient's reaction; check that their eyelines relate to the same desk interaction.
4. Use S04 only if the hand and envelope positions match the action stage in S02.
5. Exclude S05 from the safe version because it is recorded across the axis and reverses the apparent relationship without showing a transition.
6. Use S06 if the story needs a visible shift to the other side, or retain it as a re-establishing option.
7. Use S07 after the envelope is placed, not before, unless the actual performance establishes a different chronology.

**Expected output:** A 20–30 second edit in which an uninformed viewer can identify the door, desk, courier, recipient and envelope; understand the approach and placement; and correctly identify the recipient's look target. The exact runtime is a practice target, not a universal rule.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

The following is simulated teaching data, not actual footage.

### A. Top-down map

```text
                    WINDOW
          +-----------------------+
          |       RECIPIENT (B)   |
          |          [DESK]       |
          |                       |
          |                       |
          | DOOR                  |
          | COURIER (A)           |
          +-----------------------+

Primary axis: Courier A <---- desk ----> Recipient B
Camera side A: S01, S02, S03, S04, S07
Opposite side: S05
Neutral/on-axis: S06
```

### B. Continuity matrix

| Shot | A position/facing | B position/facing | Eyeline/action state | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S01 | Enters from frame-left; travels right | Seated frame-right | Both visible; establishes desk | Use first |
| S02 | Frame-left/center; faces right | Off-screen | A looks toward B | Use after S01 |
| S03 | Off-screen | Frame-right; looks left/down | B looks toward A or desk | Use after S02 if target matches |
| S04 | Hand enters from left | Not visible | Envelope moves onto desk | Match hand and envelope state |
| S05 | Reversed setup | Reversed setup | Opposite camera side | Hold for deliberate-crossing version |
| S06 | Both visible | Both visible | Spatial reset | Possible bridge/re-establish |
| S07 | Not visible | Close-up | Looks down at envelope | Use after placement |

### C. Safe edit dry run

1. S01 establishes the door, desk, window and both people.
2. S02 continues A's movement toward screen-right.
3. S03 shows B looking toward the courier/desk. Inspect the actual gaze before choosing the in-point.
4. S04 completes the envelope placement. Compare hand position and prop orientation at the cut.
5. S07 shows B's response after the envelope is present.
6. Return to S01 only if the wider view adds a meaningful reaction or confirms the next action.

**Dry-run stop condition:** If S03's gaze is directed toward a different target, do not relabel it as a match. Find compatible coverage, use the master, or document the missing coverage.

### D. Deliberate-crossing dry run

1. Begin with S01 → S02 → S03 → S04.
2. Insert S06 while both characters and the desk are visible.
3. If S06 genuinely shows the camera/subject relationship changing, continue with S05.
4. If S06 is merely a static neutral angle with no clear transition, test it with viewers. It may not sufficiently communicate the new axis.
5. Compare the viewer's description with the intended geography and document any ambiguity.

**Interpretation:** The crossing is not automatically successful because a neutral shot exists. It succeeds only if the new relationship becomes understandable in the actual sequence.

## Guided practice

### Exercise 1 — Two-person dialogue map (25 minutes)

Draw a room with two speakers, one table, one door and one window. Plot three cameras on one side and one camera on the other. Label the expected screen position and eyeline for each speaker.

**Hint:** Draw the character-to-character axis before plotting cameras.

**Success criteria:** Every camera has a side label; each speaker's look direction has a plausible target; the diagram is readable without relying on color.

### Exercise 2 — Eyeline diagnosis (20 minutes)

Use six instructor-provided stills or a fictional shot set. For every still, identify the subject, screen position, body orientation, gaze direction and possible target. Mark uncertain targets as unknown.

**Success criteria:** No inferred target is described as a fact without supporting evidence.

### Exercise 3 — Movement path (30 minutes)

Map a person entering a room, turning around a chair and exiting through a second door. Divide the path into start, travel, turn, interaction and exit. Identify where a new axis may be needed.

**Success criteria:** The map distinguishes a real turn from an apparent reversal caused by a camera-side change.

### Exercise 4 — Two-version edit (30–45 minutes)

Build a continuity-safe paper edit, then a second version with a deliberate crossing. State which transition method is used and why it is supported by the available coverage.

**Success criteria:** Both versions have shot IDs, cut order, transition rationale and a viewer-test prompt.

## Common errors and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speakers appear to swap sides | Unmotivated axis crossing | Compare camera side and establishing view | Reorder compatible coverage or establish the new axis |
| Both speakers look in the same direction | Eyeline mismatch or different target | Compare actual gaze, camera position and target height | Select compatible take or use a wider view |
| Subject reverses travel direction unexpectedly | Camera crossed axis or subject turned | Trace the route and inspect the action | Show the turn, use compatible coverage or re-establish |
| Prop jumps sides of the desk | Different action stage or setup | Compare hand, prop and table landmarks | Select a matching action state; do not conceal a factual mismatch |
| Door changes from open to closed | Coverage recorded at different times | Inspect source chronology and door state | Use a consistent take or restructure with a truthful time transition |
| Geography feels unclear despite correct camera side | Insufficient establishing information | Ask a viewer to draw the room after one viewing | Add or reposition a useful establishing/re-establishing view |
| A neutral shot does not repair the cut | It does not show the change clearly enough | Test the shot in sequence, not alone | Use a continuous move, motivated blocking or a more informative wide |
| Mirrored shot contains reversed text or details | Image transform used without full-frame review | Inspect signs, logos, handedness and landmarks | Restore original orientation and choose another repair |
| Eyeline is horizontally plausible but feels wrong | Vertical angle or distance differs | Compare target height and actor marks | Select a closer match or retain a wider shot |
| Diagram conflicts with the footage | Initial map was based on assumption | Re-watch the entire source and verify landmarks | Correct the diagram and update the continuity log |
| A cut is spatially clear but changes event meaning | Source chronology or action state was rearranged | Compare source order and performance | Restore truthful order or disclose the constructed chronology |
| Viewer test is leading | Reviewer explained the geography first | Repeat with a new viewer and neutral prompt | Ask only what happened and where people were |

## Knowledge check

1. Define scene geography and name five elements that may contribute to it.
2. How is the axis of action determined in a dialogue scene?
3. What does the 180-degree guideline preserve, and why is it not an absolute law?
4. Distinguish screen position from body orientation and eyeline.
5. Why can two actors looking in opposite horizontal directions still fail an eyeline match?
6. Name four ways to make an axis crossing legible.
7. What information belongs in a continuity matrix beyond camera side?
8. Why should an editor inspect the actual action before diagnosing a screen-direction reversal?
9. Give two risks of horizontal mirroring.
10. What is the difference between an establishing shot and a re-establishing shot?
11. Describe a viewer-comprehension test that avoids coaching.
12. When is spatial disorientation an intentional design rather than an error?

## Answer key / instructor guidance

1. The viewer's mental spatial model; people, props, entrances/exits, landmarks, positions, orientations, movement paths and sightlines are valid examples.
2. Draw a line through the primary interaction between the speakers, then plot the cameras relative to that line.
3. It generally preserves stable left/right relationships across related shots. It is a convention that can be crossed intentionally when the change is motivated and understandable.
4. Screen position is where the subject appears in frame; body orientation is where the body faces; eyeline is where the eyes/head direct attention.
5. Their vertical angle, target distance or actual off-screen target may not correspond; opposite left/right labels alone do not prove a match.
6. Continuous camera move, neutral/on-axis shot, motivated subject movement, re-establishing shot or a clear new establishing view.
7. Screen position, facing direction, eyeline target/angle, movement vector, action/prop state, source range and any transform/mirroring note.
8. The subject may have turned, the camera may have moved, or the source may be mirrored or from a different setup. A repair based on a false diagnosis can misrepresent action.
9. It can reverse readable text/logos, handedness, costume details, landmarks, controls or documentary facts; it can also create new continuity errors.
10. An establishing shot introduces the initial map; a re-establishing shot restores or updates it after movement or changed geography.
11. Show the sequence without the diagram or explanation, then ask a neutral question such as “Describe where each person was and how they moved.” Record the unprompted answer.
12. When the intended emotional or narrative effect is confusion/disorientation and the edit still communicates the essential information the story requires. Document the intention and test it.

**Pass threshold:** 70/100. Mandatory gate: no fabricated source evidence, unauthorized media use or undisclosed misleading reversal. A paper-only simulation can demonstrate reasoning but must not be represented as an executed edit.

### Remediation

- If diagram/camera analysis is below 60% of its available points, redraw the scene map from the full source before editing.
- If the continuity matrix is incomplete, repeat the shot-by-shot analysis with at least five adjacent-shot comparisons.
- If the crossing is unclear, test a different supported transition and repeat the viewer test.
- If rights or source identity is uncertain, stop practical use until authorization is clarified.
