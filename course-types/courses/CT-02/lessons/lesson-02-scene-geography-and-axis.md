# CT-02 Lesson 02 — Scene Geography, Axis of Action and Eyelines

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Beginner
- Estimated time: 2–3 hours
- Prerequisites: CT-02 Lesson 01 recommended; ability to identify a shot and basic screen directions
- Tools/environment: Top-down scene diagram, shot log, NLE; any supported version
- Version/review date: 1.0 — 2026-11-16

## Learning outcomes
Learners can:
- map character, camera and object positions in a scene;
- identify the axis of action and evaluate camera positions relative to it;
- assess eyeline and screen-direction continuity;
- diagnose confusing cuts and select a justified repair;
- cross the axis intentionally when the scene's design requires it.

## Key definitions and terminology
| Term | Definition | Context/example |
|---|---|---|
| Scene geography | Spatial relationship among people, objects and locations | Door is behind Character A |
| Axis of action | Imaginary line through the main interaction or movement | Line between two speakers |
| 180-degree guideline | Convention of keeping camera positions on one side of the axis to preserve screen relationships | A remains left, B remains right |
| Eyeline | Direction in which a subject looks | A looks screen-right toward B |
| Screen direction | Apparent direction of movement across the frame | Subject travels left-to-right |
| Neutral shot | View that does not strongly favor either side of the axis | Camera looking along the line |
| Re-establishing shot | Shot that clarifies spatial relationships after a change | Wide view after camera crosses axis |

## Purpose and professional relevance
Spatial continuity lets the audience understand where people are, who is looking at whom and how movement connects across cuts. It is essential in dialogue, action, sports, dance and any sequence where direction affects comprehension. The 180-degree guideline is a practical convention, not a law; the editor's task is to manage the viewer's spatial model intentionally.

## Theory and principles
Imagine a line connecting two characters in conversation. Camera positions on one side normally preserve a consistent left-right relationship. If Character A appears screen-left looking right, the reverse angle of Character B generally looks left. Crossing the line without a visible transition can reverse this relationship.

Continuity has several layers:
- **Position:** where each person/object is located.
- **Orientation:** which direction the subject faces.
- **Movement:** where the subject travels and whether direction is consistent.
- **Eyeline:** where the subject looks and what the look motivates.
- **Action state:** the stage of a movement or object's condition.

A neutral shot, a continuous camera move across the line, a motivated character movement, or a new wide shot can make a crossing understandable. Do not use a neutral shot as a mechanical patch if the story needs a more direct spatial explanation.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives
- **Required:** a top-down diagram or equivalent, source footage and a shot log.
- **Optional:** NLE timeline with clip markers and frame stepping.
- **Alternatives:** paper sketch, whiteboard, spreadsheet, storyboard frames.
- **Accessibility:** label positions with words/arrows as well as color.

## Procedure
1. Identify the primary interaction or direction of travel.
2. Draw the axis of action and label the two sides.
3. Mark character positions, important objects, entrances and exits.
4. Mark camera positions and assign each shot an ID.
5. For each shot, record screen position, facing direction, eyeline and movement direction.
6. Arrange candidate shots in intended edit order.
7. Compare adjacent shots for position, orientation, eyeline, screen direction and action state.
8. Flag every axis crossing. Decide whether it is motivated and legible.
9. Build a continuity-safe version using compatible coverage.
10. Build a second version with a deliberate crossing, using a continuous move or re-establishing shot.
11. Watch both versions without pausing and ask a viewer to describe the geography.
12. Record the chosen solution and any intentional discontinuity in the edit notes.

## Worked example
**Brief/input:** Two characters sit across a table. A is nearest the window; B is nearest the door. A speaks while looking toward screen-right.

**Decision process:** Draw the line between A and B. Place the master and both singles on the same side. In the reverse shot, B should look in the opposite screen direction. If a second camera angle was recorded across the line, do not insert it casually. Either omit it, use a neutral view, or show a camera/character movement that clearly changes the spatial relationship.

**Expected output:** A 20–30 second dialogue edit in which a first-time viewer can correctly identify which character is where and who is looking at whom.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough
| Shot | Camera side | Subject position | Eyeline | Expected continuity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Master | Side A | A left, B right | Mutual | Establish relationship |
| A single | Side A | A left | Looks right | Matches B's position |
| B single | Side A | B right | Looks left | Matches A's position |
| Alternate angle | Side B | Reversed | Reversed | Use only with motivated crossing/re-establishment |

If the B single shows B looking right, inspect the actual camera position and performance before changing the edit. The mismatch may come from a camera crossing, an actor's eyeline, a mirrored image, or a different scene setup.

## Guided practice
Sketch a two-person conversation from above. Place three cameras on one side and one on the opposite side. Create a shot table with screen position and eyeline. Edit a short exchange using only the first three cameras, then add the fourth shot using a motivated transition.

**Success criteria:** the diagram matches the footage, the safe edit preserves screen relationships, and the deliberate crossing is understandable without explanation.

## Independent assignment
Create a 30–60 second sequence involving two people crossing a room and exchanging an object. Submit:
- top-down geography diagram;
- camera/shot map;
- continuity table;
- continuity-safe edit;
- deliberate axis-crossing edit;
- short comparison explaining which version best serves the scene.

## Common errors and recovery
| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speakers appear to swap places | Unmotivated axis crossing | Compare shot camera sides | Reorder or establish the new axis |
| Both characters look in same direction | Eyeline mismatch or incorrect reverse | Inspect camera and performer marks | Select a compatible take |
| Travel direction reverses | Directional continuity not checked | Trace movement across shot map | Use a matching angle or show the turn |
| Object jumps sides of table | Position/action state mismatch | Compare frame-by-frame object location | Choose matching coverage or restructure transparently |
| Geography feels unclear despite correct axis | No establishing information | Watch without sound and ask viewer to map space | Add/reposition a useful master or re-establishing shot |

## Knowledge check
1. What spatial problem does the 180-degree guideline help prevent?
2. Name four continuity layers to compare across a cut.
3. Give three methods for making an axis crossing legible.
4. Why should an editor inspect footage before assuming a screen-direction error?

## Answer key / instructor guidance
1. Confusing or unintentionally reversing screen relationships.
2. Position, orientation, movement, eyeline and action state (any four).
3. Continuous camera move, neutral shot, motivated subject movement, or re-establishing shot.
4. The apparent mismatch may be explained by real movement, camera placement, mirrored footage or a different setup.
