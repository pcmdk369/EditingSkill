# CT-02 Lesson 01 — Dramatic Beats and the Paper Edit

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Foundation → Beginner
- Estimated time: 3–4 hours
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of a scene, shot and timeline; no specific NLE required
- Tools/environment: Notebook or spreadsheet; any NLE for the optional assembly; original or licensed footage
- Version/review date: 2.0 — 2026-11-19

## Learning outcomes
By the end, learners can:
- identify a scene objective, conflict, turning point and outcome;
- divide a scene into observable dramatic beats;
- distinguish story information from shot coverage;
- create a paper edit that maps beats to available shots;
- justify editorial choices without inventing events or misleading chronology.

## Key definitions and terminology
| Term | Definition | Context/example |
|---|---|---|
| Scene objective | What a character wants during a scene | A visitor wants the envelope |
| Conflict | The obstacle or opposing force | The recipient refuses to hand it over |
| Dramatic beat | A meaningful change in action, information or emotional direction | A character changes from confidence to doubt |
| Turning point | A beat that changes the direction or stakes of the scene | The envelope is revealed to be empty |
| Coverage | The recorded shots and takes available to construct the scene | Master, singles, inserts and reactions |
| Paper edit | A written shot-and-beat plan made before timeline assembly | Beat 1 uses master; beat 2 uses hand insert |
| Selects | Chosen candidate clips or ranges | Take 3, 00:12–00:18 |

## Purpose and professional relevance
Editors do not simply arrange attractive shots. They shape what the audience knows, when it is known and how a character's choices become understandable. Beat analysis prevents an edit from becoming a collection of disconnected images. A paper edit also reduces random timeline experimentation and gives collaborators a shared plan.

## Theory and principles
A useful scene can be analyzed at three connected levels:
1. **Objective:** what the character is trying to achieve.
2. **Change:** what new action, obstacle or information alters the situation.
3. **Consequence:** what the character does or understands because of that change.

A beat is not automatically equal to a line of dialogue or a camera angle. A single line can contain multiple shifts; several shots can express one beat. The editor identifies the change first, then chooses the shot that communicates it most clearly.

Use the following questions:
- What does the audience know at the start?
- What does the character want?
- What blocks that want?
- What changes the balance of the scene?
- What should the audience know at the end?
- Which performance detail communicates the change most truthfully?

Do not create a false cause-and-effect relationship by moving a reaction or action out of its actual context. In documentary and factual work, preserve chronology and disclose material restructuring where required.


### A repeatable test for identifying a beat

A beat should be describable as a **change from one state to another**. Use this four-part test:

1. **Before:** What does the character, audience or situation believe or want immediately before the moment?
2. **Trigger:** What action, information, obstacle or response occurs?
3. **After:** What is now different?
4. **Evidence:** Which observable source moment proves that change?

A useful beat label contains a verb and a consequence: “recognizes the name and withdraws the offer.” A weak label such as “close-up” describes coverage, not drama. If the before/after states cannot be stated, the editor may be looking at an action that belongs inside a larger beat rather than a separate beat.

A beat can be very brief or can unfold over several shots. Its duration is determined by the time needed for the audience to perceive the change, not by a fixed number of seconds.

### Objective, obstacle, tactic and consequence

Analyze the scene at two connected scales.

| Element | Diagnostic question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | What does the character want now? | The courier wants the recipient to accept the envelope |
| Obstacle | What prevents the objective? | The recipient refuses to touch it |
| Tactic | What does the character try next? | The courier offers an explanation |
| Change | What new fact or response shifts the situation? | The courier sees the recipient's name on the envelope |
| Consequence | What choice follows? | The courier takes the envelope back |

A scene may contain more than one objective or tactic. Record the change when a tactic fails, a new fact appears, power shifts or the character makes a consequential decision. Do not force every scene into a simplistic conflict pattern: observational, atmospheric and non-fiction scenes may develop through discovery, accumulation, comparison or a change in understanding.

### Beat, action, shot and line are different units

- An **action** is something that happens physically or verbally.
- A **line** is a spoken unit; one line can contain a question, a realization and a reversal.
- A **shot** is a recorded camera view; one beat may need several shots, and one shot may contain several beats.
- A **beat** is the meaningful change the audience should perceive.

The editor first identifies the change, then chooses the minimum sufficient coverage to communicate it. Cutting on every action can fragment a thought. Holding a shot through several changes can conceal an important reaction. The goal is not the greatest number of cuts; it is an intelligible progression.

### Point of view and the audience-knowledge ledger

For each major beat, distinguish what the character knows from what the audience knows. A shot may reveal information early, withhold it, or move the audience closer to a character's perspective. Record whether a proposed cut:
- reveals a fact to both character and audience;
- lets the audience know more than the character;
- keeps the audience aligned with a character who is uncertain;
- shifts attention to another character's response; or
- creates a question that the scene later answers.

A close-up, insert or reaction shot is not neutral. Its placement can imply recognition, knowledge or causation. Verify that the source footage supports the implication. If the audience test reveals a different interpretation from the intended one, revise the information order or explain the intended ambiguity; do not simply add more cuts.

### Performance integrity and editorial ethics

Performance continuity is not only matching body position. Compare intention, listening, emotional intensity, breath, gaze and the timing of a response. A technically clean reaction may be dramatically false if it was recorded before the relevant information or belongs to a different exchange.

In fictional work, alternate takes can be combined when the result remains a truthful construction of the performed scene and project rules permit it. In documentary, journalism, education and factual communication, the editor must not use a reaction, insert or reordered statement to imply a response, motive, event or causal relationship that the source does not support. Preserve original source IDs and ranges so a reviewer can audit consequential choices.

### When a beat boundary is uncertain

Do not manufacture certainty. Mark the moment as provisional and compare at least two plausible interpretations. Ask a viewer to describe what changed without giving them the intended answer. If the viewer cannot identify a change, the moment may be a transition, a pause inside a beat or an unresolved ambiguity. Record that decision in the paper edit.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives
- **Required:** footage you created or are authorized to edit; shot log; paper-edit table.
- **Optional:** NLE with source viewer, markers, bins and timeline.
- **Alternatives:** spreadsheet, paper cards, text transcript with timecodes.
- **Accessibility:** describe visual beats in text; do not rely on color alone to distinguish categories.


### Source-log fields and project discipline

For each selected range, record a stable asset ID, camera/angle, take, source in/out, frame rate or timebase when known, selected performance detail, beat function, rights/consent status and any uncertainty. A filename alone is not a stable source reference if files can be renamed. If the source frame rate or timecode origin is unknown, record “unknown” rather than guessing.

Use a naming pattern such as CT02_L01_SC01_PaperEdit_v01_YYYY-MM-DD. This is a suggested teaching convention, not a mandatory NLE naming rule. Keep the source inventory, paper edit and timeline version linked. Preserve original media and use duplicate project versions or non-destructive sequence changes.

### Accessibility and low-resource alternatives

A learner may complete the analysis with paper cards, a spreadsheet or a text transcript with source time references. A partner can read the beat list and report their understanding. Do not make color the only indicator of beat type; add labels or symbols. If the learner cannot view the source visually, use an authorized descriptive transcript or audio description and clearly state which visual judgments cannot be assessed.

## Procedure
1. Watch the complete scene once without pausing. Write a one-sentence summary.
2. Watch again and record each change in objective, information, tactic or emotional state.
3. Label each beat with a short action phrase, such as “asks for proof” or “recognizes the signature.”
4. Record the relevant source clip, take and time range for each beat.
5. Identify the strongest performance moment for each beat; do not automatically choose the widest or sharpest shot.
6. Note missing coverage, continuity risks and any moment that must remain in chronological order.
7. Build a paper edit: beat order, selected shot, intended cut point and reason.
8. Assemble a first pass using the plan. Watch it without stopping.
9. Revise only after identifying a specific problem: unclear information, missing reaction, weak pace or continuity break.
10. Save the paper edit and a versioned timeline.


### Detailed procedure and verification gates

**Gate 1 — Source and brief**
- Confirm that the footage is learner-created or authorized for the exercise.
- Record scene ID, source inventory, known chronology and any restrictions on editing or disclosure.
- Write the intended audience experience in one sentence. Separate a creative intention from facts the footage must preserve.

**Gate 2 — Whole-scene understanding**
- Watch the scene once without stopping. Record the beginning state, ending state and any unresolved question.
- On the second viewing, mark only moments where information, intention, power, tactic or emotional direction changes.
- On a third pass, test the provisional beat boundaries. Merge labels that describe the same continuing change; split a beat only when a distinct change is supported.

**Gate 3 — Evidence and coverage**
- Assign each beat a source evidence reference. Use exact asset IDs and source ranges.
- Review all plausible takes before selecting one. Compare performance, continuity, clarity, sound and rights restrictions separately.
- Mark missing coverage and uncertainty. Do not invent an insert or use an unrelated reaction to hide a gap.

**Gate 4 — Paper edit**
- Put the beat order, source shot, source range, intended cut point, audience-information effect and editorial rationale in a table.
- Mark any proposed time compression, flashback, parallel action or non-linear order.
- For factual material, flag every change that could affect chronology, causation, attribution or meaning for additional review.

**Gate 5 — Assembly and viewer test**
- Build a first pass from the paper edit, preserving handles where available and avoiding irreversible source changes.
- Watch the scene uninterrupted. Ask a viewer to state the objective, turning point and outcome without coaching.
- Compare the viewer's account with the intended experience and the source evidence. Record one specific revision at a time.

**Gate 6 — Revision and handoff**
- Revise only against a named problem and retain a prior version.
- Recheck adjacent shots, audio continuity, performance timing and the factual meaning of the sequence.
- Deliver the paper edit, source map, current sequence version, change note and unresolved-issues list.

## Worked example
**Brief/input:** A fictional 45-second scene. A courier enters a room, places a sealed envelope on a table, hesitates, then takes it back after noticing the recipient's name.

**Decision process:**
- Beat 1 — arrival: establish who enters and where.
- Beat 2 — offer: show the envelope being placed.
- Beat 3 — hesitation: hold on the courier's hand or face; the pause is meaningful.
- Beat 4 — recognition: show the name only if the source footage genuinely reveals it.
- Beat 5 — reversal: show the courier taking the envelope back.
- Beat 6 — consequence: end on the recipient's reaction or the courier's exit, depending on the scene's intended point of view.

**Expected output:** A 30–45 second edit with a readable objective, a clear reversal and a documented reason for each shot.


### Expanded beat-to-coverage map (fictional training data)

The following source IDs and ranges are simulated for instruction. They do not describe actual footage.

| Beat | Before → after | Candidate source | Range | Editorial question | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Arrival | Courier outside → courier enters | A01 master | 00:00–00:07 | Is the room geography clear? | Use enough of the master to establish entrance and table |
| 2 — Offer | Envelope held → envelope placed | A02 medium | 00:11–00:16 | Can the action be followed? | Use the placement; preserve the hand's complete movement |
| 3 — Refusal | Recipient neutral → recipient refuses | A03 recipient single | 00:21–00:26 | Is the refusal audible and visible? | Retain the actual refusal, not a reaction from another take |
| 4 — Recognition | Courier uncertain → courier notices name | A04 close-up | 00:31–00:36 | Does the source show the name or only the courier's gaze? | Use the face only; do not invent a readable insert |
| 5 — Reversal | Envelope offered → envelope withdrawn | A05 hand insert | 00:39–00:43 | Does the hand action match the preceding position? | Use only if the prop position and action are continuous |
| 6 — Consequence | Courier remains → courier exits | A01 master | 00:44–00:52 | Does the exit complete the scene's point of view? | Hold through the exit if it clarifies the consequence |

The example shows why the paper edit needs more than shot labels. Beat 4 has a critical evidence limitation: if the camera never records a readable name, the editor cannot claim that the audience sees it. The courier's reaction may still communicate recognition if the performance and surrounding context support that interpretation, but the edit must not invent visual proof.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough
| Stage | Editor's question | Record | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| First viewing | What happens? | One-sentence summary | No shot selection yet |
| Beat pass | What changes? | Beat list | Each beat changes information/action |
| Coverage pass | What footage expresses it? | Clip/take/timecode | Source range exists |
| Paper edit | Why this shot now? | Ordered shot plan | No unsupported event implied |
| Assembly | Does the scene read? | Timeline version | Watch uninterrupted |
| Revision | What exact issue is fixed? | Change note | Improvement is observable |


### Paper-edit dry run with explicit decision trace

Assume the editor has the six simulated sources in the worked example. The editor creates this first paper edit:

| Order | Source | Function | Planned transition | Verification before approval |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A01 | Establish entrance and room | Cut after courier reaches table | Viewer can identify who entered and where |
| 2 | A02 | Show offer | Cut after envelope is released | Prop position and action are continuous |
| 3 | A03 | Reveal refusal | Hold through the response | Dialogue and reaction belong to this exchange |
| 4 | A04 | Show recognition | Cut on the supported performance change | No unsupported readable insert or assumed knowledge |
| 5 | A05 | Show reversal | Cut after withdrawal completes | Hand, envelope and table positions match |
| 6 | A01 | Show consequence | End after exit or motivated hold | Ending follows the scene's intended point of view |

**Dry-run decisions:**
1. The editor checks whether A03's refusal is the actual cause of the courier's reversal. If the source chronology does not support that relationship, the sequence must not imply it.
2. The editor compares the start and end prop positions around A05. If they do not match, the editor uses a wider continuous action, an honest cutaway, or documents the continuity gap.
3. The editor watches the sequence with music muted. If the audience cannot understand the offer and withdrawal without a musical cue, the visual/action logic needs review.
4. A viewer is asked, “What changed for the courier?” If the answer is only “the courier picked up an envelope,” the recognition beat may not be legible.
5. The editor records the revision as a specific change, such as “extend the refusal response by one second,” rather than “make it more dramatic.”

**Expected evidence:** a beat sheet, source-range table, ordered paper edit, viewer-test note and version/change record. The dry run is a paper exercise; no footage was edited or rendered here.

## Guided practice
Use a 20–60 second fictional scene with at least two camera angles. Write a six-beat analysis. Ask a partner to read only the beat list and explain what they believe happens. If their understanding differs from the intended scene, revise the beat descriptions before editing.

**Success criteria:** each beat is observable, the scene objective is stated, source ranges are traceable, and the paper edit can be followed by another editor.


### Guided practice sequence (60–75 minutes)

1. **Brief and rights check — 5 minutes:** identify the scene, source owner and allowed use.
2. **Uninterrupted viewing — 5 minutes:** write the beginning state, objective and ending state without selecting shots.
3. **Beat marking — 15 minutes:** create 5–10 provisional beat labels. Each label must identify a change.
4. **Evidence mapping — 10 minutes:** attach a source ID/range and note any missing or uncertain evidence.
5. **Paper edit — 10 minutes:** choose an order and state what the audience should know after each beat.
6. **Partner comprehension test — 10 minutes:** a partner reads the beat list or watches the rough assembly and describes the scene.
7. **Revision note — 5–10 minutes:** revise one issue and explain why the change improves comprehension without changing source meaning.

**Hints:** If you have more than 12 beats in a short scene, check whether you have labeled every gesture separately. If you have fewer than three, check whether a major shift in tactic or information has been missed. These are review prompts, not fixed quotas.

**Success evidence:** a viewer can state the objective, identify the turning point and describe the consequence; every major beat has source evidence; and unresolved gaps are documented rather than concealed.

## Independent assignment
Create a paper edit for a 60–90 second scene. Submit:
- one-sentence scene summary and character objective;
- beat list with timecodes;
- coverage/selects table;
- paper edit with shot order and rationale;
- first assembly and a 150–250 word decision note.


### Submission and evaluation requirements

Submit a single organized folder containing:
- a one-page brief with audience, scene objective, intended point of view and source/rights declaration;
- a complete beat sheet with before/trigger/after/evidence fields;
- a coverage/selects log with stable source IDs and source ranges;
- a paper edit showing order, cut rationale and audience-knowledge effect;
- an assembly or a clearly labeled paper-only simulation if no NLE or authorized media is available;
- a viewer-comprehension note and one revision/change record;
- a short reflection naming at least one limitation or unresolved ambiguity.

**Constraints:** do not use unlicensed footage; do not fabricate a source timecode; do not combine reactions in a way that falsely implies a response; do not overwrite original media; and label simulated or paper-only work accurately.

**Evidence standard:** an instructor must be able to locate every cited source range and understand why each selected shot serves the beat. A polished montage without a traceable beat-to-source rationale does not satisfy this assignment.

## Common errors and recovery
| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edit feels like a trailer, not a scene | Shots chosen for visual impact only | Compare each shot with the beat objective | Rebuild around action and consequence |
| Audience misses the reversal | Turning point is too brief or absent | Ask a viewer to identify the change | Restore a clear action/reaction |
| Scene implies an event that did not occur | Reaction or insert moved misleadingly | Compare timeline with source chronology | Restore order or disclose restructuring |
| Too many cuts | Every line treated as a new beat | Mark actual changes in tactic/information | Hold shots through complete thoughts |
| No usable shot for a beat | Coverage gap | Review source inventory | Use truthful audio/visual bridge or report limitation |


| Beat list contains only shot sizes | Coverage has been confused with dramatic change | Ask what is different before and after each label | Rewrite labels as observable changes |
| Several labels describe one continuous thought | Every gesture or line has been split into a beat | Read the scene aloud and track objective/tactic changes | Merge labels until a distinct shift occurs |
| The turning point is unclear | Evidence is weak or the audience learns it too late | Ask a viewer to identify the reversal without prompting | Restore the necessary source moment or document the limitation |
| Audience knows something the character does not, unintentionally | Insert/reaction placement changes information order | Compare character knowledge and audience knowledge beat by beat | Reorder only when source and story logic support it |
| A reaction appears to answer a line it never heard | Material was selected from a different exchange | Check take, chronology and original context | Use a valid reaction or leave the gap visible |
| Paper edit cannot be reproduced | Source IDs, ranges or versions are missing | Ask another editor to locate each selection | Add stable IDs, in/out points and a version record |
| Scene is understandable only with music | Music is masking unclear visual or dialogue logic | Watch a review copy with music muted | Repair the story/action connection before restoring music |

## Knowledge check
1. Why is a dramatic beat not the same as a shot?
2. What information should a paper edit contain?
3. Give two ways an edit can misrepresent a scene.
4. Why should the first viewing be uninterrupted?
5. State the before/trigger/after/evidence test for a beat.
6. How do objective, obstacle and tactic help an editor find meaningful changes?
7. Why can a close-up or reaction shot alter the audience's knowledge?
8. When should a beat label remain provisional?
9. What is the difference between a source range and a timeline position?
10. Why must performance continuity be checked beyond body position?
11. What should an editor do when coverage for a beat is missing?
12. Name four pieces of evidence that make a paper edit auditable.

## Answer key / instructor guidance
1. A beat is a meaningful change in story/action; multiple shots may express one beat, and one shot may contain multiple beats.
2. Beat order, source shot/take/timecode, intended cut, audience-information effect and rationale.
3. Reordering events to imply false causation; using a reaction out of context; concealing material chronology; inventing a fact through an unsupported insert.
4. To understand the whole scene before becoming distracted by shot-level choices.
5. State what was true before, identify the trigger, state what changed after, and cite the source evidence.
6. They reveal what the character wants, what blocks it and what strategy changes; the beat often occurs when a tactic fails or new information changes the situation.
7. Shot placement can reveal, withhold or imply recognition and causation; it changes what the audience knows and when.
8. When the source does not establish the change, when two interpretations remain plausible, or when a viewer test does not confirm the intended reading.
9. A source range locates material in the original asset; a timeline position locates it in an edit. They are not interchangeable.
10. Intention, gaze, breath, listening, emotional intensity and response timing can change even when body positions match.
11. Record the gap, review all authorized source material, use a truthful alternative if available, or disclose the limitation; do not fabricate coverage.
12. Stable asset ID, source in/out, take/camera, beat function, cut rationale, version/change record and rights/consent status.

## Assessment rubric
| Criterion | Points | Evidence required |
|---|---:|---|
| Scene objective and dramatic analysis | 20 | Accurate beginning/end states, objective, obstacle and meaningful beat boundaries |
| Source traceability and coverage | 20 | Stable IDs, valid source ranges, take/angle details and documented gaps |
| Paper-edit logic and audience knowledge | 20 | Beat order, intended information, cut rationale and no unsupported implication |
| Assembly and viewer comprehension | 25 | Clear objective, turning point and consequence; revision responds to an observed issue |
| Documentation, rights and integrity | 15 | Organized versions, authorized sources, chronology/consent notes and honest limitations |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Pass threshold:** 70/100, with mandatory rights/source-integrity gates satisfied. A deliberate factual misrepresentation, unauthorized use of media or fabricated source reference must be corrected before a pass can be awarded. A paper-only simulation may demonstrate planning skills but cannot be represented as an executed edit.

## Quality-control checklist
- [ ] Source ownership, license and consent status are recorded.
- [ ] Scene objective, beginning state and ending state are understandable.
- [ ] Every beat describes a meaningful change, not merely a shot or gesture.
- [ ] Beat boundaries are supported by observable source evidence.
- [ ] Selected ranges use stable asset IDs and valid source in/out references.
- [ ] Performance intention, listening, gaze and response timing are reviewed.
- [ ] The audience-knowledge order is deliberate and documented.
- [ ] No unsupported chronology, causation, attribution or readable insert is introduced.
- [ ] Missing coverage and unresolved ambiguity are disclosed.
- [ ] The paper edit can be followed by another editor.
- [ ] The assembly is watched uninterrupted and tested with a viewer where possible.
- [ ] Each revision responds to a named issue and retains a prior version.
- [ ] Project files and original media remain organized and recoverable.
- [ ] Simulated, paper-only and executed work are clearly distinguished.
