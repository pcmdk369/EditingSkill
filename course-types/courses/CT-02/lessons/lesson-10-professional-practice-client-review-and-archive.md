# CT-02 Lesson 10 — Professional Practice, Client Review and Archive

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Professional Practice / Master
- Estimated time: 6–8 hours
- Prerequisites: Lessons 01–09; completed or simulated capstone edit; understanding of picture lock, finishing, rights, consent and version control
- Tools/environment: Project storage, review system, media player, file manifest, checksum utility where available, written brief and retention policy
- Software policy: Tool-independent. Record exact application, operating system, storage system and utility versions when used. Review platform and archive feature behavior is version-dependent.
- Version/review date: 2.0 — 2026-11-17

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

1. Translate a creative brief into a documented scope, acceptance criteria, decision authority, review schedule and revision process.
2. Publish review material with correct status, version identity, access permissions and time-reference conventions.
3. Convert stakeholder feedback into an actionable, prioritized and traceable disposition log.
4. Resolve ambiguous, conflicting, out-of-scope and high-impact notes through the agreed approval process.
5. Prepare a portable project package containing authorized media and required dependencies.
6. Create a delivery manifest and verify file identity and project reopen/restore evidence.
7. Apply rights, consent, privacy, confidentiality, accessibility and retention requirements to client delivery and archive.
8. Design an archive and recovery plan that identifies ownership, storage locations, access, retention, migration and restore testing.
9. Communicate limitations, risks, schedule changes and handoff responsibilities professionally.

**Evidence standard:** Submit a scope/approval matrix, review-copy record, feedback log, revision history, rights/credits checklist, portable project manifest, transfer verification, archive record and restore-test evidence. Simulations must be labeled as such.

## Prerequisites and preparation

Before beginning, learners should be able to identify:

- the approved brief, picture-lock version and final delivery specification;
- project files, original media, proxies, graphics, fonts, audio, captions and export dependencies;
- rights, releases, licenses and restrictions for source material;
- review version IDs, timecode conventions and change-control records;
- archive ownership and the difference between a backup, working copy and long-term archive.

Prepare a completed short scene or a clearly labeled simulated project package. Use learner-created or authorized assets. Do not place private client media in public repositories, personal cloud accounts or unapproved review services.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Professional significance |
|---|---|---|
| Creative brief | Document describing purpose, audience, message, deliverables and constraints | Establishes the work being commissioned |
| Scope of work | Agreed tasks, deliverables, exclusions, schedule and responsibilities | Prevents unapproved expansion |
| Acceptance criteria | Observable conditions used to decide whether a deliverable is accepted | Makes completion testable |
| Stakeholder | Person or group with an interest in or authority over the work | Not every stakeholder has final approval authority |
| Decision owner | Person authorized to resolve creative or delivery decisions | Prevents contradictory instructions |
| Review round | Bounded cycle of distributing a version, receiving notes, deciding and revising | Controls feedback and schedule |
| Revision | Authorized change to a deliverable after review | Must be distinguished from correction of a defect |
| Change request | Formal request to alter agreed scope, schedule, cost or approved content | Creates a decision record |
| Feedback disposition | Recorded status of a note: accepted, rejected, deferred, clarified or duplicate | Prevents notes from disappearing |
| Version ID | Stable label identifying one exact project or export state | Connects notes and approvals to the right cut |
| Handoff | Transfer of project, deliverables, dependencies and responsibility | Makes the next person's work possible |
| Consolidation | Collection of required project media and dependencies into a controlled package | Supports portability |
| Manifest | Structured inventory of package contents and identifiers | Makes completeness checkable |
| Checksum | Digest calculated from file contents to detect changes or transfer errors | Verifies file identity, not creative correctness |
| Backup | Additional copy intended to support recovery after loss or corruption | Should be separate from the only working copy |
| Archive | Managed preservation of selected project records and deliverables for future access | Requires ownership, retention and restore planning |
| Restore test | Attempt to recover and open archived material in a separate location/environment | Evidence that recovery is practical |
| Retention schedule | Rule specifying what is retained, for how long, where and by whom | Limits unnecessary storage and exposure |
| Chain of custody | Documented control and transfer history for sensitive or evidentiary material | Important when integrity and handling history matter |
| Accessibility | Design and delivery practices that make content usable by people with different access needs | Includes captions, readable graphics and accessible review materials |

## Purpose and professional relevance

Professional editing is not complete when the export finishes. Editors work within agreements, schedules, approval structures, rights restrictions and technical handoffs. A strong edit can still fail professionally if the client reviews the wrong version, a private link is exposed, a licensed track is delivered outside its permitted scope, or the project cannot be reopened.

The professional editor must manage both creative quality and process reliability:

- communicate what is included and excluded;
- identify who can approve decisions;
- keep feedback specific and version-linked;
- distinguish correction from new scope;
- preserve source material and project dependencies;
- protect private media and personal information;
- document rights and credits;
- deliver files in a usable, verifiable form;
- retain only authorized records;
- test whether the archive can actually be restored.

This lesson is an operational learning resource, not legal, tax, employment or contract advice. Applicable obligations depend on jurisdiction, agreement, client policy and asset type. Seek qualified advice when legal interpretation is required.

## Theory and professional principles

### 1. A brief is an agreement about outcomes

A creative brief may describe tone and audience, but professional work also needs operational clarity. Translate it into:

- deliverable list and formats;
- intended audience and distribution;
- target runtime and language versions;
- technical specification and accessibility needs;
- source-material responsibilities;
- rights and consent responsibilities;
- review rounds and deadlines;
- authorized decision owner;
- acceptance criteria;
- exclusions and change-request route.

If a requirement is ambiguous, record the question and obtain clarification. Do not silently interpret “final,” “all formats” or “unlimited revisions” as unlimited work.

### 2. Separate stakeholder input from approval authority

A producer, director, client contact, legal reviewer and platform representative may all provide useful notes. Their roles may differ. A note is not automatically an approved instruction.

Create an approval matrix showing who may:
- provide creative feedback;
- approve story/performance decisions;
- approve factual or legal content;
- approve budget/schedule changes;
- approve final delivery;
- authorize archive or deletion.

If authority is unclear or stakeholders conflict, pause the disputed change and request a decision from the named owner.

### 3. Notes should be actionable and traceable

A useful note identifies:
1. exact review version;
2. timeline timecode or other unambiguous reference;
3. observed issue or desired outcome;
4. priority and reason;
5. requester and decision authority;
6. status and disposition.

“Make it more cinematic” is a preference, not an executable instruction. Ask which audience response or specific moment is not working. “At v005, 00:01:12:08, the response begins before the question finishes; preserve the full question or confirm the intended overlap” is actionable.

Use one controlled log to prevent duplicate notes and contradictory revisions. Record rejected notes respectfully with the reason; do not erase them.

### 4. Scope and change control protect quality

A correction restores the agreed specification: for example, fixing a typo or replacing an accidentally offline shot with the approved source. A scope change alters the agreed work: new runtime, new language, new deliverable, new creative direction, additional revision round or newly requested synthetic content.

Before accepting a scope change, record:
- request and reason;
- requester and approval authority;
- impact on time, cost, rights and dependencies;
- new acceptance criteria;
- schedule implications;
- version and delivery consequences.

Do not promise a delivery date or price before understanding the impact.

### 5. A portable project is more than a project file

An NLE project may refer to media, proxies, fonts, plugins, LUTs, graphics, templates, audio libraries and external paths. A project file without its dependencies may not reopen correctly.

A portability test should use a separate folder, drive or clean environment where feasible. Confirm that media relinks, the sequence opens, critical graphics and fonts are available, and a short representative section plays. Document dependencies that cannot be transferred because of licensing or client restrictions.

### 6. Backup, synchronization and archive are different

- **Working copy:** the actively edited project and media.
- **Backup:** a separate recovery copy of important files.
- **Synchronization:** keeping locations updated; accidental deletion or corruption may also synchronize.
- **Archive:** a selected, documented, access-controlled set retained for future retrieval.

Do not assume that a synced folder is a complete backup or that a checksum proves a file can be decoded. Use multiple copies in appropriately separate locations according to the project policy, and test restoration.

### 7. Retain only what is authorized

More retention is not automatically safer. Client contracts, personal data, releases, licenses, security requirements and legal holds may determine what can be retained, who can access it and when it must be deleted. Keep a retention schedule and deletion authorization record. Do not retain private footage indefinitely for a personal portfolio without permission.

### 8. Professional communication is part of quality control

Communicate status using precise terms:
- draft for review;
- awaiting notes;
- revisions in progress;
- proposed picture lock;
- approved picture lock;
- finishing in progress;
- QC failed / correction required;
- QC passed against named specification;
- delivered;
- archived;
- archive restore tested.

Avoid vague claims such as “all done” when approvals, QC, transfer or archive remain outstanding.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives

| Tool/category | Purpose | Alternatives and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Written brief / scope document | Record deliverables, constraints and approval authority | A structured text document or signed project record |
| Review platform | Distribute versioned review copies and collect time-linked notes | Secure approved file transfer plus a controlled spreadsheet |
| Feedback log | Track every note and disposition | Spreadsheet, issue tracker or project-management system |
| Project manager / NLE | Maintain versions and project dependencies | Duplicated project files with explicit naming and change log |
| Media manifest | List files, roles, sizes and identifiers | CSV or plain-text inventory |
| Checksum utility | Verify file identity after copying | Trusted SHA-256 tool; exact command varies by platform |
| Backup storage | Maintain independent recovery copies | Approved local, removable or managed storage in separate locations |
| Archive catalogue | Record owner, location, retention and restore history | Controlled archive register |
| Restore environment | Test project and media recovery | Separate workstation or isolated folder with documented dependencies |

No single storage pattern, number of copies, retention period or checksum algorithm is universal. Follow the project security policy and recipient requirements. Do not put confidential links, passwords, access tokens or personal data into public logs or repository files.

## Reproducible professional workflow

### Phase A — Intake and scope agreement

1. Read the brief and identify purpose, audience, message, distribution and intended use.
2. List all deliverables, including picture versions, aspect ratios, languages, captions, audio layouts, thumbnails and project files where required.
3. Record target runtimes, technical specifications and accessibility requirements from the authoritative brief.
4. Identify client-supplied assets and editor-supplied assets.
5. Record rights, licenses, releases, restrictions and responsibility for obtaining missing permissions.
6. Identify stakeholders and the decision owner for creative, factual, legal, budget and final-delivery decisions.
7. Agree review rounds, dates, revision limits and feedback format.
8. Define acceptance criteria and exclusions.
9. Record unresolved questions and do not treat them as approved assumptions.

**Exit evidence:** approved scope, deliverable matrix, decision/approval matrix, schedule and rights responsibility record.

### Phase B — Prepare and issue a review copy

1. Save a new review version without overwriting the previous version.
2. Verify sequence identity, runtime, frame rate and status.
3. Export or publish to the approved review destination.
4. Label the file or review page with project ID, version, date, runtime and status.
5. Confirm the recipient list and access permissions.
6. Set link expiry or access removal where supported and required.
7. Include review instructions: deadline, time-reference format, priority rules and decision owner.
8. Test the link or file from an authorized recipient perspective.
9. Record distribution date, recipients and version ID.

**Exit evidence:** exact review asset, access record, version label and review instructions.

### Phase C — Collect and disposition notes

1. Collect notes in one controlled location.
2. Preserve original note wording and identify author and date.
3. Normalize references to the exact review version and timecode basis.
4. Group duplicate notes without losing the original authors.
5. Classify each note as objective defect, brief requirement, creative preference, unclear, conflicting or out-of-scope.
6. Assign priority based on acceptance, risk and downstream impact.
7. Ask for clarification where the requested outcome is not testable.
8. Escalate conflicting or high-impact notes to the decision owner.
9. Record each disposition: accept, reject with reason, defer, clarify, duplicate or out-of-scope/change request.
10. Confirm the accepted work and schedule before editing.

**Exit evidence:** complete feedback log with no unresolved note silently treated as accepted.

### Phase D — Implement revisions and approve

1. Preserve the reviewed version as a recoverable checkpoint.
2. Create a new version for the authorized revision round.
3. Implement accepted notes only; keep a change log linking each edit to a note.
4. Recheck story, performance, continuity, sync, captions, titles and runtime after changes.
5. Re-export with a new version ID.
6. Compare the new review copy against the prior version at changed sections and across the full programme.
7. Return unresolved or rejected notes with clear reasons.
8. Obtain approval from the authorized decision owner.
9. Record final approval against the exact version and delivery specification.
10. If approval is not received, retain the status “awaiting approval” or “proposed final.”

**Exit evidence:** version history, note disposition, QC evidence and traceable approval.

### Phase E — Prepare a portable handoff

1. Create a new handoff folder; do not reorganize or delete the only working copy.
2. Include the approved project file and the required sequence/version.
3. Include authorized source media, audio, graphics, captions, title assets and proxy relationships as permitted.
4. Record external dependencies such as fonts, plugins, LUTs, templates and application versions.
5. Include media manifest, relink notes, delivery specification, change log, rights/credits record and QC report.
6. Exclude unrelated private files, credentials, temporary caches and unlicensed assets.
7. Calculate checksums for critical files if required and supported.
8. Copy the package to a separate location.
9. Compare file counts, sizes and checksums where applicable.
10. Open the project from the copied location and test a representative sequence.
11. Record missing dependencies and resolve or disclose them before handoff.

**Exit evidence:** manifest, verified copy, successful reopen test or explicit documented limitations.

### Phase F — Archive, retention and recovery

1. Confirm which materials the client/project owner authorizes for retention.
2. Separate deliverable masters, editable project, source media, licenses/releases, review records and temporary working data.
3. Assign archive owner, location, access list, retention date and deletion authority.
4. Store at least the number of independent copies required by project policy; do not treat one synchronized location as the only backup.
5. Store the manifest and recovery instructions with the archive.
6. Record file checksums where useful and supported.
7. Protect archive access and sensitive records according to policy.
8. Test restoration to a separate location; verify file identity and open the project where practical.
9. Record test date, environment, missing dependencies and result.
10. Schedule future integrity and format review based on retention requirements.
11. Delete temporary or expired copies only after authorization and according to the documented process.
12. Record the archive state as “stored” or “restore-tested”; do not claim recoverability without a test.

**Exit evidence:** archive register, retention decision, manifest, copy verification and restore-test record.

## Worked example — Client review with conflicting notes

**Brief:** A client commissions a 90-second profile video with one master, one vertical derivative, English captions and two review rounds. The client names a project manager as the final creative decision owner. Legal review must approve names and claims.

**Review round 1:** The client sends 18 notes. The editor groups duplicate requests, records one objective spelling correction, and identifies two contradictory requests about the opening shot. A stakeholder asks for a 20-second extension, which changes the agreed runtime.

**Decision process:**
1. The spelling correction is accepted as an objective defect.
2. The opening-shot conflict is escalated to the named decision owner.
3. The runtime extension is recorded as a change request with schedule and derivative impact; it is not silently implemented.
4. A vague note “make the ending stronger” is clarified by asking which message or audience response is missing.
5. All notes remain linked to review version R01.

**Review round 2:** The approved changes are implemented in version R02. The editor records each accepted note and checks the 90-second runtime, caption timing, names, rights and vertical framing. Legal approval is recorded for the relevant claims.

**Handoff:** The final project, authorized media, manifest, master, vertical derivative, captions, rights/credits record, QC report and change log are packaged. A separate copy is opened to test relinking. The archive record states owner, access, retention and restore-test result.

**Expected result:** controlled revisions, resolved authority, transparent scope handling and a portable, documented handoff. The example is hypothetical and does not represent a real client approval.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

Create a simulated review for version R02 with these five notes:

| Note | Type | Correct action | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| “The guest's surname is misspelled at 00:00:24.” | Objective defect | Verify against authorized source and correct | Factual spelling can be checked |
| “Hold the reaction longer after the reveal.” | Creative preference | Clarify intended emotional effect; route to decision owner | Needs editorial judgment |
| “Use shot A” and “Do not use shot A” | Conflicting | Pause and obtain decision from authorized owner | Editor should not guess |
| “Add another language version” | Scope change | Record cost/time/rights/spec impact and seek approval | New deliverable |
| “Fix the section around 40 seconds.” | Unclear | Ask for exact version/timecode and observed problem | Reference is ambiguous |

**Trace the process:**
1. Log each note with its original wording, author and received date.
2. Confirm R02 is the reviewed version and clarify whether timecode is timeline or source time.
3. Assign classification, priority and decision owner.
4. Record the disposition and rationale.
5. Implement only authorized changes in R03.
6. Re-QC changed areas and the full deliverable.
7. Issue the next review copy and preserve R02.
8. Record final approval only when it is actually received.

## Guided practice

### Exercise A — Scope and approval matrix (40 minutes)

Write a brief for a 60–90 second profile video. Include at least three deliverables, two review rounds, a technical specification, caption requirement, rights responsibilities, exclusions and one named role as decision owner (use fictional roles, not real personal data).

**Success criteria:** every deliverable has an acceptance test; approval authority is explicit; unresolved requirements are listed.

### Exercise B — Feedback disposition (45 minutes)

Create at least ten simulated notes, including one defect, one preference, one unclear note, one conflicting pair, one duplicate and one out-of-scope request. Complete the log and write a concise client-facing clarification for the unresolved items.

**Success criteria:** every note has a status, owner and version reference; no conflicting note is guessed.

### Exercise C — Portable project test (60 minutes)

Package a short scene with media, audio, graphics and captions that you own or are authorized to use.

1. Create a manifest.
2. Copy the package to a separate folder or drive.
3. Open the project from that location.
4. Check media, graphics, fonts and sequence playback.
5. Record missing dependencies and repair the package.
6. Re-test after repair.

**Success criteria:** project reopens from the package location or limitations are accurately documented.

### Exercise D — Archive and restore simulation (45 minutes)

Create a fictional archive record with owner, authorized contents, storage locations, access, retention and deletion authority. Perform a restore test if practical; otherwise label it as a designed procedure, not executed evidence.

**Success criteria:** archive and backup are distinguished; restore evidence is recorded; retention is not indefinite by default.

## Independent assignment — Professional client handoff portfolio

Prepare a complete professional handoff for a 3–5 minute scene or a clearly labeled simulated project.

### Required deliverables

1. Creative brief and scope-of-work summary.
2. Deliverable and acceptance-criteria matrix.
3. Stakeholder/decision-owner matrix.
4. Rights, licenses, releases and credits register.
5. Review-copy record with version, date, status and access controls.
6. Feedback disposition log with at least ten notes.
7. Revision and change-request record.
8. Final approval record or explicit “awaiting approval / simulated approval” status.
9. Project/media manifest and dependency list.
10. Transfer verification record and project reopen test.
11. Archive register with retention and access policy.
12. Restore-test record or clearly labeled planned test.
13. Reflection (500–800 words) covering communication, risk, scope, portability and lessons learned.

### Submission rules

- Use fictional names and contact details in simulated work.
- Do not include passwords, private links, access tokens or personal data in public materials.
- Do not claim client approval or restore success without evidence.
- Do not retain media beyond authorization.
- Do not claim legal compliance from a checklist; record the actual policy or qualified advice relied upon.
- Keep originals and prior approved versions recoverable.

## Common errors, diagnosis and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notes refer to wrong frame | Version or time-reference convention missing | Compare note with exact review copy | Reissue correct version and clarify timecode basis |
| Conflicting instructions implemented together | Decision authority unclear | Inspect approval matrix | Pause and escalate to decision owner |
| Revision count grows without agreement | Scope and revision limits not defined | Compare request with brief | Issue change request and update schedule |
| Client receives draft as final | Status and naming ambiguous | Inspect filename, review page and manifest | Correct status, notify recipient and reissue |
| Project opens with offline media | Dependencies not consolidated | Open package from separate location | Relink authorized media and update manifest |
| Fonts/plugins unavailable | Third-party dependency omitted or unlicensed | Inspect project dependency list | Supply authorized dependency or render/replace under approval |
| Checksum differs after transfer | File changed or copy incomplete | Recalculate from source and destination | Re-transfer from trusted source and investigate |
| Archive exists but cannot restore | No restore test or missing environment details | Attempt recovery in separate location | Rebuild package and document tested dependencies |
| Sensitive footage is retained too long | No retention owner or schedule | Review agreement and archive register | Restrict access and follow authorized deletion process |
| Portfolio contains unlicensed music/footage | Rights review omitted | Check licenses and intended use | Remove or replace with authorized material |
| “QC passed” but only a sample was checked | Test scope overstated | Compare report with actual checks | Correct report and complete required full review |
| Review link is exposed | Access list or expiry not checked | Inspect access settings and incident policy | Revoke access, notify owner and document response |

## Answer key and instructor guidance

1. The brief describes intended creative outcomes and audience; scope specifies deliverables, tasks, exclusions, responsibilities, schedule and acceptance.
2. It prevents unauthorized changes and resolves conflicting notes or approvals.
3. Version, timecode/reference basis, observed issue, desired outcome, author, priority and status are valid.
4. An objective defect is testable against a source or specification; a creative preference requires interpretation and approval.
5. Pause the disputed change and request resolution from the named decision owner.
6. A request that adds deliverables, changes runtime/format/language, changes direction or exceeds agreed rounds is likely a scope change; confirm against the agreement.
7. It ensures notes, approval and export refer to the same exact editorial state.
8. A project file may reference external dependencies; a portable package includes the required authorized media and dependency information.
9. A checksum helps detect whether file contents match; it does not prove that media is correct, licensed, decodable or creatively approved.
10. Backup supports recovery; synchronization propagates updates and deletions; archive is a managed, selected record retained under policy.
11. A successful reopen from a separate package location, with required media/dependencies online and representative playback.
12. It limits unauthorized access and indefinite retention and identifies who can authorize disposal.
13. Confirm contractual/rights basis, restrict access and follow an authorized retention policy; do not promise indefinite storage without authority.
14. A restore test demonstrates that the package can actually be recovered and opened, not merely that files appear to exist.
15. Awaiting approval or proposed final; never approved final.
16. Public or portfolio use may exceed the original license, consent or release scope.

**Instructor guidance:** Award full credit for reasoned equivalents. Critical failures include fabricated approval, unauthorized publication, deliberate rights misrepresentation, unprotected disclosure of confidential material or claiming restore/QC execution without evidence.

| Criterion | Points | Full-credit evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Scope and acceptance definition | 15 | Complete brief, deliverables, exclusions, schedule and acceptance tests |
| Approval and communication | 15 | Decision authority, review protocol, clear status and actionable communication |
| Feedback and change control | 15 | Complete note log, conflict handling, scope decisions and traceable revisions |
| Rights, privacy and accessibility | 15 | Asset/consent/credits review, confidentiality, access and retention controls |
| Project portability and handoff | 15 | Complete manifest, dependencies, transfer record and successful reopen test |
| Archive and recovery | 15 | Authorized contents, owner, retention, separate copies and restore evidence |
| Professional reflection | 10 | Accurate limitations, risks, decisions and improvements |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Pass threshold:** 80/100, with successful project reopen or a clearly documented environment blocker and a complete remediation plan. Fabricated approval, unauthorized rights use or false restore claims are critical failures.

**Remediation:** Correct any rights/privacy failure before resubmission. For portability/archive failures, rebuild the package, repeat the separate-location test and update the manifest. For communication failures, revise the approval matrix and feedback log.

## Quality-control checklist

### Scope and review
- [ ] Brief, scope, exclusions and deliverables are recorded.
- [ ] Acceptance criteria are observable and tied to the delivery brief.
- [ ] Decision owners and approval authority are explicit.
- [ ] Review copy has correct version, date, runtime and status.
- [ ] Access list and review destination are authorized.
- [ ] Notes are version/time-linked and dispositioned.
- [ ] Conflicts, unclear notes and scope changes are resolved or held.

### Rights, privacy and accessibility
- [ ] Media, music, graphics, fonts and likeness rights are checked.
- [ ] Releases/consent and restrictions are documented.
- [ ] Portfolio/publication permissions are separately confirmed.
- [ ] Confidential media and personal data are access-controlled.
- [ ] Captions, language and accessible review requirements are addressed.
- [ ] Retention and deletion are authorized and documented.

### Handoff and portability
- [ ] Correct approved project and sequence are included.
- [ ] Manifest lists required files and dependencies.
- [ ] Media, graphics, fonts, plugins and proxies are accounted for.
- [ ] Delivery specification, credits, rights notes and QC report are included.
- [ ] Transfer identity is checked where required.
- [ ] Project is reopened from a separate location or blocker is documented.

### Archive and recovery
- [ ] Archive owner and location are recorded.
- [ ] Authorized contents and retention period are specified.
- [ ] Access controls and deletion authority are recorded.
- [ ] Backup and archive are not confused with synchronization.
- [ ] Independent copies follow the project policy.
- [ ] Restore test is performed or clearly marked as planned.
- [ ] Recovery environment and missing dependencies are documented.
