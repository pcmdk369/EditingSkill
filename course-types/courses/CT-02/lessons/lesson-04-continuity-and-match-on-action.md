# CT-02 Lesson 04 — Continuity Editing and Match on Action

- Course: CT-02 — Film & Cinematic Editing
- Level: Beginner → Intermediate
- Estimated time: 4–5 hours (including practice and review)
- Prerequisites: CT-02 Lessons 01–03; basic shot logging, scene geography, screen direction and timeline navigation. Learners who have not used an NLE may complete the paper-edit alternative.
- Tools/environment: Original, learner-created or licensed footage; two or more views of the same action where available; an NLE with source/timeline frame stepping and audio monitoring. Tool-independent workflow; paper contact sheets and frame logs are valid alternatives.
- Version/review date: 2.0 — 2026-11-22

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

1. Build an action-state map for at least one continuous action across two or more shots, identifying preparation, initiation, contact, peak, release and completion where those phases apply.
2. Complete a continuity matrix that checks spatial, temporal, action, performance, prop/wardrobe, screen-direction, eyeline and sound relationships for at least six adjacent edit boundaries.
3. Select a match-on-action boundary using observable event anchors and compatible movement phases, then explain why the chosen outgoing and incoming frames preserve the intended action.
4. Diagnose at least five continuity defects, classify their likely cause, and propose a reversible correction or an explicit accepted limitation.
5. Distinguish an intentional ellipsis, jump cut or other discontinuity from an accidental error by documenting the narrative purpose, audience cue and comprehension risk.
6. Construct a sound-continuity plan that identifies production-sound ownership, room-tone needs and any J-cut or L-cut without duplicating or falsely implying an event.
7. Compare two edit versions using the same source evidence and a stated review question, then justify the preferred version.
8. Submit a traceable edit or paper-edit dossier that preserves originals, records source ranges and clearly labels simulated, unexecuted or unverified work.

## Readiness check and prerequisites

Before starting, confirm that you can:

- distinguish a shot, take, source range and timeline position;
- describe the scene axis, screen direction and eyeline relationship;
- identify the dramatic beat served by the action;
- locate source material without changing camera originals;
- explain the difference between a source timecode and a timeline timecode.

If footage is unavailable, use the fictional data in this lesson and the Section 24 workbook worksheet. A paper-only exercise demonstrates reasoning, not actual playback, frame accuracy, sound quality or software execution.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Context/example |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity editing | A set of editorial choices that helps the viewer understand intended space, time, action and meaning across cuts | A hand reaches for a cup and the next view continues the same reach |
| Consistency | Matching a visible or audible detail between sources | A cup remains in the same hand |
| Spatial continuity | Legibility of positions, directions and relationships in the scene | A character remains on the intended side of a table |
| Temporal continuity | Legibility of event order and elapsed time | A cup is not placed on the shelf before it is picked up |
| Action continuity | Coherent progression of a physical movement across shots | Reach, contact, grip and lift form one action |
| Performance continuity | Coherent behavior, gesture, breath, gaze and emotional progression | A listening reaction does not jump to a later emotional state |
| Prop continuity | Consistency of an object's position, state, orientation and handling | Liquid level and cup orientation remain plausible |
| Wardrobe continuity | Consistency of clothing and wearable details when the story requires it | A sleeve is not wet before the spill occurs |
| Screen direction | Apparent direction of movement within the frame | A person crosses left-to-right in both connected views |
| Eyeline continuity | A believable relationship between a person's gaze and the object/person viewed | A glance toward an off-screen person is supported by geography |
| Action state | An observable condition or phase of an action at a specific moment | Hand approaching, contacting, gripping or releasing a handle |
| Event anchor | A repeatable, observable point used to compare coverage | First contact, latch click, object leaving a surface |
| Match on action | A cut between views that presents one movement as a continuous action | Cut during a reach from a medium shot to a detail |
| Cut on action | A cut placed during a movement or action event | The camera angle changes as a character turns |
| Overlap | A portion of the same action covered in more than one shot | Both views show the hand approaching the cup |
| Handles | Extra source frames before and after a selected range, retained for trimming or transitions | Additional frames around the grip |
| Action vector | The direction and path of a movement as observed in the scene | Hand travels from the actor toward the table |
| Jump cut | A visible discontinuity in time or position within a related view or action | The hand suddenly skips forward |
| Ellipsis | Deliberate omission of a period of story time | Cut from leaving a room to arriving outside |
| J-cut | Incoming audio begins before the incoming picture | The next room's ambience begins before the cut to that room |
| L-cut | Current audio continues after the picture changes | A speaker's line continues over the listener's reaction |
| Room tone | The underlying ambient sound of a location recorded or represented without prominent foreground events | A quiet interior bed used to avoid unnatural silence |
| Production sound | Sound captured during the original production | Dialogue, handling noise and location ambience |
| Intentional discontinuity | A purposeful break in continuity that supports a stated expressive or structural goal | A fragmented sequence representing disorientation |

## Purpose and professional relevance

Continuity is a contract with the viewer: the edit supplies enough evidence for the viewer to understand what happened, where it happened and how one event relates to another. A technically smooth cut can still be misleading if it changes chronology or assigns a reaction to the wrong event. Conversely, a visible jump can be effective when the audience can recognize its purpose.

Editors use continuity reasoning in narrative film, documentary, interviews, instructional media, advertising, product demonstrations and multicamera production. The same method protects factual sequence, performance integrity and audience comprehension. The professional task is not to make every cut invisible; it is to make every discontinuity intentional, understandable and supported by the source.

## Theory and principles

### 1. Continuity is multidimensional

Do not reduce continuity to matching a hand or prop. Review the relationship at several levels:

1. **Story and chronology:** Does the edit preserve the intended order of events and claims?
2. **Geography:** Can the viewer understand where people and objects are?
3. **Action:** Does the movement progress without an accidental skip or repeat?
4. **Performance:** Do gesture, gaze, breath and response remain credible?
5. **Objects and wardrobe:** Are visible states compatible with the story time?
6. **Sound:** Does audio preserve location, event ownership and a believable acoustic bed?
7. **Technical integrity:** Are frames, sync, media identity and playback behavior reliable?

A lower-level match cannot repair a higher-level contradiction. For example, matching a cup's position does not justify showing the cup being picked up before the character reaches for it.

### 2. Map the action as states and events

A physical action can be decomposed into observable phases. A useful model is:

- preparation: the body or object is ready for the action;
- initiation: movement begins;
- travel: the movement proceeds toward its target;
- contact: the hand, body or object makes contact;
- transfer or manipulation: grip, pressure, rotation or force changes;
- release: contact ends or control transfers;
- completion: the object or body reaches a stable end state;
- reaction: a subsequent response becomes visible or audible.

Not every action contains every phase. A blink may have closure, hold and reopening; a door action may have reach, contact, turn, opening and release. Use only phases supported by the source.

An event anchor should be observable and repeatable. Prefer “fingers first touch the handle” over “the character decides to open the door.” The first can be located in footage; the second is an interpretation.

### 3. Match movement, not merely the object position

For each candidate cut, compare:

- direction and path of movement;
- relative position of hand, body and object;
- movement phase and approximate speed;
- contact or release state;
- camera angle, scale and screen geography;
- what the viewer is expected to notice.

The outgoing frame and incoming frame do not need identical pixels. They need compatible action states and a coherent perceived progression. A change in scale or angle may alter screen position while preserving the real-world relationship.

### 4. Understand overlap and the cut boundary

Two angles may record the same movement at different performances or slightly different timing. The overlap gives the editor a choice of where to transfer attention.

- Too much repeated action can make a reach, lift or turn appear to happen twice.
- Too little shared action can create a skip or an unexplained state change.
- A cut before the meaningful event may hide the event the viewer needs to understand.
- A cut after the action has settled may make the second angle feel like an unrelated insert.

Treat the cut as a boundary between an outgoing frame and an incoming frame. Record both source ranges and the action state at that boundary. Do not assume that the same source timecode exists across separately recorded takes.

### 5. Separate source time, timeline time and story time

Source timecode identifies a position in a media source. Timeline timecode identifies a position in the edit. Story time describes when the event occurs in the fictional or factual sequence. They are related but not interchangeable.

For every continuity decision, record the time basis and frame rate used. If source timecode is missing, variable, uncertain or inconsistent, document the uncertainty instead of inventing a precise frame reference. When using a paper contact sheet, label it as approximate unless frame-level source data are available.

### 6. Preserve performance and factual chronology

A reaction shot can change the apparent meaning of an event. Before inserting a reaction, verify that the person could have seen or heard the triggering event at that point in the source chronology. Do not use an unrelated reaction to imply knowledge, agreement, emotion or causation that the source does not support.

For documentary or factual work, maintain the applicable editorial policy and source traceability. If a temporal compression changes the apparent sequence or causal relationship, disclose it or choose another construction.

### 7. Sound is part of continuity

Picture edits can be supported by continuous production sound, room tone, ambience or a motivated J-cut/L-cut. Listen for:

- duplicated contact sounds;
- abrupt ambience changes;
- missing room tone;
- dialogue or breath that changes unnaturally;
- a sound that implies an event not shown or not supported;
- phase, sync or source-ownership uncertainty.

Use original production sound where appropriate. Any replacement effect, music or ambience must be authorized and documented. Do not add a sound merely to conceal a factual mismatch.

### 8. Intentional discontinuity needs a test

A discontinuity is defensible when the editor can state:

- the intended audience effect;
- the story or formal reason for the break;
- the cue that helps the viewer recognize the break;
- the risk of confusion or false inference;
- why a continuous alternative is less suitable.

If the only explanation is “it looks cinematic,” the decision has not been adequately justified. Review the edit at normal speed and ask whether the intended effect is legible.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives

**Required for an executed edit**

- Original, learner-created or licensed source media.
- At least two views of the same action for a conventional match-on-action exercise. If unavailable, use a single-view continuity diagnosis and mark the multi-angle requirement as not demonstrated.
- An NLE or media viewer that can step through frames and play audio.
- A project copy, source inventory and backup location.

**Useful but optional**

- Source and timeline viewers;
- clip markers and notes;
- waveform display and audio meters;
- contact sheets or frame exports;
- a comparison viewer or split-screen display;
- proxy media, provided proxy/original relationships are verified;
- a second playback display for review.

**Tool-independent alternatives**

- Frame-by-frame paper log with source IDs and time basis;
- printed contact sheets with readable source references;
- action-state diagrams and a continuity matrix;
- written sound-bridge plan;
- peer review using a neutral description of the intended action.

**Accessibility and safety**

- Use clear headings and table labels; do not communicate a defect only by color.
- Provide text descriptions for any visual evidence needed by a reviewer.
- Preserve originals and work non-destructively.
- Use authorized media and obtain appropriate participant permissions.
- Do not upload confidential or identifiable footage to a cloud service without authorization and a suitable privacy review.
- Record the application, version, operating system, frame rate and relevant project settings if software is used.

No application-specific shortcut, trim mode, synchronization feature or audio tool is prescribed here. Those operations vary by NLE and version and must be checked against the selected application's current documentation before a software-specific walkthrough is published.

## Reproducible procedure

1. **Confirm authorization.** Record source ownership/license, participant consent, usage restrictions and any cloud-processing prohibition.
2. **Create a protected working copy.** Preserve camera originals, project files and earlier approved versions.
3. **Inventory the sources.** Record stable source ID, camera/angle, take, duration, frame rate if known, source timecode basis and any uncertainty.
4. **Define the scene objective.** State what the viewer must understand before, during and after the action.
5. **Identify the action.** Describe the physical event in neutral, observable language.
6. **Find all relevant coverage.** Review the full source ranges; do not select only the moment that appears to solve the cut.
7. **Build an action-state map.** Mark preparation, initiation, travel, contact, manipulation, release and completion where applicable.
8. **Choose event anchors.** Use observable events such as first contact, object lift, latch movement or release.
9. **Establish geography.** Check camera side, actor orientation, screen direction, eyelines and object relationships using Lessons 02–03.
10. **Check source chronology.** Confirm whether the angles represent the same take, separate takes of the same action, or different events. Record what is known and unknown.
11. **Create a continuity matrix.** Compare spatial, temporal, action, performance, prop/wardrobe and sound states at every proposed boundary.
12. **Select a candidate outgoing range.** Retain handles and avoid ending on a frame that makes the action appear complete too early.
13. **Select a candidate incoming range.** Begin at a compatible action phase and preserve the movement needed to complete the event.
14. **Assemble a first pass.** Use the best supported source ranges; do not add a reaction, insert or sound event that changes meaning.
15. **Inspect the boundary.** Step through the outgoing and incoming frames. Record the action state immediately before and after the cut.
16. **Play at normal speed.** Check perceived movement, timing, geography and performance without pausing.
17. **Listen separately.** Review production sound, room tone and any proposed J-cut/L-cut. Check for duplicated or missing events.
18. **Test intentional alternatives.** If a jump, ellipsis or fragmented version is proposed, make a separate version and document its purpose.
19. **Run a neutral review.** Ask a reviewer what happened and whether any action or reaction seemed out of order. Do not lead the reviewer.
20. **Correct and retest.** Make one documented change at a time; recheck adjacent cuts and the complete scene.
21. **Save evidence.** Record source ranges, timeline version, candidate cut, decision, unresolved issues and reviewer feedback.
22. **Handoff responsibly.** Include project/media dependencies, version information, rights/consent status and all unverified items.

## Worked example — A cup moved from table to shelf

**Brief/input:** A fictional café scene shows a person lifting a cup from a table and placing it on a shelf. The exercise uses two simulated views: A-WIDE and B-DETAIL. The example assumes an integer 25 fps timebase only for teaching the timecode notation; it is not a footage measurement.

**Source inventory**

| Source ID | View | Simulated source range | Coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-WIDE | Medium-wide, actor and table visible | 00:00:12:00–00:00:16:00 | Reach, grip, lift and carry |
| B-DETAIL | Close detail of hand and cup | 00:00:31:00–00:00:35:00 | Contact, grip, lift and cup orientation |
| C-SHELF | Medium view of shelf | 00:00:44:00–00:00:47:00 | Placement and release |

All IDs, ranges and observations below are fictional teaching data.

**Action-state comparison**

| Event anchor | A-WIDE | B-DETAIL | Editorial question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand begins reach | 00:00:12:12 | 00:00:31:18 | Is the movement direction compatible? |
| First contact | 00:00:13:04 | 00:00:32:04 | Is the cup in the same place and orientation? |
| Grip becomes secure | 00:00:13:08 | 00:00:32:08 | Does the hand close around the same part of the cup? |
| Cup leaves table | 00:00:13:15 | 00:00:32:15 | Does the lift progress without a repeated or skipped state? |
| Carry begins | 00:00:14:05 | 00:00:33:02 | Does the hand travel in a compatible direction? |

**Decision process**

1. The dramatic purpose is to show that the person has decided to move the cup, not to emphasize a technical trick.
2. A-WIDE establishes the actor, table and shelf geography.
3. B-DETAIL supplies the grip and cup-handling information.
4. The candidate cut is placed after the first contact in A-WIDE and at a compatible grip phase in B-DETAIL. The editor tests several nearby frame boundaries rather than treating the simulated timecodes as a universal formula.
5. C-SHELF is used only after the carry is established. Its first frame must not show the cup already placed if the edit still needs to show the arrival.
6. The cup handle, hand position, liquid level, screen direction and actor's body orientation are checked.
7. The cup-contact sound is used once. Room tone continues underneath; the detail shot does not introduce a second artificial clink.
8. A neutral reviewer is asked to describe the action without being told the intended sequence.

**Expected output:** The viewer understands one continuous cup-lift and placement action across the selected views. The cut may be noticed as a change of angle, but the movement does not repeat, reverse or skip an essential event.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

This paper dry run demonstrates the decision record. It is not a rendered edit.

| Stage | Observation | Risk | Decision / evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source review | A-WIDE and B-DETAIL show the same planned cup action | Separate takes may not be frame-identical | Mark each take's event anchors independently |
| Geography | Actor moves the cup toward the shelf on screen-right in the wide view | Detail view has no wide background reference | Check hand/cup relation and return to the wide geography |
| Contact | Both views show first contact with the cup | Contact timing differs | Compare the physical state, not matching source timecodes |
| Grip | Detail view shows fingers closing around the handle | A cut after the grip may repeat the closure | Test the boundary just before and just after the grip event |
| Lift | Cup clears the table in both views | One view may skip the first part of the lift | Select a compatible state and inspect at normal speed |
| Carry | Wide view establishes direction toward shelf | Detail view alone cannot prove destination | Return to a geography-establishing view before placement |
| Placement | Shelf view shows the cup arriving | Cup may appear before the carry is complete | Preserve chronology or document a deliberate ellipsis |
| Sound | One cup contact event and continuous café ambience | Duplicate click or ambience jump | Use one verified event and an appropriate ambience bed |
| Review | Reviewer describes reach, lift, carry and placement in order | Prompting may bias the result | Use neutral questions and record exact confusion |

**Boundary decision trace**

- Candidate A: cut before first contact. Rejected because the detail shot must supply too much missing context.
- Candidate B: cut after grip begins but before the lift is complete. Retained for testing because the viewer can follow the hand and cup into the detail.
- Candidate C: cut after the cup is fully raised. Rejected if the detail shot repeats the lift; acceptable only if the detail starts after the same state and no repeated movement is visible.

The selected boundary is the version that preserves the action and intended emphasis after normal-speed review. No fixed frame offset is prescribed.

## Guided practice

**Exercise A — Single-action match**

1. Choose an authorized action covered by two views, such as opening a drawer or lifting a book.
2. Log the source IDs and time basis.
3. Mark at least four event anchors.
4. Build a continuity matrix for action, space, object state and sound.
5. Create two paper edit alternatives or two non-destructive timeline versions.
6. Review both at normal speed and frame by frame.
7. Record the preferred version and one unresolved limitation.

**Hints:** Compare action state, not only hand position. A cut may change screen location because the camera angle changes. If the source does not show a required event, do not invent it.

**Success criteria:** The action is understandable, source ranges are traceable, no essential movement is repeated or skipped, and any remaining discontinuity is documented.

**Exercise B — Sound continuity**

Prepare a sound-only plan for the same action. Mark production dialogue, handling sound, room tone and any audio bridge. Identify which sound event occurs once and which ambience can continue across the picture cut. If the source does not contain a usable sound, mark the gap and specify the authorized replacement requirement; do not pretend a sound was captured.

**Success criteria:** Every sound has a source or authorization record, no event is duplicated, and the plan does not imply an unsupported action or location.

**Exercise C — Intentional ellipsis**

Create a paper edit that omits the carry from table to shelf. State what the viewer must infer, what cue signals elapsed time and what information is lost. Compare it with the continuous version.

**Success criteria:** The time omission is legible and does not falsely change event order or imply a causal relationship absent from the source.

## Independent assignment — Continuity evidence sequence

Create a 45–90 second sequence containing at least three linked actions. Include two or more views of at least one action and one transition where sound continuity matters. Use original, licensed or explicitly authorized media.

Submit:

1. Brief and intended audience effect.
2. Source inventory with source IDs, takes, time basis and rights/consent status.
3. Action-state map with at least six observable event anchors.
4. Geography and screen-direction diagram.
5. Continuity matrix covering at least six edit boundaries.
6. Two candidate versions of one match-on-action boundary, or a documented paper-only comparison if no NLE is available.
7. Sound-continuity plan with source ownership and room-tone/bridge notes.
8. Three annotated frame pairs around the selected cut, with source IDs and time basis.
9. A short explanation of one intentional discontinuity or a statement that none was used.
10. A neutral review record, or an explicit “not conducted” entry.
11. Final version manifest, known limitations and QC checklist.

Do not submit confidential footage or third-party media without permission. Do not claim frame-level or playback verification for a paper-only simulation.

## Common errors and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Safe recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand or body jumps forward | Incompatible action phases | Compare event anchors and adjacent frames | Test a nearby boundary or another take |
| Movement repeats | Excessive overlap | Watch at normal speed and count the gesture | Remove duplicated movement while preserving the event |
| Contact disappears | Insufficient overlap or missing coverage | Check whether the source shows first contact | Use a supported angle, restructure truthfully or record the gap |
| Object changes position | Prop continuity mismatch | Compare object-to-hand and object-to-surface relationships | Select compatible coverage or accept/document the mismatch |
| Object orientation flips | Different take or camera relation | Inspect handle, label, liquid and grip orientation | Choose a consistent take or use an establishing view |
| Movement reverses | Screen-direction mismatch | Trace the action vector through the geography | Use a compatible angle or show a motivated turn/reorientation |
| Eyeline appears wrong | Camera side or target relationship is unclear | Recheck Lesson 02 geography and eye target | Re-establish geography or choose a different view |
| Reaction appears too early | Chronology or reaction source is unsupported | Verify when the person could perceive the event | Reorder only when truthful, choose another reaction or omit it |
| Performance jumps emotionally | Gesture, breath or gaze is from a different moment | Compare performance state before and after the cut | Select a compatible take or preserve a motivated change |
| Sound clicks twice | Two production events overlap | Listen to the edit and inspect source ownership | Retain one supported event and repair ambience |
| Ambience changes abruptly | Room tone or location bed is missing | Listen across the cut without picture | Add authorized matching ambience or retain the documented gap |
| Dialogue becomes out of sync | Source/timeline timebase or sync assumption is wrong | Verify source identity, frame rate and sync reference | Reconcile media and resync from a reliable reference |
| Time jump is confusing | Ellipsis lacks a cue | Ask a neutral viewer what elapsed | Add a truthful cue or restore necessary action |
| Detail shot changes meaning | Insert/reaction implies an unsupported event | Compare source chronology and editorial policy | Remove it or document and obtain required approval |
| Edit works paused but not in motion | Static frame match hides timing/speed mismatch | Play at normal speed and review adjacent frames | Adjust boundary or select another source range |
| Fix creates a new defect | Only one boundary was checked | Review the full scene and neighboring edits | Retest adjacent cuts and save a new version |

## Answer key / instructor guidance

1. Consistency is matching details; continuity is the viewer's broader understanding of connected space, time, action and meaning.
2. Spatial, temporal, action, performance, prop/wardrobe, screen direction/eyeline, sound and technical integrity are acceptable examples.
3. Observable anchors can be located and compared in sources; intentions and emotions are interpretations unless supported by evidence.
4. Source timecode identifies a source position; timeline timecode identifies an edit position; story time is the event's place in the narrative or factual chronology.
5. They may be separate takes, cameras or recordings with different start points. Compare event state and verified chronology, not numeric equality.
6. It can repeat the gesture or movement and make the action appear to happen twice.
7. Camera angle and scale change screen position; compatible action state, direction and spatial relationship matter more than pixel identity.
8. Verify source chronology, eyeline/geography, when the person could perceive the event and whether the reaction implies unsupported knowledge or emotion.
9. They let audio cross a picture edit and can motivate a transition or maintain a continuous sound environment when semantically appropriate.
10. It falsely suggests two physical events and distracts from the action; use the supported event once and repair ambience separately.
11. A stated expressive purpose, a cue the audience can recognize, source support, a comprehension-risk assessment and comparison with a continuous alternative.
12. Record uncertainty, avoid invented precision, verify source identity/timebase and use only claims supported by the available evidence.
13. Frame inspection can hide perceived speed, repetition, performance rhythm and sound problems that are apparent only in motion.
14. It overstates evidence; a paper simulation cannot prove actual playback, frame accuracy, sound quality or software behavior.
15. Source IDs/ranges, timebase/frame rate, version ID, decision rationale, rights/consent status, unresolved issues, reviewer evidence and known limitations.
16. When source coverage is missing, a correction would misrepresent facts/performance, or a mismatch is unavoidable and can be honestly documented and approved.

## Assessment rubric

| Criterion | Points | Evidence required |
|---|---:|---|
| Source authorization and traceability | 10 | Source inventory, permissions/consent and stable IDs |
| Action-state and event-anchor analysis | 20 | Observable phases and repeatable anchors across coverage |
| Match-on-action timing and movement | 20 | Compatible states, no accidental repeat/skip, normal-speed review |
| Spatial, prop and performance continuity | 15 | Geography, screen direction, object state, gesture and eyeline |
| Temporal and factual integrity | 10 | Chronology verified; uncertainty and ellipsis documented |
| Sound continuity | 10 | Event ownership, room tone, sync and bridge plan |
| Editorial reasoning and alternatives | 5 | Intentional discontinuity rationale or justified continuous choice |
| Evidence, organization and handoff | 10 | Version manifest, annotations, QC, limitations and review record |
| **Total** | **100** | |

**Pass threshold:** 70/100, with all critical gates passed. A learner cannot pass by concealing a rights/consent failure, fabricating source evidence, or presenting a paper-only exercise as an executed/verified edit. A critical chronology error that changes factual meaning requires remediation even if the numeric score is 70 or higher.

**Remediation:** Rebuild the action-state map, correct the source/time basis, compare two candidate boundaries, recheck sound ownership and submit a revised continuity matrix with a change log.

## Quality-control checklist

**Source and project**

- [ ] Source media are authorized and traceable.
- [ ] Originals and earlier project versions are preserved.
- [ ] Source IDs, ranges, frame rate and time basis are recorded.
- [ ] Unknown or missing metadata is explicitly marked.

**Story, space and action**

- [ ] Scene objective and action are stated in observable terms.
- [ ] Event anchors are supported by the source.
- [ ] Spatial relationships, axis, screen direction and eyelines are legible.
- [ ] The outgoing and incoming action states are compatible.
- [ ] No essential movement is accidentally repeated or skipped.
- [ ] Prop, wardrobe and body positions are plausible.
- [ ] Performance and reaction chronology remain credible.

**Sound and technical review**

- [ ] Production sound and replacement sound are identified.
- [ ] No duplicated contact event or unexplained ambience jump remains.
- [ ] Dialogue/sync and source identity are checked.
- [ ] The edit is reviewed frame by frame and at normal speed.
- [ ] Neighboring cuts and the complete scene are rechecked after changes.

**Ethics, accessibility and handoff**

- [ ] Intentional discontinuities have a documented purpose and audience cue.
- [ ] Documentary/factual chronology is not misrepresented.
- [ ] Consent, licensing, privacy and cloud-processing restrictions are respected.
- [ ] Evidence is readable and not color-only.
- [ ] Simulated, paper-only, unexecuted and unverified items are labeled.
- [ ] Reviewer notes, unresolved issues and accepted compromises are recorded.
- [ ] Final project dependencies and version information are included.
