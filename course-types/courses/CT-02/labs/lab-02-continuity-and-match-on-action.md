# CT-02 Lab 02 — Continuity Editing and Match on Action

**Level:** Intermediate  
**Estimated time:** 90–150 minutes  
**Software:** Any NLE with frame-accurate trimming, source/timeline viewers and audio waveform display.  
**Output:** A 30–60 second continuity edit and a documented continuity report.

## Learning outcomes
The learner can match action across shots, evaluate eyelines and screen direction, identify prop/body-position discontinuities, and distinguish intentional discontinuity from accidental errors.

## Prerequisites and materials
- CT-02 Lab 01 completed.
- A short action with at least two angles or shot sizes.
- A scene with a visible object/prop is recommended.
- A continuity report worksheet.

## Key terminology
- **Match on action:** a cut during movement so the action continues across camera views.
- **Eyeline match:** compatible gaze directions that establish what a character looks at.
- **Screen direction:** the direction of movement or gaze as presented on screen.
- **Continuity error:** an unintended mismatch in space, action, performance, sound or object state.

## Procedure

### A. Prepare the material
1. Duplicate the Lab 01 sequence; never overwrite the original assembly.
2. Label the relevant clips by angle and take.
3. Find a movement visible in at least two shots: opening a door, picking up an object, sitting down or turning.
4. Mark the movement's start, contact/turning point and completion in each take.

### B. Match the movement
1. Place the first angle on the timeline.
2. Find a frame shortly before the action becomes clear.
3. Place the second angle so its action continues naturally.
4. Trim the outgoing and incoming clips to align the movement.
5. Review one or two seconds before and after the cut at normal speed.
6. Inspect frame-by-frame only to diagnose a specific jump; judge the final result at normal playback speed.
7. Confirm the movement remains physically plausible.

### C. Check eyelines and screen direction
1. Identify the character's gaze direction in the outgoing shot.
2. Identify the gaze direction in the incoming shot.
3. Compare both with the geography diagram.
4. Verify whether the character's movement direction is consistent with the scene.
5. If the axis is crossed, use a motivated camera move, neutral shot or clear re-establishment when appropriate.

### D. Check object and performance continuity
Compare hand position, prop state, clothing, posture, background movement and emotional intensity. A continuity mismatch may be solved by selecting another take, shortening the shot, changing the cut point or choosing a different coverage angle. Do not conceal a story-critical mismatch by creating an impossible action.

### E. Build and review
1. Make a clean continuity pass.
2. Make a second pass focused only on performance.
3. Make a third pass focused on spatial comprehension.
4. Watch the complete edit without pausing.
5. Ask a reviewer to describe the action and character positions without seeing the source footage.

## Worked example
A character reaches for a cup in a medium shot and lifts it in a close-up. Cut as the hand approaches the cup, then continue the movement in the close-up. Compare the cup level, finger placement and direction of movement. If the close-up begins after the cup has already moved, trim or select a different range so the action does not jump.

## Troubleshooting
| Symptom | Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Hand jumps between shots | Different action phase or take | Re-align movement or select a compatible take |
| Gaze seems disconnected | Eyeline mismatch | Verify axis and camera placement |
| Movement reverses | Screen direction mismatch | Check geography; use a motivated transition if crossing is intended |
| Cut feels technically smooth but emotionally wrong | Reaction or pause removed | Restore performance timing |
| Prop changes state | Takes differ | Find a matching take or restructure honestly |

## Submission evidence
- 30–60 second continuity edit.
- Before/after timeline screenshots.
- Continuity report with at least five inspected edits.
- One example where a technically possible cut was rejected for performance reasons.

## Assessment — 25 points
- Match-on-action quality: 7
- Eyeline and screen-direction reasoning: 6
- Prop/body/performance checks: 6
- Report and evidence: 6

**Pass:** 18/25.

## Quality gate
No unexplained reversal of movement, unintelligible eyeline, physically impossible action or accidental prop-state change remains in the submitted sequence. Intentional discontinuities are identified and justified.

## 1. Professional brief

A short narrative scene contains a simple physical action with an observable beginning, contact or transfer event, completion and reaction. The available coverage may include a master or medium shot, a closer angle, a reaction and separate production sound. Some shots may come from separate takes rather than simultaneous cameras.

The editor must construct a 30–60 second sequence that communicates the action, geography, chronology and performance without accidental repetition, omission or impossible object movement. The editor must distinguish a technically alignable cut from a narratively and perceptually credible cut.

**Primary deliverables**

1. A 30–60 second continuity edit, or a clearly labeled paper-only edit plan if no authorized media/NLE is available.
2. A source inventory and action-state/event-anchor map.
3. A geography and eyeline record.
4. A continuity matrix covering at least five proposed or inspected edit boundaries.
5. A match-on-action candidate comparison for at least two boundaries.
6. A sound-continuity plan and a record of the sound-only review, if performed.
7. A defect, correction and retest log.
8. A handoff note identifying the exact version, dependencies, limitations and evidence status.

## 6. Theory and decision framework

### 6.1 Continuity is a set of audience-facing relationships

Continuity is not simply matching the position of an object between adjacent frames. It is the audience's ability to understand the relationship between shots across several dimensions:

- **Temporal:** the order and duration of events.
- **Spatial:** where people and objects are, and how they relate to the scene.
- **Action:** the physical state and phase of a movement.
- **Performance:** gesture, gaze, pause, vocal delivery and emotional progression visible in the source.
- **Object/wardrobe:** prop state, hand contact, costume and other persistent details.
- **Sound:** dialogue sync, sound-event ownership, room tone, perspective and bridges.
- **Factual/causal:** whether the edit implies an event, reaction, knowledge or cause that the source does not support.
- **Graphic/accessibility:** titles, captions and other overlays remain synchronized with the final picture and do not obscure essential information.

A cut can match action while breaking geography; it can match geography while removing a meaningful pause. Evaluate the whole relationship, not a single visual coincidence.

### 6.2 Describe action as states and events

An action-state map describes observable phases rather than inferred intention. A common physical interaction may include:

1. **Preparation:** body and object are in a stable starting state.
2. **Initiation:** movement begins.
3. **Travel:** the hand/body/object moves toward a target.
4. **Contact:** two surfaces meet or an object is grasped.
5. **Manipulation/transfer:** grip, weight, orientation or location changes.
6. **Release:** contact ends or control transfers.
7. **Completion:** the action reaches a new stable state.
8. **Reaction:** a visible or audible response follows.

Remove irrelevant phases and add action-specific ones. A door, cup, phone, bag and person sitting down do not share an identical state model.

An **event anchor** is a source-supported observable point such as first hand movement, first contact, a latch engaging, an object leaving a surface, a foot landing or a line beginning. “The character realizes the truth” is an interpretation, not a frame-level anchor.

### 6.3 Source time, timeline time and story chronology

- **Source time** identifies a location within an original recording, using its actual frame/time basis.
- **Timeline time** identifies where a selected source range appears in the edited sequence.
- **Story chronology** describes when the represented event occurs relative to other story events.

These are not interchangeable. A cut can place a reaction later in the timeline while the reaction was recorded earlier in the production day. The editor must still ensure that the scene's implied chronology is supported.

For separate takes, source frame 0142 in Camera A is not synchronized with source frame 0142 in Camera B. Compare observable action states and performance; do not treat local frame labels as a common clock.

### 6.4 Match movement phase, direction and cause

A match-on-action cut is usually motivated by a movement that carries the viewer across the boundary. Compare:

- movement direction and screen vector;
- speed/acceleration and visible motion blur where relevant;
- body and hand position;
- object orientation, height and contact state;
- camera side, scale and viewing angle;
- action phase immediately before and after the cut;
- whether the incoming shot repeats, skips or reverses an essential event.

Do not force numerical frame equality across separate takes. Frame stepping helps diagnose a specific discontinuity, but a technically aligned frame pair may still feel unnatural because performance timing, camera perspective or motion velocity differs.

### 6.5 Handles, overlap and the cut window

A **handle** is usable source material before or after a selected range. A **cut window** is the range of plausible outgoing/incoming boundaries that preserve the event and performance.

Some simultaneous camera coverage may contain the same action at the same moment; the editor can compare synchronized angles. Separate takes may contain different timing, gesture or object states. In either case, the edit must not accidentally show an essential event twice or omit it without a legible ellipsis.

A small amount of repeated movement may be acceptable if it is imperceptible or deliberately stylized, but it is not automatically a correct match. Review at normal speed and document the reason.

### 6.6 Geography, eyelines and screen direction

For each shot, identify:

- the primary axis or interaction line;
- camera side and viewing direction;
- subject position and facing;
- movement vector;
- eyeline target and whether it is visible/established;
- object and set relationships;
- any neutral or re-establishing shot that clarifies a crossing.

The 180-degree guideline is a continuity aid, not a law. A crossing can be intentional if the viewer can understand the transition through a motivated camera move, neutral view, clear re-establishment or deliberate disorientation. A mirror transform is not a universal repair because it can reverse text, handedness, logos, lighting and geography.

### 6.7 Performance and reaction integrity

Record what is visible or audible before interpreting it. A pause, glance, breath or hand hesitation may be dramatically important. Avoid removing it solely to make the edit faster.

A reaction shot must be checked against chronology and perception: could the person see or hear the triggering event at that point? Does the edit imply knowledge or causation unsupported by the source? If the chronology is intentionally rearranged, the structure must not misrepresent factual material or mislead the audience in a way prohibited by the brief.

### 6.8 Sound is part of continuity

Treat dialogue, production sound, room tone, handling sounds, effects and music as separate elements with identifiable source/rights and sync relationships.

- A **J-cut** lets incoming audio begin before its picture.
- An **L-cut** lets outgoing audio continue after its picture.
- A **sound bridge** connects two picture segments through continuing or overlapping sound.
- **Room tone** is the ambient sound of a location/recording environment and may help maintain a natural bed.
- **Sound-event ownership** identifies which source/event justifies a sound, reducing accidental duplication or false implication.

Check that a cup contact, door latch or footstep is not duplicated, shifted or assigned to a different action in a misleading way. Review the audio alone as well as with picture.

### 6.9 Intentional discontinuity and ellipsis

A jump cut, ellipsis, montage fragment or temporal compression can be valid when it serves a stated dramatic/formal purpose and the viewer receives enough information to understand the intended transition. Record:

- what time/action is omitted or disrupted;
- why the discontinuity is used;
- what visual, sound or narrative cue makes it legible;
- what comprehension or factual risk it creates;
- what continuous alternative was considered;
- who approved the decision where approval is required.

An accidental continuity defect must not be relabeled “style” after the fact without evidence of intent.

## 7. Tools, requirements and alternatives

### Tool-independent method

The core method is a source inventory, action-state map, geography record, candidate comparison, continuity matrix and evidence-based review. It can be completed before opening an editor.

### NLE or timeline application

Use an application that can inspect sources, place ranges on a timeline, trim at the required precision, review playback and manage audio. Verify the exact edition/version and its current official documentation before relying on a specific feature. This lab does not prescribe menu paths, keyboard shortcuts, automatic matching, multicamera mode or export settings.

### Paper-only alternative

A paper-only learner can use source stills/contact sheets, production notes, written shot descriptions or an authorized shot log. The learner may plan cut windows and complete the matrices, but must label frame-level playback, audio sync and rendered-file tests “Not Run.” Paper analysis cannot earn credit for executed playback or export checks.

### Optional supporting tools

A frame/timecode display, waveform view, audio meters, media metadata inspector, storyboard/contact-sheet method and spreadsheet can help. They are aids, not evidence by themselves. Record product/version only when actually used.

## 8. Reproducible implementation procedure

### Phase A — Authorize and protect the source

1. Confirm the scene brief, intended audience and deliverable duration.
2. Verify source owner, license/permission, participant consent and restrictions.
3. Record the original filenames, source IDs and storage location.
4. Preserve originals. Create a separate project/workspace and a rollback copy using the production's approved method.
5. Mark unknown rights, missing releases or uncertain source identity as a hold; do not proceed to public delivery.

### Phase B — Inspect and inventory

6. Identify whether each angle is simultaneous coverage or a separate take.
7. Record source duration, frame rate/timebase, audio properties and any variable-frame-rate uncertainty where known.
8. Review all relevant material or record exactly which ranges were not reviewed.
9. Label angle, take, camera side and source ID consistently.
10. Record missing action phases, missing handles, pickups or unusable material.

### Phase C — Map action and chronology

11. Write the scene objective in observable terms.
12. Break the main physical action into relevant states.
13. Mark at least four event anchors per key action.
14. Record each anchor's source ID, source range, time basis and confidence.
15. Separate observable action from emotional interpretation.
16. Identify reaction shots and verify whether the person could perceive the triggering event.
17. Record any chronology change or intentional ellipsis before editing.

### Phase D — Establish geography

18. Draw or update the scene axis and subject/object layout.
19. For each selected shot, record camera side, subject facing, movement vector and eyeline target.
20. Identify neutral/re-establishing views or motivated crossings.
21. Flag any angle whose geography is unknown; do not assume a reverse angle is automatically compatible.

### Phase E — Compare cut candidates

22. For each proposed boundary, select at least two outgoing/incoming range combinations where the source permits.
23. Compare action phase, direction, speed, body position, prop state, eyeline, performance and sound.
24. Record whether the sources are simultaneous or separate takes.
25. Reject candidates that repeat/skip an essential action, reverse direction without motivation or imply unsupported chronology.
26. State the reason for the selected candidate and one reason for rejecting an alternative.

### Phase F — Build non-destructively

27. Duplicate the working sequence or create a new version.
28. Assemble the scene in story order before adding decorative transitions.
29. Place each cut according to its event/performance purpose.
30. Preserve enough handles for revision and avoid overwriting source material.
31. Keep dialogue and production sound associated with the correct event/source.
32. Add room tone or a bridge only when it is supported and improves continuity.
33. Label intentional discontinuities and temporary placeholders.

### Phase G — Review in separate passes

34. **Action pass:** watch the sequence at normal speed; note repeated, skipped, reversed or physically impossible movement.
35. **Geography pass:** review subject positions, camera side, movement vectors and eyelines.
36. **Performance pass:** review pauses, gestures, reactions, vocal delivery and emotional progression.
37. **Object pass:** review prop/wardrobe/set state and hand contact.
38. **Sound-only pass:** listen for sync, duplicated events, gaps, perspective and unsupported sound cues.
39. **Factual/chronology pass:** check whether the edit implies knowledge, causation or sequence not supported by the source.
40. Use frame stepping only to diagnose a named boundary; return to normal-speed playback to judge the result.

### Phase H — Test comprehension and revise

41. If a reviewer session is authorized, show the cut without explaining the intended action.
42. Ask neutral prompts: “What happened?”, “Did any action repeat or disappear?” and “Where were the people/objects?”
43. Record the exact version, reviewer context and limitations. If no session occurred, record “Not conducted.”
44. Revise only after documenting the issue and its reason.
45. Recheck the changed boundary, neighboring cuts and the complete sequence.

### Phase I — Defect control and handoff

46. Classify each issue as critical, major, minor or observation using the impact definitions in Section 12.
47. Correct in a new version; preserve the previous version and the defect record.
48. Retest the affected boundary and adjacent continuity dimensions.
49. Complete the QC checklist and evidence status.
50. Handoff the project/version, source dependencies, rights restrictions, unresolved issues and rollback reference.

## 9. Worked example — A cup moves from table to shelf

**All IDs, frame labels and observations in this example are simulated teaching data.** The two picture sources are separate takes recorded at a nominal 25 frames per second. Their local frame counts are not a common clock.

### Input inventory

| Source ID | View / take | Relevant action | Local source frame labels |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRC-A | Medium, take 02 | Hand reaches, grasps cup, lifts it | Reach begins 0100; contact 0142; cup clears table 0158 |
| SRC-B | Close-up, take 03 | Hand and cup detail | Reach begins 0076; contact 0118; cup clears table 0134 |
| SRC-C | Reaction, take 01 | Character looks toward shelf after placement | Reaction begins 0080; gaze settles 0092 |
| AUD-A | Production sound, take 02 | Dialogue and cup handling | Source timing requires verification |

Because SRC-A and SRC-B are separate takes, frame 0142 in SRC-A is not synchronized with frame 0118 in SRC-B. The matching event labels indicate comparable action phases, not a shared clock.

### Candidate comparison

| Candidate | Outgoing choice | Incoming choice | Predicted result | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | SRC-A ends immediately before its contact anchor | SRC-B begins immediately after its contact anchor | Cut crosses the grasp/contact event; inspect hand and cup state | Preferred for the paper plan, pending normal-speed review |
| B | SRC-A ends after the cup has already cleared the table | SRC-B begins at its contact anchor | The cup may appear to return to the table or repeat the lift | Reject unless a clear motivated time reset is intended |
| C | SRC-A ends before contact | SRC-B begins well before its contact anchor | The reaching motion may visibly repeat | Reject if repetition is perceptible and not intentional |

### Decision trace

1. The scene's action is described as “the character grasps the cup, lifts it, carries it to the shelf and releases it.”
2. The selected boundary is designed to cross the contact event, not to match the numerical frame labels.
3. The editor compares the hand's direction, cup orientation, grip, camera side and motion speed.
4. If the close-up shows a different hand, grip or cup level, the editor searches another range or take rather than hiding a physically impossible jump.
5. The shelf placement is checked as a separate action with its own contact/release anchors.
6. The reaction shot is placed only if its chronology supports the character seeing or hearing the placement.
7. The cup-contact sound is used once and aligned to the supported event. If the production sound does not support the selected take, the limitation is recorded rather than silently shifting an unrelated sound.

**Expected output:** a 30–60 second continuity sequence or paper edit, a selected/rejected candidate record, a continuity matrix, a sound note and a QC report. No actual timeline or playback result is implied by this example.

## 10. Dry run — annotated boundary review

Use this paper sequence to rehearse decisions before editing. All observations are simulated.

| Step | Observation | Question | Decision / evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| D01 | Three sources show the same cup action in separate takes | Are the sources simultaneous? | Record “separate takes”; do not compare source frame numbers as sync |
| D02 | Medium shot shows reach and contact | Which event anchors are visible? | Mark reach start, contact and lift |
| D03 | Close-up begins with cup already lifted | Does it skip a required action? | Compare story need; reject if the grasp/lift becomes unintelligible |
| D04 | Alternate close-up begins before contact | Does it repeat the reach? | Compare movement and decide whether overlap is perceptible |
| D05 | Reverse angle changes screen direction | Is the camera crossing motivated or re-established? | Check geography diagram and neutral view |
| D06 | Prop level differs between takes | Is the mismatch visible and story-critical? | Find a compatible take/range or record the limitation |
| D07 | Reaction shot was filmed earlier | Does story chronology support this placement? | Verify that the reaction could follow the event; do not imply unsupported knowledge |
| D08 | Cup sound appears twice | Is one event duplicated? | Identify source ownership and remove unsupported duplicate |
| D09 | A pause disappears at the cut | Does it carry performance meaning? | Compare a longer candidate before shortening |
| D10 | Revised cut is structurally plausible | Has it been watched at normal speed? | If not, mark playback “Not Run”; do not claim continuity pass |

**Branching rule:** When the source cannot establish an action state, chronology, rights status or geography, mark the uncertainty and choose a safer edit or hold for clarification. Do not fill gaps with invented observations.

## 11. Guided practice

### Exercise A — Action-state map (20–30 minutes)

Choose a simple authorized action: opening a door, picking up a phone, sitting down or placing an object.

1. Define the action in observable language.
2. List at least four relevant states/events.
3. Identify which source shows each state.
4. Mark missing phases and uncertainty.

**Success criterion:** Every event anchor is observable, source-linked and distinguishable from interpretation.

### Exercise B — Two-candidate match-on-action test (30–40 minutes)

For one boundary, compare two incoming ranges.

1. Record the outgoing last usable action phase.
2. Record the incoming first visible action phase.
3. Compare direction, speed, body position, prop state and camera side.
4. Predict any repeated/skipped movement.
5. State which candidate should be tested first and why.

**Success criterion:** The selection is justified by at least four dimensions, not simply “the frames look similar.”

### Exercise C — Continuity matrix and defect classification (30–40 minutes)

Inspect five proposed boundaries. Record temporal, spatial, action, performance, prop/wardrobe and sound status. Classify each issue as critical, major, minor, observation or unknown.

**Success criterion:** Every non-pass has a reason, owner or next action; “not checked” is not recorded as “pass.”

### Exercise D — Sound-only and chronology audit (20–30 minutes)

Listen to the audio sequence without picture if authorized. Identify dialogue sync, room tone, duplicated handling sounds, abrupt perspective changes and any sound that implies an unsupported event. Then compare the reaction-shot order with the source chronology.

**Success criterion:** Sound-event ownership and reaction chronology are documented separately from picture continuity.

### Exercise E — Intentional discontinuity decision (15–20 minutes)

Design a justified ellipsis or jump cut. Record the intended effect, omitted time/action, audience cue, comprehension risk, continuous alternative and required approval.

**Success criterion:** The discontinuity has a stated purpose and cannot be confused with an accidental mismatch.

## 12. Independent assignment — Continuity scene dossier

Create a 30–60 second scene using authorized footage or submit a paper-only plan.

### Required submission

1. Brief, intended audience and acceptance criteria.
2. Source/rights/consent inventory.
3. Project/environment/version record if software was used.
4. Action-state map with at least four event anchors.
5. Geography/axis/eyeline record.
6. Continuity matrix covering at least five boundaries.

8. Performance and prop/wardrobe notes.

11. Version/change log, defect/retest record and QC checklist.

### Defect severity

- **Critical:** a defect that invalidates the scene's essential meaning, creates a materially false chronology/causal claim, violates source rights/consent, or makes the action impossible/unintelligible. Hold submission until corrected or formally excluded from use.
- **Major:** a clear continuity/geography/performance/sound defect that materially interrupts comprehension or violates a stated acceptance criterion. Correct and retest.
- **Minor:** a limited mismatch that does not prevent comprehension but remains below the brief's quality expectation. Record and disposition.
- **Observation:** a non-blocking suggestion or preference, not a failed requirement.
- **Unknown / not tested:** evidence is unavailable or the check was not performed. Never convert this status into a pass.

For every defect, record ID, severity, source/boundary, requirement, observed result, likely cause, owner, correction, new version and retest result.

## 14. Troubleshooting and rollback

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Safe recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand or prop jumps | Different action phase, take or object state | Compare event anchors and adjacent ranges | Try another candidate or record a limitation |
| Action repeats | Incoming range begins too early | Review the movement across the boundary at normal speed | Trim or select a later incoming range |
| Action disappears | Incoming range begins too late | Compare required event sequence with source coverage | Restore the missing phase or use a legible approved ellipsis |
| Movement reverses | Camera side or take direction differs | Revisit geography and camera-side record | Use a motivated/re-established transition or another angle |
| Eyeline feels disconnected | Target or axis is unclear | Compare gaze direction and target geography | Re-establish the target or choose a compatible view |
| Cut is smooth but emotionally wrong | Pause/reaction removed or take performance differs | Review full performance context | Restore a longer range or alternate take |
| Prop state changes | Separate takes differ | Compare grip, orientation, fill/position and set state | Find a compatible take; do not fabricate a physical state |
| Reaction appears to precede cause | Production chronology differs from story order | Review source chronology and perception | Reorder with a clear structure or remove unsupported implication |
| Sound event repeats | Same handling sound exists in two sources | Listen to each source and identify event ownership | Use one supported event or a justified sound design replacement |
| Room tone changes abruptly | Location/recording perspective mismatch | Compare ambience and recording context | Use authorized matching room tone or retain the audible transition intentionally |
| Project relinks incorrectly | Source moved/renamed or proxy mapping is unclear | Compare source inventory and original path | Restore the documented mapping on a copy; preserve originals |
| Frame-step looks correct but cut feels wrong | Motion/performance differs at normal speed | Return to normal playback and review context | Select by perceptual continuity, not a single still frame |

**Rollback rule:** Preserve the previous project/sequence version and original media. Make corrections in a new version. If a change worsens comprehension, restore the prior known version and document the rejected experiment. Never conceal an unresolved issue by deleting its record.

## 15. Performance, security and portability

- Use proxies only when the original/proxy relationship is documented and the final work reconnects to the intended source.
- Do not assume variable-frame-rate media behaves like constant-frame-rate media; verify source properties and the selected application's supported workflow before any conversion.
- Avoid unnecessary transcoding or optical-flow/frame-interpolation operations to “repair” a continuity issue; these can create motion artifacts and do not resolve chronology or performance problems.
- Keep confidential media and personal data within authorized storage and access boundaries.
- Do not send source footage to an AI/transcription/cloud service without permission, privacy review and an appropriate retention/processing decision.
- Record fonts, plugins, LUTs, linked audio, graphics and other dependencies that affect project portability.
- Test a copied project separately if portability is part of the assignment. A project opening on the original workstation does not prove that a recipient can reopen it.
- Keep review exports separate from source/project files and identify them by version.
- A screenshot of a timeline is supporting evidence, not proof of complete playback, correct source rights or audience comprehension.

## 18. Knowledge check and answer guidance

1. Why is source frame 0142 in one separate take not necessarily synchronized with frame 0142 in another take?
2. Give four examples of observable event anchors for a physical action.
3. What is the difference between source time, timeline time and story chronology?
4. Why is matching the position of a cup insufficient to establish continuity?
5. Name five dimensions that should be checked at a proposed edit boundary.
6. Why should a cut be judged at normal playback speed after frame-level diagnosis?
7. What is a handle, and why is it useful?
8. When can an axis crossing remain understandable?
9. What is the difference between a J-cut and an L-cut?
10. Why must a reaction shot be checked against chronology and perception?
11. What evidence is needed before calling a discontinuity intentional?
12. Why should a continuity defect not be “fixed” by silently mirroring a shot?
13. What does a paper-only exercise demonstrate, and what can it not prove?
14. Why is a sound-only review useful?
15. What should be retested after changing an edit boundary?
16. Name three examples of evidence that must never be fabricated.

### Answer guidance

1. Separate takes have independent local recording timelines; frame labels are not a shared clock unless a verified common timebase/sync relationship exists.
2. First hand movement, first contact, latch engagement, object leaving a surface, foot landing, release or line onset.
3. Source time locates media in an original recording; timeline time locates it in the edit; story chronology is the represented event order.
4. Hand grip, motion direction, object orientation, camera side, action phase, performance and sound may still conflict.
5. Temporal, spatial, action, performance, object/wardrobe, sound, factual/causal and graphic/accessibility dimensions.
6. Motion, rhythm, performance and perceptual continuity are temporal experiences; a still frame cannot reveal all of them.
7. Usable source material before/after a selected range; it permits alternative cut positions and safer revisions.
8. When the transition is motivated and the viewer receives a clear move, neutral view, re-establishment or deliberate formal cue.
9. A J-cut begins incoming audio before its picture; an L-cut continues outgoing audio after its picture.
10. The edit may imply a response or knowledge that the source chronology/perception does not support.
11. A stated purpose, source-supported rationale, audience cue, risk/alternative and required approval/disposition.
12. Mirroring can reverse text, handedness, logos, light direction and spatial relationships; it may conceal rather than solve the underlying mismatch.
13. It demonstrates planning and reasoning; it cannot prove actual frame playback, sound sync, rendered output or viewer response.
14. It reveals sync, duplicated events, gaps, room-tone changes, perspective jumps and unsupported sound cues.
15. The changed boundary, neighboring cuts, affected action/prop/sound/eyeline dimensions and the full sequence.
16. Viewer results, timecodes/measurements, approvals, rights/consent records, screenshots, export metadata, checksums or playback results.
