# CT-03 Lesson 06 — Master Creative Direction and Campaign Systems

- Course: CT-03 — Short-Form & Social Video Editing
- Level: Master
- Estimated time: 240–300 minutes
- Prerequisites: Lessons 01–05; multi-format edit, source/rights manifest and editorial-test record
- Tools/environment: Brief, storyboard/shot map, any capable editor, graphics/audio tools, review log and delivery manifest; spreadsheet or plain-text alternatives are acceptable.
- Version/review date: Academic revision 2.0; 2026-11-19. Tool-neutral; verify current platform specifications and applicable law.

## Learning outcomes

Learners can:
- Translate an objective into an audience-centered strategy and testable deliverables.
- Build a creative bible that defines a coherent system without making every edit identical.
- Connect audience, message, proof, format, action and acceptance criteria in a content matrix.
- Separate factual, rights, accessibility, brand and creative approvals.
- Prototype and evaluate a pilot before scaling production.
- Govern versions, feedback, provenance, localization and post-campaign learning.
- Defend creative choices with source evidence and documented trade-offs.

| Creative bible | Concise reference for principles, visual/audio rules, examples and permitted variation | Team handoff |

## 2. Purpose and professional relevance

A campaign is not a folder of unrelated clips. It is a coordinated set of audience experiences. The editor/director makes the central promise recognizable, then decides what each deliverable should explain, demonstrate or ask the viewer to do.

A system helps when multiple people or formats must be produced consistently. It becomes harmful when templates suppress context, create misleading uniformity or encourage unsupported claims. A repeatable system therefore defines both rules and exceptions: what must remain stable, what may change, and who can authorize a change.

Governance matters. A client preference about pacing is not the same as factual approval, rights clearance, safety approval or accessibility review. Assigning decisions to the correct people reduces rework and prevents attractive editing from being mistaken for substantiation.

## 3. Prerequisites and readiness gate

2. Source assets have an owner/source, permission status and restrictions recorded.

## 5. Theory and decision principles

### 5.1 Objective, audience and promise

State who the audience is using relevant evidence rather than stereotypes; what they need to understand or decide; what the campaign can truthfully promise; what evidence supports it; and what action is appropriate. A useful strategy sentence is:

"For [audience/context], this deliverable helps them understand [need] by showing [evidence], so they can [reasonable action]."

Treat this as a hypothesis, not proof of audience behavior.

### 5.2 Message hierarchy and format roles

Rank information: (1) core message/question, (2) essential proof/context, (3) supporting detail, (4) optional detail/next step. Do not remove a qualification that changes a claim's meaning to meet a runtime target. If responsible compression is impossible, change the format, split the subject or revise the promise.

### 5.3 System versus sameness

A creative bible may define type hierarchy, caption behavior, logo use, sound principles, framing and tone. It must also define exceptions. A testimonial may need uninterrupted speech; a demonstration may need close detail. Record why an exception is necessary.

### 5.4 Evidence and claims

Maintain a claim ledger containing exact wording, source, source date/version, owner, status, reviewer and permitted context. Distinguish observed fact, attributed opinion, inference, promotional claim requiring substantiation, and hypothetical illustration. Do not turn an opinion into fact by removing attribution. Do not imply that attractive footage proves durability, safety, environmental impact, medical efficacy or superiority without evidence and authorized review.

### 5.5 Accessibility and inclusion

Plan accurate readable captions, adequate contrast, legible type, clear hierarchy, understandable pacing and alternatives for information conveyed only through sound or visuals. Avoid color as the sole carrier of meaning. Review actual destination/device placements; no single safe-area template is guaranteed across platforms.

### 5.6 Stakeholder governance

Separate creative, factual/subject-matter, rights/privacy, accessibility/localization, brand and delivery review. One person may hold multiple roles, but record which responsibility they exercised. A job title alone does not prove legal authority; confirm delegated authority.

## 6. Step-by-step campaign procedure

### Phase A — Intake and strategy
1. Record commissioning party, decision owner, objective, audience, deadline and distribution context.
2. List deliverables and the purpose of each; avoid formats without a reason.
3. Separate confirmed facts, assumptions, open questions and prohibited claims.
4. Build a claim ledger with source references and review status.
5. Define success criteria and guardrails; use metrics only where a reliable method exists.

### Phase B — Creative bible
6. Write a creative thesis: audience, central idea, tone and intended experience.
7. Define framing, composition, typography roles, color roles, graphics, motion and transitions.
8. Define dialogue/music/effects principles and derive audio targets from the actual delivery specification.
9. Define caption/accessibility rules and review responsibility.
10. Use owned/licensed references; record source and the principle being studied. Do not copy a competitor's distinctive execution.
11. Define permitted variation by format, audience, language and story type.

### Phase C — Content architecture
12. Build one matrix row per deliverable: audience, objective, message, proof, story, format, CTA, length range, rights, reviewer and acceptance test.
13. Check that deliverables complement rather than merely repeat one another.
14. Map every key statement to a source and identify context that must remain in adaptations.
15. Create a shot/story plan with source IDs and time ranges. Never invent timecodes for unseen media.

### Phase D — Pilot and validation
16. Choose a representative deliverable that tests consequential parts of the system.
17. Build a pilot using authorized media copies and clearly labeled draft graphics.
18. Review comprehension, evidence, accessibility, rights, brand fit, format and technical acceptance.
19. Ask focused questions, such as "Can you identify the claim and evidence?" rather than only "Do you like it?"
20. Record defects, decisions, owners, due dates and retest evidence.
21. Revise the bible only when a decision changes the reusable system; record version and reason.

### Phase E — Scale and control
22. Create reusable project structures with clear names and editable sources.
23. Assign unique deliverable IDs and states: draft, review, approved, released or superseded.
24. Apply format-specific editorial judgment; do not blindly resize or reuse timing.
25. Log every requested change and assess effects on meaning, runtime, captions, rights, schedule and other deliverables.
26. Obtain approval from the designated authority for the affected decision.
27. Protect approved masters; make derivatives from controlled copies.
28. Run technical and editorial QC on every output, not only the pilot.

### Phase F — Release and learning
29. Assemble manifest, source/rights records, approvals, captions/transcripts and specifications.
30. Confirm the released file is the approved version and the recipient has access.
31. Record corrections/withdrawals with affected version IDs.
32. Hold a retrospective on effort, defects, feedback and verified outcomes.
33. Update templates only when learning is evidence-supported; do not turn a one-off exception into a universal rule.

## 7. Worked example — fictional community repair workshop

**Brief:** A fictional workshop wants to explain how residents can book an introductory session. It supplies an authorized interview, original demonstration footage and booking instructions.

**Strategy:** Help residents understand what happens at a first session and how to reserve a place. Do not imply every repair can be completed or that all materials are supplied.

| Proposed claim | Evidence | Status | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Introductory session is free" | Current written booking policy | Awaiting owner confirmation | Hold until confirmed |
| "Bring the item you want to discuss" | Booking instructions | Confirmed in this scenario | Show as practical step |
| "All repairs are guaranteed" | No evidence | Unsupported | Exclude |

| Deliverable | Audience need | Message | Proof/context | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explainer | Understand process | What happens at a first visit | Interview and booking steps | Clear sequence |
| Demonstration | See the environment | How an inspection begins | Authorized workshop footage | Visual detail |
| Invitation | Know next action | Where/how to book | Approved booking details | CTA and qualification |

Use shared type hierarchy, caption treatment and graphic style. Let the demonstration use longer shots and the explainer use labeled steps. The invitation may be shorter but must retain booking conditions.

**Expected output:** Creative bible, three briefs, claim ledger, approval matrix, source/rights manifest, revision log and QC evidence. This is a paper teaching scenario, not a real campaign or actual business approval.

## 8. Dry run — conflicting change request

Baseline: pilot v0.3 has an approved message sequence but remains in review. One reviewer requests faster cuts; a subject-matter reviewer says the shortened version removes a necessary safety qualification.

| Step | Decision trace |
|---|---|
| Request | Shorten opening by four seconds |
| Impact | Proposed trim removes a qualification |
| Risk | Audience may misunderstand the demonstration limits |
| Options | Shorten repetition; split into two clips; revise runtime expectation |
| Authority | Subject-matter reviewer decides factual adequacy; creative owner selects among valid options |
| Decision | Retain qualification and remove repetition, subject to retest |
| Evidence | New version ID, change log, sign-off and playback QC |

The rule is not "client always wins" or "editor always wins." Follow the agreed authority. If authority is unclear, pause release.

## 9. Guided practice

1. **Strategy statement:** Write two alternatives for a fictional public library event. State audience, need, message, evidence and action; identify an assumption requiring research. Success: specific, evidence-aware and no promise outside campaign control.
2. **Creative bible:** Write a one-page guide for typography, color roles, framing, motion, audio, captions and at least three justified variations. Success: another editor can apply it without guessing.
3. **Claim audit:** Write six fictional claims; classify each as verified, attributed, inferred, unsupported or awaiting review. Add source and reviewer. Success: unresolved claims are excluded from release.
4. **Approval matrix:** Assign creative, factual, rights/privacy, accessibility/localization and delivery roles; define a fallback. Success: each release-critical decision has an accountable role.
5. **Pilot review:** Review a storyboard or pilot against the matrix. Record five observations with severity, owner, correction and retest evidence. Success: actionable feedback tied to criteria, not taste alone.

## 11. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every clip feels identical | Rules mistaken for mandatory templates | Compare narrative purpose | Keep identity rules; permit justified variations |
| Campaign promise is vague | Objective not translated into audience need | Ask what viewer should understand/do | Rewrite strategy and acceptance criteria |
| Claim lacks support | Claim entered before evidence review | Trace wording to source | Remove, qualify or hold |
| Reviewers contradict one another | Decision rights unclear | Inspect approval matrix | Assign accountable owner per category |
| Late change breaks other formats | No impact analysis | Compare affected deliverable IDs | Reopen affected derivatives and regression-test |
| Captions/graphics inconsistent | No shared design/accessibility rules | Compare actual-size outputs | Update rules and inspect all outputs |
| Localization changes meaning | Literal translation only | Check claims, units, names and context | Qualified language review and re-approval |
| Final file is ambiguous | Weak version discipline | Match IDs, approvals and manifest | Quarantine ambiguous files; issue controlled manifest |
| Pilot works but scaled outputs fail | Pilot misses edge cases | Compare formats/languages/assets | Add representative cases and acceptance tests |
| Template leaks client data | Project reused without sanitizing | Inspect metadata and linked assets | Stop distribution and follow incident process |

## 13. Answer key and reasoning

1. Strategy explains why, for whom and toward what outcome; direction translates it into message, tone and execution.
2. Audience/message, visual rules, audio rules, typography/graphics, caption/accessibility guidance, variations and examples.
3. Different versions serve different audience questions or stages; distinct roles reduce redundancy.
4. Evidence supporting a claim; record exact wording, source, date/version, owner, status and reviewer.
5. Identity can remain consistent while pacing and narrative adapt to purpose.
6. System, comprehension, rights, accessibility and workflow problems before scale increases cost.
7. Creative approval addresses execution; factual approval verifies accuracy/context and may belong to another authorized role.
8. Preserve the qualification or redesign the edit; do not release a misleading version.
9. Exact wording, source, date/version, status, reviewer and permitted context.
10. Changes may affect meaning, rights, captions, runtime, other versions, schedule and approval.
11. To prevent drafts or superseded files being mistaken for releases.
12. Caption accuracy/readability, contrast, text size, sound-independent meaning and safe placement.
13. Hold asset until ownership/license/permission is verified; replace if verification is unavailable.
14. It records observations; it does not prove audience impact without reliable measurement.
15. Approved output ID, specification, QC, rights/claim approvals, captions as required, manifest and handoff record.
16. Pause release, document the issue and escalate to accountable authority.

| Criterion | Points | Evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Strategy and audience logic | 12 | Objective, audience need, promise and realistic action |
| Creative bible | 14 | Reusable rules and justified variations |
| Campaign architecture | 14 | Distinct roles, message hierarchy and matrix |
| Evidence/editorial integrity | 16 | Claim ledger, traceability and no unsupported claims |
| Pilot/review quality | 12 | Representative pilot, criteria, feedback and retest |
| Approval/change governance | 10 | Decision rights, revision control and escalation |
| Rights/privacy/accessibility | 10 | Provenance, consent, accessible design and safe handling |
| Handoff/retrospective | 12 | Manifest, QC, release record and evidence-based learning |
| **Total** | **100** | **Pass: 80; critical integrity failures override score** |

Critical failures: knowingly publishing an unsupported material claim; using unauthorized assets; fabricating approval/evidence/results; exposing confidential/personal data; or releasing a version known to misrepresent source material. Correct and reassess regardless of score.

## 15. Quality-control and release gates

**Strategy:** objective, audience, deliverable roles, message hierarchy, qualifications and assumptions are explicit.

**Creative system:** bible defines rules and exceptions; typography, graphics, audio and captions are consistent where intended; references are authorized.

**Evidence/governance:** claims link to evidence and authorized review; creative/factual/rights/delivery approvals are distinct; changes and versions are traceable; release-critical conflicts are resolved.

**Accessibility/rights:** captions match source; text is readable; color is not the sole signal; essential text is checked against actual interface overlays; consent, releases, licenses and restrictions are recorded; personal data is minimized.

**Delivery:** each output meets actual destination specification; approved versions are independently inspected; manifest identifies output/version/language/format/approval; recipient handoff is recorded; measured results are separated from impressions.
