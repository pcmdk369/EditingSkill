# CT-03 Lesson 06 — Master Creative Direction and Campaign Systems

- Course: CT-03 — Short-Form & Social Video Editing
- Level: Master
- Estimated time: 180–240 minutes
- Prerequisites: Lessons 01–05; completed multi-format project
- Tools/environment: Creative brief, storyboard, editing application, review system and delivery manifest
- Version/review date: Tool-neutral curriculum

## Learning outcomes
Learners can direct a coherent short-form campaign, define creative principles and guardrails, manage stakeholder feedback, and design a repeatable production system without sacrificing editorial integrity.

## Key definitions
| Term | Definition | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Creative direction | Intentional definition of message, tone, visual language and audience experience | Campaign brief |
| Creative system | Reusable rules and assets that keep outputs coherent | Typography, framing, sound |
| Brand consistency | Repeated recognizable qualities without forcing identical edits | Campaign family |
| Editorial integrity | Faithful representation of evidence, events and claims | Documentary or product proof |
| Approval matrix | Record of who may approve which decisions | Legal claim vs creative edit |
| Design token | Reusable named design value such as spacing or type scale | Motion/graphics system |

## Purpose and theory
A campaign is more than a collection of clips. It is a system of related messages that should feel coherent while adapting to audience, platform and context. The master editor translates a strategy into practical creative rules and defines where variation is allowed.

Creative consistency must not become mechanical repetition. A template should accelerate production but still permit a shot to breathe, a subject to speak naturally and a complex idea to receive adequate explanation. Stakeholder review requires clear decision rights: a factual or legal approval is not interchangeable with a preference about pacing.

## Procedure
1. Translate the campaign brief into audience, promise, proof, tone and action.
2. Identify claims that require evidence or specialist approval.
3. Define the visual system: framing, typography, color, motion, sound and caption conventions.
4. Define variation rules by platform, audience segment and runtime.
5. Create a content matrix showing each deliverable's message and evidence.
6. Build a representative pilot edit before scaling production.
7. Review pilot for comprehension, accessibility, rights and brand fit.
8. Establish a review matrix: creative, factual, legal/rights and final delivery approvals.
9. Define revision rounds and how contradictory feedback is resolved.
10. Create reusable project structures and graphics while preserving per-edit creative judgment.
11. Document campaign asset provenance and approved versions.
12. Conduct a post-project review and update the system based on verified lessons.

## Worked example
A sustainable product campaign needs an educational clip, a product demonstration and a founder story. Shared visual identity may include type and color rules, but each edit has a distinct story structure. Sustainability claims require substantiation; attractive footage is not proof of environmental performance.

## Dry run
Two stakeholders request conflicting changes: one wants a faster cut, the other says the process is no longer understandable. Return to the agreed audience and outcome. Present two versions with the exact trade-off and ask the designated decision owner to approve. Record the decision and rationale.

## Guided practice
Create a one-page campaign creative bible and three deliverable briefs. Build one pilot and review it against the system.

## Independent assignment
Submit a campaign package containing creative bible, content matrix, pilot edit, approval matrix, asset manifest, revision log and post-project review.

## Common errors and recovery
| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| All edits feel identical | Template overuse | Compare story structures | Allow discipline-specific variations |
| Brand claims unsupported | Visual polish mistaken for evidence | Audit claim sources | Remove or qualify claim pending approval |
| Conflicting feedback stalls project | Approval rights unclear | Inspect approval matrix | Assign accountable decision owner |
| Revisions lose traceability | Informal messages only | Audit version history | Centralize change log and approvals |

## Knowledge check
1. What belongs in a creative bible?
2. Why should factual approval be distinct from creative preference?
3. How can campaign consistency coexist with different story structures?
4. What is the purpose of a pilot edit?
5. What should happen when stakeholder feedback conflicts?

## Answer guidance
1. Audience, message, tone, visual/audio rules, accessibility and variation guidance.
2. Factual/legal correctness has different authority and risk from subjective preference.
3. Reuse visual identity while tailoring narrative and evidence to each deliverable.
4. It tests the system before production scales.
5. Refer to agreed goals and approval matrix, document trade-offs and obtain the designated decision.

## Assessment rubric — 30 points
Creative system 7; campaign coherence 6; editorial integrity 6; stakeholder governance 5; documentation/handoff 6. Pass: 24/30; unsupported claims are a critical failure.

## QC checklist
