# CT-03 Lesson 01 — Audience, Platform and Format Design

- **Course:** CT-03 — Short-Form & Social Video Editing
- **Level:** Foundation → Beginner
- **Estimated time:** 150–180 minutes, including a short review
- **Prerequisites:** Basic file management; no editing application experience required
- **Tools/environment:** Paper or notes app, spreadsheet, media file browser and any editor that supports a custom sequence/canvas. A phone preview is recommended but not mandatory.
- **Version/review date:** Revision 2.0 — 2026-11-18. Platform specifications are intentionally treated as live requirements and must be checked before each delivery.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner can:

1. Convert a vague social-video request into a measurable communication brief.
2. Separate audience, viewer need, message, evidence, desired action and creative treatment.
3. Explain aspect ratio, pixel dimensions, frame rate, duration, orientation and safe composition.
4. Select a delivery format from the brief and source constraints rather than habit.
5. Compare vertical, square and landscape compositions and identify what must be recomposed.
6. Specify captions, audio, accessibility, rights, privacy and approval requirements.
7. Produce a versioned one-page delivery specification that another editor can follow.

## Key definitions and terminology

| Term | Definition | Context/example |
|---|---|---|
| Short-form video | A concise moving-image work designed around a specific communication goal and viewing context. There is no single universal duration definition. | A 25-second product demonstration. |
| Audience | The people a communication is intended to reach. | First-time buyers comparing handmade products. |
| Audience need | A question, task, concern or interest the intended viewer brings. | They need to see how a clasp works. |
| Message | The one principal idea the viewer should understand after watching. | The clasp can be opened with one hand. |
| Evidence | Footage, demonstration, testimony or sourced information that supports the message. | A continuous close-up showing the mechanism. |
| Hook | An opening image, action, question or statement that establishes relevance or curiosity. | A close-up of the clasp being operated. |
| Call to action (CTA) | The next step requested from the viewer. | View the product details. |
| Aspect ratio | The proportional relationship between frame width and height. | 9:16 is portrait; 16:9 is landscape. |
| Frame dimensions | Pixel width and height of the image. | 1080 × 1920 pixels. |
| Frame rate | Number of frames represented or displayed each second, expressed in frames per second (fps). | 25 fps or 30 fps, selected for the production. |
| Duration | The total running time of the edit. | 00:00:25:00 in a 25 fps timebase. |
| Safe composition | A deliberately protected region for essential visual information, text and graphics. | Captions kept clear of edge crops and interface overlays. |
| Deliverable specification | A written record of the intended output, constraints, acceptance tests and handoff requirements. | A vertical master plus a landscape adaptation. |
| Version | A uniquely identified state of a project or deliverable. | CampaignA_9x16_v03_review.mp4. |
| Acceptance criterion | A testable condition that determines whether an output meets the brief. | The product mechanism remains visible throughout the demonstration. |

## Purpose and professional relevance

A technically valid export can still fail when it addresses the wrong viewer, communicates too many ideas, hides a product detail, or uses a format that does not fit the destination. Professional editing begins before the timeline: the editor must understand what the work is supposed to achieve and how success will be judged.

A short video is not simply a long video with sections removed. Its limited duration increases the importance of selecting one message, choosing evidence, designing the opening and protecting essential context. A vertical frame is common in phone-first feeds, but it is not a universal requirement. The written brief and current destination requirements govern the format.

A clear specification prevents expensive rework. It lets a producer, editor, captioner, reviewer and client work from the same assumptions. It also creates a record when a platform changes its interface or delivery rules.

## Theory and decision principles

### 1. Separate the communication problem from the format

Use this order:

1. Who is the intended viewer?
2. What does that viewer need to know, feel or do?
3. What single message can the video support?
4. What evidence is available and authorized?
5. What is the most useful viewing context?
6. What format and versions does the brief require?
7. What tests will prove that the result is acceptable?

Do not begin by choosing a popular aspect ratio or copying another creator's style. A format is a delivery decision, not the message itself.

### 2. Make the message observable

Weak brief: “Make an exciting video about the product.”

Improved brief: “Show first-time buyers how the locking clasp opens and closes, using a close-up demonstration, and direct interested viewers to the product information page.”

The improved brief identifies a viewer, a question, evidence and a next action. It does not promise a result that the footage cannot prove.

### 3. Match format to source and destination

Aspect ratio and resolution are different. A 9:16 frame can be 720 × 1280, 1080 × 1920 or another valid pixel size. The chosen dimensions must satisfy the brief, source quality, editor capabilities and destination's current requirements.

Frame rate is not a quality score. Preserve a suitable source cadence unless the creative or delivery requirement calls for a conversion. Frame-rate conversion can duplicate, blend or discard frames depending on the method; inspect motion after conversion.

### 4. Treat safe areas as design checks, not universal pixel coordinates

Platform controls, captions, device crops and interface layouts can change. Use current destination guidance and inspect the actual preview where possible. Keep essential information away from edges and likely interface overlays, but do not assume one static safe-area template is correct for every placement.

### 5. Define acceptance before editing

An acceptance criterion must be testable. “Looks professional” is not sufficient by itself. Combine measurable checks (duration, dimensions, audio presence, file opens) with reviewable creative criteria (message clarity, evidence, legibility, tone and brand consistency).

## Tools, requirements and alternatives

| Need | Suitable options | Alternative or limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Brief capture | Paper, text document, spreadsheet, project-management tool | A verbal brief should be written down and confirmed before production. |
| Media inspection | Operating-system file information, editor metadata panel, trusted metadata utility | If metadata is unavailable, record it as unknown; do not guess. |
| Format calculation | Calculator or spreadsheet | Manual arithmetic is acceptable; show the formula. |
| Editing | Any NLE or mobile editor with custom sequence/canvas and export | If the tool cannot set the required dimensions, use another approved tool rather than forcing a crop. |
| Safe-area preview | Current platform preview, device preview, temporary guide overlay | A guide is only a planning aid; inspect the final platform placement when possible. |
| Captions | Human transcript, caption editor, subtitle tool or NLE text track | Automated speech recognition is a draft and requires source-audio review. |
| Accessibility review | Caption checklist, contrast checker, peer review and device preview | If a tool is unavailable, perform and document a manual review. |
| Version control | Structured filenames and a change log | Avoid relying on “final-final” filenames or a single overwritten project. |

Software-neutral steps are used throughout this lesson. Menu labels and export presets differ by application and release. Do not copy a tutorial's exact setting without checking the installed version and delivery brief.

## Procedure: create a production-ready brief

1. **Read the request once without editing.** Mark the audience, purpose, key message, destination, language, duration, deadline and requested action.
2. **Identify the decision-maker.** Record who can approve creative changes, factual claims, brand use and final delivery.
3. **Write the audience sentence.** Use: “This video is for [specific viewer] who needs/wants [specific need].”
4. **Write the message sentence.** Use: “After watching, the viewer should understand [one principal idea].”
5. **Choose the evidence.** List the shot, demonstration, interview, data source or document that supports the message. Mark any missing evidence.
6. **Check truthfulness.** Remove claims that are unsupported, exaggerated or contradicted by the source. Keep necessary limitations and context.
7. **Define the CTA.** Choose one useful next action. If no action is needed, record that the video is informational.
8. **Confirm destination and placement.** Record the actual platform, placement, organic/paid context, intended device and any cross-posting requirement. “Social media” is not a complete destination.
9. **Choose the aspect ratio.** Select portrait, square or landscape from the placement and brief. Do not assume a vertical version is always required.
10. **Select pixel dimensions.** Check the current destination specification, editor capability and source resolution. Avoid unnecessary upscaling.
11. **Select frame rate.** Review source metadata and motion. Record the chosen timebase and any conversion reason.
12. **Set a duration range.** Use the brief and narrative needs. Do not remove required context merely to reach an arbitrary duration.
13. **Plan safe composition.** Identify faces, hands, products, subtitles, logos and other elements that must remain visible. Mark areas likely to be covered by interface controls.
14. **Specify audio.** Record dialogue/music/effects needs, language, channel expectations and any loudness or peak specification supplied by the client or destination.
15. **Specify captions and accessibility.** Decide whether burned-in captions, a sidecar subtitle file, descriptive text or multiple language versions are required. Define who verifies accuracy and readability.
16. **Record rights and privacy.** Identify source ownership, licenses, releases, music rights, location permissions, personal information and restrictions on external AI processing.
17. **Define outputs and acceptance tests.** List each version, file format, naming rule, review copy, project package and test.
18. **Confirm the brief.** Send the specification to the authorized decision-maker. Do not treat silence as approval when the scope or rights are ambiguous.
19. **Create a versioned project folder.** Preserve source media; use separate source, project, graphics, audio, review and delivery folders.
20. **Begin editing only when material uncertainties are resolved.** If safe to proceed with a reversible draft, state assumptions and mark the draft as unapproved.

## Worked example

**Brief/input:** A fictional maker wants a 20–30 second video showing a handmade wallet. The intended viewer is comparing craftsmanship and practical use. The only approved footage is a learner-created recording of stitching, edge finishing, the wallet opening and the finished item. The CTA is to view the product information page.

**Decision process:**

- Audience: people comparing handmade accessories.
- Viewer need: see evidence of construction quality and one practical feature.
- Message: the wallet is handmade and includes a clearly demonstrated card compartment.
- Evidence: close-up stitching, edge finish and a continuous shot of the compartment being used.
- Hook: show the compartment opening, then state the feature accurately.
- Structure: opening demonstration → construction details → use demonstration → finished product and CTA.
- Destination: one vertical phone-first placement, with a separate landscape website version requested.
- Format: choose dimensions only after checking the destination's current official requirements and the source's usable resolution.
- Captions: accurate text for all spoken information; no essential detail is communicated only by color or sound.
- Rights: learner-created footage; obtain permission for any visible person, logo, music or private location before release.
- Acceptance: the compartment remains visible, the claim is supported by footage, captions are accurate, and both requested versions are independently reviewed.

**Expected output:** A written brief, a source/rights inventory, one vertical specification, one landscape specification and a list of testable acceptance criteria. No media is rendered in this paper exercise.

## Dry run / annotated walkthrough

### A. Crop geometry

A source clip is 1920 × 1080 pixels (16:9). A vertical destination is 9:16. If the full source height of 1080 pixels is retained, the width of a matching 9:16 crop is:

Width = 1080 × 9 ÷ 16 = 607.5 pixels.

The crop therefore retains about 608 pixels of the original 1920-pixel width. This is only about 31.7% of the original horizontal field. A centered crop may remove a person, product or action.

**Decision trace:**

1. Inspect the source frame and locate the important subject and action.
2. Check whether the subject remains within the crop throughout the shot.
3. If not, test repositioning/keyframes, an alternate shot, a split composition or a new vertical recording.
4. Inspect the result for excessive enlargement and lost detail.
5. Record which version preserves the required evidence best.

### B. Specification dry run

| Field | Draft value | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | First-time handmade-accessory buyers | Confirmed in brief |
| Message | Demonstrate one card compartment | Must be visible in source footage |
| Destination | Vertical social placement | Exact placement still to be confirmed |
| Aspect ratio | 9:16 | Confirm current placement guidance |
| Dimensions | Pending | Do not guess |
| Frame rate | Pending source inspection | Record source and sequence timebase |
| Duration | 20–30 seconds | Check narrative completeness |
| Captions | Required | Human-check transcript and timing |
| Audio | Dialogue plus approved music, if licensed | Confirm music permission and mix requirements |
| CTA | View product information | Confirm destination URL/text |
| Rights | Learner-created footage | Check releases, logos and music |
| Approval | Client/brief owner | Record approval before release |

The correct response to an unknown field is “pending verification,” not a plausible-looking invented value.

## Guided practice

**Task:** Create a specification for a 15–30 second video about a simple learner-created process, such as folding paper, arranging stationery or demonstrating a household object.

1. Write the audience and viewer need.
2. State one message.
3. List at least three pieces of evidence needed.
4. Choose a destination and justify the format.
5. Inspect the source media and record dimensions and frame rate, or mark them unknown.
6. Specify captions, audio, CTA, rights and accessibility.
7. Write at least five acceptance tests.
8. Ask a peer to identify one unsupported claim or ambiguous requirement.

**Hints:** Choose a process you can record yourself. Do not use commercial music, third-party logos or identifiable people without appropriate permission. Keep the first draft simple; the purpose is specification, not visual polish.

**Success criteria:** Another editor can identify the intended viewer, message, source evidence, required formats, constraints and approval owner without needing to guess.

## Independent assignment

Prepare a two-version brief for the same message:

- Version A: vertical phone-first placement.
- Version B: landscape website or presentation placement.

Submit:

1. Audience and message statement.
2. Source inventory and evidence/selects list.
3. Format specification for each version.
4. Reframing plan showing which shots require repositioning or replacement.
5. Caption, audio and accessibility requirements.
6. Rights/consent and privacy checklist.
7. At least eight acceptance tests.
8. A short rationale explaining what must be recomposed rather than simply cropped.

**Constraints:** Use learner-created or properly licensed material. Do not include personal data, private conversations or identifiable people without authorization. If no media is available, submit the paper specification and label media-dependent tests as not run.

## Common errors, diagnosis and recovery

| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Safe recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brief says only “make it viral” | Goal is vague or not measurable | Ask what viewer should understand or do | Replace the vague goal with a measurable communication outcome; do not promise virality |
| Format chosen before destination | Habit or imitation | Compare the actual placement and deliverable list | Reconfirm placement and current official requirements |
| Subject disappears in vertical crop | Landscape source has important off-center action | Review every frame and movement | Reframe with keyframes, use another shot or redesign the composition |
| Text is hidden by interface | Safe area assumed rather than checked | Preview the actual placement and device | Reposition text and re-test after upload preview |
| File dimensions are correct but image is soft | Source was enlarged beyond useful detail | Compare source and output at normal viewing size | Use a better source, wider composition or alternate shot |
| Duration is met but message is unclear | Content removed without preserving context | Ask an unfamiliar viewer to summarize it | Restore evidence or a qualifier; remove repetition instead |
| Captions are inaccurate | Transcript was not checked against audio | Listen while following each cue | Correct names, numbers, negation and technical terms; recheck timing |
| Music is present but rights are unknown | Asset origin was not recorded | Inspect license/permission evidence | Replace with authorized audio or hold delivery |
| Review comments conflict | Approval authority or priority was not defined | Compare comments with brief and decision owner | Log each note, classify it and request a decision on conflicts |
| Export specification is incomplete | Client requirements were not captured | Check every output field | Mark unknowns and obtain confirmation before final export |

## Knowledge check

1. Explain the difference between audience, viewer need and message.
2. Why is “make it viral” not a sufficient acceptance criterion?
3. How do aspect ratio and pixel dimensions differ?
4. Why is frame rate not a simple quality ranking?
5. What should an editor do when a platform's current safe-area guidance is unknown?
6. A 1920 × 1080 source must become 9:16. Why is a center crop risky?
7. Give two examples of evidence that can support a product claim.
8. What is the difference between a burned-in caption and a sidecar subtitle file?
9. Name four rights/privacy checks that should happen before external publication.
10. What should be recorded when a required technical field is unknown?

## Answer key / instructor guidance

1. Audience is who the work addresses; viewer need is the question or task they bring; message is the principal idea the video should communicate.
2. Virality cannot be guaranteed or reliably controlled by the editor. Acceptance should describe observable communication, creative and technical requirements.
3. Aspect ratio is the width-to-height proportion; pixel dimensions are the actual number of pixels.
4. Frame rate describes temporal sampling/cadence. Suitability depends on source, motion, creative intent and delivery requirements.
5. Check current official platform documentation and the actual placement preview; do not invent pixel coordinates.
6. A vertical crop retains a narrow horizontal portion and may remove the subject or action.
7. A continuous demonstration, a properly authorized interview, a measured test or a reliable sourced fact are possible examples.
8. Burned-in captions are part of the rendered image; sidecar subtitles are a separate timed-text file.
9. Examples: source license, music permission, model/location release, privacy of personal information, brand/logo permission, consent for external processing.
10. Mark it pending verification, identify who can supply it and do not treat the export as approved until it is resolved.

## Assessment rubric — 100 points

| Criterion | Points | Evidence required |
|---|---:|---|
| Audience and viewer need | 12 | Specific, coherent audience and need statement |
| Message, evidence and truthfulness | 16 | One message supported by identified source evidence; unsupported claims excluded |
| Format and technical reasoning | 14 | Aspect ratio, dimensions, frame rate and duration are distinguished and justified |
| Reframing and safe composition | 12 | Shot-specific crop risks and safe-composition plan |
| Captions, audio and accessibility | 12 | Requirements are explicit and testable |
| Rights, privacy and approvals | 12 | Source, consent, license, data and approval checks recorded |
| Acceptance tests and versioning | 12 | Clear tests, outputs and naming/version plan |
| Documentation and handoff | 10 | Another editor can reproduce the specification without guessing |

**Pass threshold:** 80/100, with no unresolved critical rights/privacy issue and no invented technical requirement. A learner who fails a mandatory rights or factual-integrity criterion must correct it before passing, regardless of total score.

## Quality-control checklist

- [ ] Audience, viewer need and principal message are explicit.
- [ ] Evidence supports each factual or product claim.
- [ ] Destination and placement are identified.
- [ ] Aspect ratio, dimensions, frame rate and duration are separately recorded.
- [ ] Source limitations and unknown technical fields are visible.
- [ ] Reframing risks are documented shot by shot.
- [ ] Caption, audio and accessibility needs are specified.
- [ ] Rights, consent, privacy and AI-processing restrictions are recorded.
- [ ] Acceptance tests are observable and assigned to a reviewer.
- [ ] Project and output versions are named and source media is preserved.
- [ ] Platform-dependent values are checked against current official guidance before delivery.

## Accessibility, rights, privacy and professional practice

- Provide accurate captions for spoken content when required; review automated transcripts against the original audio.
- Use readable type, sufficient contrast, sensible line breaks and placement that does not obscure essential action.
- Do not communicate essential meaning through color alone. Where relevant, describe important visual information in accompanying text or narration.
- Avoid flashing patterns and unnecessarily rapid visual changes; consider viewers sensitive to motion or flicker.
- Do not assume that a platform's automatic captions or accessibility controls satisfy the brief.
- Record the origin and permission basis for footage, music, fonts, logos, photographs and templates. “Found online” is not a license.
- Obtain appropriate consent for identifiable people and private locations; minimize collection and exposure of personal information.
- Do not send confidential footage, unreleased campaigns or personal data to an external AI service unless the user/client authorizes it and the provider terms and data handling are acceptable.
- Keep factual qualifiers and context. Do not edit a statement so that it appears to mean the opposite of what the speaker said.
- Record client approvals and material changes. A review export is not a final approved master.
