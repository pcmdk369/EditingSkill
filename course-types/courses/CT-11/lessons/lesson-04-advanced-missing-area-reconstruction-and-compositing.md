# CT-11 Lesson 04 — Missing-Area Reconstruction and Compositing

- Course: CT-11 — Photo Restoration & Manipulation
- Level: Advanced
- Estimated time: 120–180 minutes
- Prerequisites: Previous CT-11 lessons and authorized source images
- Tools/environment: Raster editor, optional scanner/RAW processor and secure storage; record app/version and scan properties
- Version/review date: Tool-neutral; archival and privacy requirements depend on collection context

## Learning outcomes
Reconstruct missing image areas using evidence-based cloning, geometry and authorized references while distinguishing restoration from creative completion.

## Key definitions
- **Restoration:** Repairing damage while preserving the source's historical/visual meaning.
- **Provenance:** Documented origin and history of an image.
- **Clone/heal:** Pixel-copy and texture-blending repair methods.
- **Reconstruction:** Rebuilding missing content from evidence or interpretation.
- **Colorization:** Adding color to an originally monochrome image.
- **Preservation master:** High-quality retained version for long-term use.
- **Access copy:** Derivative optimized for viewing or distribution.
- **Intervention log:** Record of restoration operations and decisions.

## Purpose and theory
Reconstruction ranges from copying repeated texture to inferring missing architecture or facial detail. The more unique and meaningful the missing content, the less defensible invention becomes. Use reference sources with clear provenance and label reconstructed areas in the project notes.

## Tools, requirements and alternatives
Use a capable raster editor and a scanner/RAW workflow as needed. Preserve originals, create working copies and store layered masters. Use only owned or authorized images. Keep sensitive family/archival material secure.

## Procedure
1. Map missing region and classify content. 2. Search authorized duplicate prints or related frames. 3. Record reference provenance. 4. Align perspective and scale. 5. Reconstruct repeated patterns from local evidence. 6. Blend texture, grain and lighting. 7. Keep uncertain details separate on named layers. 8. Create comparison and disclosure note.

## Worked example
A missing corner of a patterned wall can be reconstructed from repeated neighboring pattern; a missing personal inscription cannot be invented.

## Dry run
A facial feature is reconstructed without reference. Preserve a neutral repair or clearly identify a creative reconstruction rather than presenting it as fact.

## Guided practice
Repair one missing region using an authorized reference and one region using conservative local reconstruction.

## Independent assignment
Apply the method to a second image with a different damage type. Submit source, restored version, editable project, provenance and intervention log.

## Common errors and recovery
| Symptom | Likely cause | Diagnosis | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submit reference log, layers, uncertainty notes and final comparison. | Restoration issue | Compare to original and reference evidence | Revert unsupported edit and document |

## Knowledge check
1. Why must the original scan be preserved?
2. How do clone and healing tools differ?
3. What is the risk of reconstructing missing unique details?
4. Why should colorization be distinguished from restoration?
5. What belongs in an intervention log?

## Answer guidance
1. It preserves evidence and enables future re-evaluation.
2. Clone copies pixels; healing blends sampled texture/color with surroundings.
3. It can invent historical details and mislead viewers.
4. Colorization adds interpretive color not present in a monochrome source.
5. Operations, tool/version, source references, uncertainty and approvals.
